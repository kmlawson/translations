The animal statues in the first courtyard cast their long, dark shadows across the white stone slabs as we prepared to depart. With heavy, dragging steps, a Russian guard moved along the courtyard walls from pagoda to pagoda. The dark silhouettes of their shaggy papakhas and pointed bayonets between the delicately curved lines of the Manchu buildings resembled a walking anachronism!...

[[p162]]

We took our leave of the temples, of the dark grove in which the cypresses were now rustling mysteriously and whose white stone paths lay upon the ground like a skeleton. That it would be a farewell forever, we scarcely imagined then.

Around Mukden there are several other imperial tombs, yet none equals in beauty and solemn serenity the one just described. When people speak of imperial tombs in general, it is this one they mean.

## War Correspondents and the Russian Censorship.

“Just introduce yourself right away to Colonel Pestich,” Major Baron Tettau told me, having looked after me in the kindest manner during the absence of our officers. “Colonel Pestich is the head of the censorship, with whom you will often have to deal, a charming gentleman who speaks German just as the two of us do.”

The censorship office was housed in building No. 14, not far from the railway station, separated from it by a large, dusty square.

Along this square, arranged in three rows, stood low, gray cottages of identical gray color and identical design.

One among them was two stories high—in this one the former Viceroy Prince Alexeyev had lived. The entire complex of houses, which also included a large, square wooden building—the post office—and several border-guard barracks now converted into hospitals, formed the Russian settlement of Mukden.

Over the dark shingled roofs gazed the “Bénédictine bottle,” as people here were wont playfully to call the dome of a lama temple standing at the entrance to the sacred quarter. Temple courtyards with evergreen dark cypresses, artfully carved gates, and latticework enclosed the consecrated dwellings of the priests there, while here the bare, uniform timber-framed walls of the Russian houses, set side by side on the brown sand like soldiers, made a bleak, dreary impression.

The house numbers were painted on the walls in large, clumsy digits, so that House No. 14 was soon found.

[[p166]]

It was a cold, snowless winter morning; from the Hunhe the wind was whistling across again, swirling dust, straw, and other refuse through the wide lanes of the settlement.

[Illustration: Pagoda, called the Bénédictine bottle.]

Tied to the doorknob of the cottage was a small, shaggy nag from which a tall foreigner—probably a correspondent—had just dismounted and entered the house. For one recognizes a non-Russian from afar as such, especially if he is an Englishman or an American. That the censorship office was not very spacious was already betrayed by the dark, narrow entryway, from which, groping forward at random, one entered a clerk’s room in which stood a huge lithographic press. The next little room was designated for the officers assigned to the colonel. The senior among them was Stabsrittmeister Baron van der Höven; by his side was Lieutenant Fehrmann, both of German origin. “The colonel has already been notified of your arrival; I will announce you to him,” said Fehrmann, offering me in the meantime his vacated chair.

“So, a new correspondent!” Baron Höven laughed after a friendly greeting had been exchanged. “Hopefully you know nothing of military matters and don’t speak Russian either—those are the ones we like best, just like that fellow there,” whereupon he tapped the gentleman who had entered before me familiarly on the shoulder, introducing him to me as the correspondent of the Reuters Office. I was wearing civilian clothes at the time, and Baron van der Höven was somewhat astonished when Lieutenant Fehrmann, with the words, [[p167]] “The colonel requests the Rittmeister’s presence,” invited me to follow him.

Colonel Pestich received me exceedingly kindly. He showed me the telegram that, at the instigation of our ambassador, had arrived at the army high command about a month earlier. “*You have only this support to thank*,” remarked the colonel, pointing to the telegram, “*that you are here, for for some time past*

[Illustration: On the left Colonel Kovanko, chief of the aeronautical department, on the right Colonel Pestich, chief of the censorship.]

*all requests from foreign officers who wished to proceed to the theater of war have been rejected without exception.*”

My papers were then checked; I received a red armband and several passes entitling me to enter Mukden and to take photographs outside the perimeter of the fortifications, the railway, the barracks, and bridges.

Finally, Lieutenant Fehrmann handed me a voluminous lithographed sheet—it contained the regulations, [[p168]] or more accurately, the prohibitions, by which correspondents and other foreigners were to govern their conduct with the army.
