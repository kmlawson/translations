From an operational standpoint, the supply situation was likewise not unfavorable. An army on the retreat is easier to provision than an advancing one, since it continually draws closer to the stores stockpiled to the rear. Furthermore, the entire campaign was composed of a series of operational pauses, during which provisioning was all the easier. During battles and other operations, however, the commissariat’s supply arrangements very often failed as a result of the army’s poorly organized supply train. At Mukden, the *one-month supply of provisions for the entire army had to be burned*; after such catastrophes there were, to be sure, days of hunger as well. The Russian soldier is a hearty eater, yet he does not grumble when he must go without food for days on end. His midday meal, humble though it may be, the Russian soldier never takes without thanking God for it. How often did this make me think of our Hungarian soldiers, who throw their midday meal into the refuse if it does not happen to suit their spoiled palates. Often it was already late in the evening before the men reached their march objective or returned from their positions. Patiently they then waited until the unlimberable kitchen wagon—an excellent, commendable arrangement—came driving up. They passed the time until then with innocent banter; one or another would pull out his harmonica, and there were always men who were not yet too tired to dance to it—perhaps they did so merely to keep warm. Once [[p255]] the steaming little field kitchen had finally arrived, then—without jostling or shoving—the men formed up radially around the wagon; no officer was present, no non-commissioned officer shouted about, everything proceeded quietly and patriarchally. When at last the final man in turn had been served, they took off their caps as if on command and sang a brief prayer.

Yet even with meager fare they never forgot God; they thanked Him just the same for a midday meal that consisted only of tea and black hardtack. Never was I witness to a quarrel among them, nor did I ever hear a coarse word from these good-natured men. Not so the Caucasians. The proprietor as well as the waiters of the military attaches’ dining car were Georgians—in their appearance and speech bearing the closest resemblance to Israelites. Not a night passed without a brawl breaking out among these people, and even in the most peaceful interaction they shouted and clamored at one another. At the railway station in Harbin there was never such a commotion as when Caucasian infantry arrived.

As for the role that tea plays in the Russian army, both as a refreshing beverage and as a preventive measure against various infectious diseases, I have already discussed this at the beginning of this book.

It remains now only to say a few words about the all too well-known and frequently discussed drunkenness of the Russian, and here too let it be said straightaway that in this respect as well the muzhik is better than his reputation—the dangerous drunkards are to be sought not in the ranks of the common soldiers, but *much further up*! That the Russian soldier, when he gets liquor, drinks it and, being unaccustomed to it, quickly becomes intoxicated, is certain; but that happens elsewhere as well. Cases of drunken soldiers in the area of the operational army were rare. After exhausting marches and engagements, when one or more jugs of *tschinn* fell into the men’s hands, they quenched their thirst with this fiendish beverage and soon lay on the ground as if senseless. When during the retreat from Mukden the military attaches proceeded farther north with their train from Tieling [[p256]], the train had to stop suddenly at Kaiyuan. The Chunchuses were said to have cut the line north of the station, and an attack by them on the train was to be expected. Weapons were now sought out, and the Russian escort soldiers in the baggage car were also to be fetched. But they were so drunk that they could scarcely be awakened. Such and similar instances certainly occurred, but they permit no conclusions to be drawn about the troops on duty facing the enemy. Unfortunately, the Russian officer is rarely suited to set the enlisted man a good example in abstinence from alcohol. While today scarcely anyone remains in doubt that even moderate consumption of alcohol impairs the physical performance of the body, I repeatedly heard Russian officers claim that the Japanese always get drunk before their attacks in order to more easily overcome cold and exertion!

The whole country over which the war down there was fought was of no concern at all to the poor muzhik. Never in his life had he longed for Manchuria or for sovereign rights over Korea; the Japanese was unknown to him. That this man, leaving his homeland and kin, was supposed to have gone off to war with enthusiasm could hardly be expected of him. That his military training had been neglected was not his fault either; but what was demanded of him, he did. Willingly and faithfully he held out—and waited until God would help. That God did not help was not his fault either—for he, the muzhik, had prayed enough.
