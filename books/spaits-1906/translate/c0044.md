At dinner, more guests were usually gathered than at midday, as now one, now another attaché had brought along his friends from the positions to this lavish table. Cheerful though the mood was, people rarely stayed together past 9 o'clock. Then a [[p154]] little chat was held in the corridors of the carriages and good night was wished.

Many a man would then take out the letters from home in his quiet retreat, which he had only hurried through during the day, to reread them now word for word in peace and with the quiet joy of remembrance.

## Visit to the Imperial Tombs.

Captain Edlund's horse had already been sold. Binder-Kriegelstein had forestalled me and purchased the bay for a relatively high sum. Binder seemed to have been lavishly endowed by his newspapers, for money was no object to him. For me, only Edlund's Chinese saddle remained, in which, incidentally, it was far more comfortable to sit

[Illustration: A cart on the road]

than in the thickly padded Cossack saddles. There was now also a small Mongolian grey for sale, one of those horses that had been assigned to the Spanish military attachés. This little grey was reputed to have the habit of refusing to be mounted and of bucking. Nobody wanted to ride him; thus the balky animal enjoyed a tranquil life, having grown round and fat, while his four-legged comrades [[p156]] were ridden ragged daily at a brisk trot on the hard roads.

The malice of this Mongol horse was not so severe. His refusal to let anyone mount was more a fear of men, who had certainly treated him roughly, than genuine malice, just as his bucking was merely harmless playfulness, out of joy at finally being allowed out of the stable—if one could call the airy reed-mat structure in which the attachés' horses were quartered by such a name.

An opportunity for a trial ride soon presented itself. Lieutenant Colonel Rostagno and Dr. Follenfant invited me to accompany them on a pleasure ride to the Imperial Tombs. We took advantage of the warm midday hours to ride northwards out of Mukden along the railway; then the railway was left behind to the right, and we headed across the fields, past several tombs, toward the dark grey grove whose tall treetops beckoned pleasantly from the monotonous brown of the plain.

A ride around Mukden gave one an idea of the requirements of an army in the field. Reserve installations were established around the city: field hospitals, field bakeries, ammunition depots, a large artillery ordnance depot where damaged guns and other weapons were repaired, a balloon detachment, and various other depots for reserve personnel and horses.

After an hour's ride, the path led through a copse of low trees, from whose green bushes cracked pillars and shattered animal statues peered out. The first temple we came to lay outside the high perimeter wall that encloses the sacred grove. On this one—whether it also contained a tomb, we did not know—the ravages of time had already done considerable destructive work. The massive stone slabs on the ground were cracked, the arch that had once joined two splendid pillars into a portal had collapsed; only the broad marble staircase, which leads up through a second portal to an open terrace flanked by resting stone dragons, is still well preserved. In the background of the terrace rises a high wall upon which images of animals and deities stand out in relief in [[p157]] vibrant colours. The colonnades that enclose the terrace on both sides have fallen into ruin, and the little gods with benign and malevolent faces sitting here are exposed defenceless to the rain and the scorching sun.

[Illustration: First temple courtyard; on the right, Lieutenant Colonel Rostagno and Staff Surgeon Follenfant.]

What a pity that we had no one who could have explained all these things to us. Thus we stood before the monument of a millennia-old culture; in the solemn silence in which these marble colossi have slumbered since ancient times, we sensed the sanctity with which a cult foreign to us had enveloped this place of peace, without understanding the mystical secret that seemed to conceal itself here in every crevice of the walls.

Onward we now went toward the imperial tomb proper, along a narrow bridle path between undergrowth and thorn hedges—we encountered no one.

In the middle of the thick stone wall that encloses the sacred grove in a rectangle, a moderately high gate leads into a well-tended garden whose paths are paved with white stone slabs [[p158]]. A Chinese man has hurried toward us at the gate; he does not permit leading the horses across the threshold—they must stay outside. A second temple keeper approaches and demands—how profane!—fifty kopecks admission. He will accompany us; what a pity that we do not understand him. Fortunately, Dr. Follenfant has read about this burial place of an emperor of the Manchus—the names of these rulers cannot be kept in mind anyway—and acts as our interpreter. From the garden, whose dark green evokes a somber mood, we enter the first temple courtyard; it is the largest of the three which, separated from one another by high, broad walls, lead to the emperor's burial mound. In this first courtyard, our attention is drawn to magnificent elephant statues standing along its sides and leading toward a high pagoda. On the ground floor of the pagoda rests a tortoise of yellow marble, from whose back a delicate obelisk reaches up to the ceiling of the tower—it is the tortoise of longevity, a sacred symbol that is also commonly found by the wayside.
