As chance would have it, after leaving the bank we witnessed a destructive fire. Evidently during the preparations for the great illumination in which the streets of Mukden were to blaze that evening, one of those airy little wooden houses had caught fire and was now blazing brightly toward the sky. In an instant, several hundred Chinese had gathered at the scene of the fire. Everyone was shouting and gesturing; it was a tremendous uproar, but no one was willing to lift a hand against the crackling element that was already threatening the neighbouring houses. Not a trace of a fire brigade, of a fire engine, or even of a few water buckets! Fortunately, a Russian

[Illustration]

officer pushed his way through the crowd with several soldiers, and their resolute intervention succeeded in preventing the fire from spreading any further. To me, the passivity of the otherwise so bustling Chinese in the face of the devastating fire remained an enigma.

Meanwhile, evening had come. Lanterns in the most colourful hues, painted with fierce dragons, hung from the eaves of the houses. The colourful street life, now joined by processions in the most extravagant masquerades—reminiscent of our carnival revelries—was bathed in the shifting tinted glow of Bengal fire, while rockets banged and crackled in between.

[[p187]]

From the pagodas flickered transparencies bearing the images of Chinese deities, while on the cornices wax candles flickered in coloured glasses—just like at home on similar occasions!

All this splendour did not last long. Toward nine o'clock in the evening the last lantern had gone out, and the city once again lay in that nocturnal silence peculiar to the settlements of this land. “By night the evil spirits fly over the roofs,” says the Manchu, and shuts himself into his *fansa* after sunset.

Yet of the wild, riotous life of the Russian soldiery, of which one could read so much in European newspapers, scarcely anything was to be seen.

That here and there people drank well over their thirst, and mostly strong liquor at that, cannot be denied; yet nowhere was the peace disturbed; on the contrary, the

We looked into one more teahouse—our Chinese rickshaw pullers even claimed to know more amusing establishments—yet I preferred the way home, which I covered running alongside the rickshaw on account of the icy cold.

The next morning, dense crowds streamed from Mukden and the surrounding villages toward the Lama Temple, which lay close to the road leading from the railway station to the western city gate. But people had also come from further afield, from Northern Manchuria and even from across Mongolia.

When I arrived with Lieutenant Fehrmann at the artfully carved wooden archway leading to the sprawling complex of the Lama Temple, a whole wagon-camp of *fourtunkas*—the light Chinese cart—had already driven up. The very exterior of these carts, their bodies covered with blue fabrics and their back panels decorated with distinctive poker-work, pointed to the high standing of their occupants.

As cold as it had been during the night, the sun now beat down just as hotly upon the sandy square, so that the Chinese, in whom I had on every occasion observed great care and affection for animals, drew sunshades over the various quadrupeds harnessed to the *fourtunkas*. How gratifying it is, even here in the Far East, among peoples of heathen faith, to find feeling and heart for the defenceless beast, whereas at home the most Christian countries of all, such as Spain and Italy, arouse the revulsion of every finer-feeling human being by the acts of animal cruelty encountered there at every turn!

This time we had the opportunity to observe the little ladies in their festive finery at our leisure. Seated at the front edge of the wagon box, their tiny feet drawn in and their hands gesturing vigorously, they sat chatting and giggling with their neighbours. Merrily they swayed their little heads from side to side, never resting, always in motion, while their narrow, slanting, yet vivacious black eyes sized us up in turn. Unfortunately, we seemed to find little favour with the beauties, for all attempts to strike up a most respectable acquaintance with them were often cut short by quite vulgar pantomimes. [[p189]] Even the timid attempt to photograph a baby that its pretty sister was carrying in her arms was thwarted by a pack of ugly old women, who, with every sign of indignation, held their bony yellow hands before the child’s face. Here we seemed to have no luck at all, and so we turned toward the great temple courtyard, where the clamorous beating of tin drums drowned out the cries of a crowd of thousands.
