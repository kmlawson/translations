How much further we would have marched on with this stoic calm, I do not know; fortunately, at six o'clock in the evening Dolgorukov came toward us with the third sotnia. He told us that the previous day in Tapu he had met half a sotnia of the 2nd Argun Cossack Regiment and urged its commander to advance today jointly via Qingjiapuzi and attack the enemy remaining at Tesinda from the south. The half-sotnia, however, did not comply with this request (why not an order?!), so that Dolgorukov [[p286]] without them advanced once again toward Tesinda this morning with his two sotnias and the infantry company. Arriving toward noon north of the village—roughly where we had dismounted for a firefight yesterday—it was established that the enemy had placed two guns on the height southwest of the village in addition to the two infantry companies, so that Dolgorukov was forced, without accepting battle, to beat a retreat via Mamangou. The infantry then separated from the detachment and reported back to General Maslov.¹)

We had halted in the meantime. Our commander, Lieutenant Colonel Slatin, decided to call off the further advance to Tapu—where we would have run into superior Japanese forces—and to branch off further to the east.

After a short rest in a nearby fanza, in which Dolgorukov's officers had already quartered themselves, we continued toward the northeast. We wanted to reach the next parallel valley to the east that very day, in order to attempt reconnaissance toward the south from there on the morrow. The third sotnia remained behind.

Our march became arduous. By seven o'clock it was dark in the mountains, and the small horses struggled onward with difficulty through the deep snow that covered the narrow floor of the mountain gorge, often leaving barely enough room for a single rider.

The advance guard often became stuck, so that we closed right up behind them and then had to wait again until they had gained some ground ahead. Toward nine o'clock in the evening we were finally on the other side of the ridge and had a wide valley before us. We could not see the advance guard, and the track, too, was barely discernible under the freshly fallen snow that blanketed slope and valley alike.

So we rode on, slowly feeling our way forward step by step in the darkness, until suddenly we stood right before a steep slope—it was the bluffy bank of a river. But now [[p287]] we could already hear men from the advance guard shouting for us to turn right, as a village lay barely a hundred paces to our right.

Soon we were in the narrow lanes. The gates were firmly closed. No one showed himself. I had dismounted from my horse because my feet were aching from the cold. We were just halting in front of a large gate, on which we had begun to knock, when suddenly a shot rings out in a side lane—and immediately after it a second, a third, hard and sharp through the silent night! For a second, as if paralyzed by fright, everything stands still, then everyone reaches for his rifle—but toward where? from where was the firing coming? It is so dark that we cannot see how high the wall beside us is; I recognize Lieutenant Colonel Slatin only by the silhouette of his well-shaped, bristly papakha.—Like him, everyone else on foot quickly vaults into the saddle so as not to be ridden down in the dark by the scattering Cossacks. A few more shots crack through the air—whether our own or the enemy's, nobody could say—then off they go in a wild dash out of the village, through the nearest lanes where men had just been standing. Thank God, the exit is unbarred, and soon we are out again on the wide snowfield. We? Barely fifty riders are behind us—all the rest are scattered to the four winds. Again we are down by the steep riverbank; a few men dismount to find a crossing. All around us everything is quiet once more—from the village nothing stirs. Cautiously we ride upriver—now here, now there, a dark group approaches us, against which each time we ready our carbines to fire—yet time and again they are our own men. But now the Cossacks themselves have surrounded the village; they know this kind of petty warfare from the last campaign against the Chinese, in which almost all of them took part.

In twos and threes they rode back toward the exits of the village and guarded them until the sotnias had assembled in the valley. It was well past midnight when the non-commissioned officers reported that no one was missing or wounded.

During these turbulent hours, the helplessness of a cavalry detachment in the dead of night and in enemy territory had become [[p288]] even clearer to me than I had already been convinced of before. To be sure, the Cossacks had disregarded the simplest security precautions, yet even these would have been of little use under the circumstances. In the dark, a horseman is defenseless; a stray shot brings the advance guard to a halt, a rope stretched across the road cuts off the squadron's retreat! And yet Lieutenant Colonel Slatin acted entirely correctly in not having us bivouac in the open that night, but instead moving with his sotnias into the next village—it was Zabei, which we reached around one o'clock in the morning. In village streets, the effect of enemy bullets is limited, and once one has quartered oneself in the houses with the necessary caution and barricaded the entrances to the village, the enemy will hardly be able to surprise the resting troops. It is otherwise in an open bivouac—where we traditionally expect a higher state of readiness. In an open bivouac at night, a few shots striking among the horses are enough to turn everything into a pell-mell, in which everyone merely tries not to be trampled by horses tearing about in wild stampede.
