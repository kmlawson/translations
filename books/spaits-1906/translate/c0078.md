Once again I was heading into the unknown, toward what lay ahead in anticipation. I felt as easy and free as I had back then when I rode away from Huangshan. Soon I began to chat with my two new companions. They did not have much to tell; both had only arrived a few weeks earlier from the 1st sotnia, which had been stationed to the rear near Tieling as line-of-communication troops since December. To my surprise, one of them asked me if I knew German—and then began to stammer out a broken jargon that he claimed to have learned in a “German town.” His geographical lexicon was so poorly stocked, however, that despite all my efforts I could not figure out where this “German town” was located—and what its name was, he had forgotten himself.

We had probably been riding for two hours in the dark night when we reached Yaogou. A sentry challenged us and led me to the officers, who were sitting together over tea in a [[p266]] fanza. My arrival aroused surprise—which struck me almost as suspicion. My papers were soon inspected—the precaution of carrying my railway pass with its affixed photograph also proved its worth here—and warm handshakes soon showed me that I was welcome.

### Attack of Prince Dolgorukov's Detachment

The sun had not yet risen above the dark, jagged heights when the 3rd sotnia assembled in the narrow lanes of Yaogou.

Dolgorukov did not fail to send a few men ahead to send back reports to us about the enemy; it was the first time that I had seen a Cossack detachment march off in good order and properly screened, and with the dispatch service not neglected; this inspired confidence in me toward the detachment and its commander. Behind us marched the sotnia of the 2nd Argun Cossack Regiment, and to our flank half a company of the 24th Infantry Regiment. Its scout detachment had already set out before us; we caught up with it along the way, but let it proceed ahead again onto the heights flanking the road, while we remained down in the valley.

Our route led along a river that flowed now against one rock face, now against the other, so that we had to cross it frequently.

The ice was smooth and hard, so that the wind, which was raging once again, drove the little horses before it across the slick surface. This time we still envied the infantrymen, who with sure footing in their rough felt boots marched across the slippery expanse, in which the fiery-red ball of the sun, now rising in the east above the heights, reflected its first warming rays.

It had been very cold in the fanza; the chill was still deep in my bones.

Russian officers. Repeatedly he rode forward to the vanguard and gave instructions to its commander; not a single Chinese did he let pass without questioning—one of our Cossacks acted as interpreter; he was constantly studying the map and explaining the situation to me as far as it was known to him. On our right, that is to the west of us, General Danilov stood with six battalions; we had been hearing his artillery since sunrise. Behind us to the north stood General Maslov with four battalions, a few guns, and the 3rd and 6th sotnias of the Chita Cossack Regiment. His task was to block the road to Yingpan.

Thorough military education by our standards Esaul Dolgorukov probably did not possess either; he had previously served in the Guards in Petersburg and had later been attached to the embassy in Constantinople to learn Oriental languages. Yet what he needed to be a capable cavalry officer his active, open mind had soon acquired in the course of the campaign. *A clear head and steady nerves* in a cavalry officer will always make up for a certain lack of formal military schooling, whereas a horseman made short-sighted and nervous by rigid routine and the nitpicking inspection drill is utterly useless before the enemy. Thus among the former Guards officers assigned here to the Cossack regiments there were many whose military knowledge was just as full of gaps as that of the original Cossack officers, and yet they were far more useful than the latter. They were men of the world with quick perception, whereas the others had lost all elasticity of thought and action through the monotonous life of barracks and taverns.

Around 7 o'clock in the morning we thought we heard Japanese artillery fire from the south—and still the horsemen sent ahead had brought us no word of the enemy. I say horsemen sent ahead deliberately here, and not “reconnaissance patrol,” for such things did not really exist among the Cossacks. Dolgorukov had the right instinct; annoyed that he had still received no report about the enemy, whom he was bound to be very close to by now, he rode forward to the vanguard himself. “You see,” he said to me, “that is where our Cossacks fall short; they are dashing, daring fellows, they would [[p270]] go through fire for me—but of dispatch service as handled in modern cavalries, they haven't the foggiest notion! I sent the best men of my sotnia ahead, but knowing the Cossacks, they've spotted a few Japanese somewhere and are now riding after them until they've cut off their heads or ended up lying there themselves with a bullet in the belly—that they are supposed to send me reports seems secondary to them—and nobody ever taught them, either!”
