“You see,” Colonel Pestics added with a sour expression, “even that the Russian censorship has to let through.”

## The Catholic Mission. The New Year’s Festival in the Lama Temple.

In war, Sundays and holidays lose all right to a special status. Of the latter, it had only been Christmas whose pious celebration the Russians did not forget, even in threatening proximity to the enemy. Wherever Russians lived, in the mud huts, in the *fanzis*, even in the shrapnel-proof dugouts of the positions, the *yolka*, the Christmas tree, burned on Christmas Eve. The troops arriving from home had brought their comrades little spruce trunks or at least green twigs from the forests of the Urals, whose resinous fragrance wafted through the camps like a greeting from the homeland.

Later, no time remained for such celebrations, and the priest had to be glad if he could recite a short Mass for his men on Sunday morning, which among the Russians—by our standards—still lasts long enough.

The attachés attended these religious services only on special solemn occasions. The French, on the other hand, seemed to be more devout than the rest; they went every Sunday to Mass, which a French bishop celebrated in the mission church.

The way to the mission led from our railway car along the southern city wall past the Chinese cemetery, an uneven, neglected place made as repulsive as possible by heaps of rubble, scattered refuse, and carrion. Here lie the dead in free-standing stone or earthen coffins, placed alongside one another in disorder, many of them overturned by the dogs sniffing for prey that lounge here in packs. The stench of carrion was so overpowering that we left our curiosity to inspect some of the seemingly quite handsomely painted coffins [[p181]] unsatisfied. Through crooked, filthy lanes, in which *arbas* laden with sacks of flour frequently blocked our way, we went on until a halt was made before a stone perimeter wall. We entered. A broad open space, where the ruins of a tall church portal stand before us. As if seeking to warn, the mighty pillars of the wall tower toward the sky; they tell us that the ancient conqueror of the world, the Christian Church, having sent one of her children here, must still halt before this soil for a good while yet. The church had been built by the French in the nineties and fell victim to the fury of the Boxers in 1900. The Christian community, Europeans and converts, had withstood the onslaught of the besiegers at the outer stone wall for days, but then Chinese regular troops arrived, before whose artillery fire the Christian community fled into the interior of the church, where they were slaughtered to the last man by the Boxers storming in after them. The rubble of the breached church walls covered the bodies.

[Illustration: The Catholic mission church destroyed by the Boxers in 1900.]

[[p182]]

Today the bones of the victims, so far as they could still be found, rest in a grave against the churchyard wall; the interior of the ruin has been cleanly cleared of debris, but for divine service a bell invites the faithful, tolling from the roof of that plain little house that stands low and modest beside the tall, cracked pillars. The congregation is already gathered upon our arrival. The Mass has begun. A hymn resounds from within; it is meant to be a Christian church hymn, yet foreign, almost incomprehensible is its tune. We enter. The bishop, with the golden tiara on his head, stands at the altar; two yellow-skinned altar boys swing the censers. The space is densely packed with believers; they sing in chorus, they sing the same hymn that the missionary sings to them, and yet it sounds so completely different! A Catholic Mass hymn with Chinese rhythm! Yet that signifies nothing; the children of the mission are said to be good, upright Christians who love their faith and have embraced it out of conviction. So the missionaries told us, who lead a life of renunciation here in the service of their calling. One may think what one will about the usefulness or harmfulness of missions; one may also hold the view that missions, in their striving to make proselytes, have often caused discord and grave misfortune in the countries concerned—yet this one thing all of us who have encountered these champions of the Christian faith on their thorny path must admit: that not worldly reward, but only faith in its most sublime form can move a man to devote himself to such a life, which brings only toil and danger, but never earthly gratitude.

Here in Mukden, too, opinion about the mission was divided. Most people said, and there will certainly be much truth in it, that many a Chinese becomes a Christian merely to be protected by the mission against the avenging arm of Chinese justice.

After Mass had ended, the mission bishop delivered an address in Chinese, which he seemed to speak fluently, whereupon the faithful lined up along the walls for Confirmation, which the bishop now [[p183]] began to administer. Two Chinese mission priests accompanied him and distributed holy pictures to the children, which the little rascals came to show us with boisterous delight.
