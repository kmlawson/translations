### Grouping for the Attack.

*12 January, 5 p.m.*

This attack grouping is certainly quite a peculiar one.

Of the available cavalry, one *twelfth* is designated to carry out the attack proper, but *eleven twelfths* [[p84]] to cover it—that is, for a secondary task. This measure does not appear to have been unjustified, since Yingkou was occupied by only weak forces, while stronger forces were to be expected from Dashiqiao.

Unjustified, on the other hand, is the haphazard throwing together of the attack group from twelve squadrons, each from a different regiment! In vain would one search the textbooks of tactics or army organization to find even the slightest justification for such an arbitrary tearing apart of units.

[Illustration: Transbaikal rifleman.]

It was exclusively *personal*, private motives that guided the commander in such arrangements: trusting in numerical strength, he was certain of his success at Yingkou. Yet the individual regimental commanders were also jealous of the laurels yet to be won—each wanted to have taken part in this victorious action. The relationship of the Russian superior to his subordinates is, while preserving discipline in every respect, an extremely collegial one. Mishchenko was one of those superiors whom his subordinates loved without exception and for whom the men were enthusiastic. By our standards, his good-naturedness and consideration for personal interests often went too far. So too here. In order that he might pin the St. George upon the breast of each of his regimental commanders, every one of the twelve regiments was to have its share in the glorious capture of Yingkou!

Similar motives may have accounted for the failure to place the attack group under a unified commander, the immediate consequence of which was that the mining detachment moved into the firing line alongside the squadrons, both of its officers were shot dead, and the mining detachment performed the demolition work assigned to it later only deficiently.

To open the engagement, the artillery opened fire at 6 p.m.—again, therefore, too late! The fire, already begun at dusk, could be sustained for half an hour at most; then it was night, and the movements of their own troops could no longer be seen from the artillery position.

As can be seen from the text sketch below, the infantry attack could only begin after their own artillery fire had ceased.

How severely its effect was already impaired by the darkness is shown by the circumstance that, shortly after the start of the action, a Japanese officer departed Yingkou station on a locomotive heading eastward, without the Russian artillery succeeding in hitting the locomotive brought under fire.

Just as unhindered at the same time, a train arriving at Yingkou from Dashiqiao was able to bring up approximately a battalion of infantry on open flatcars and detrain them right there.

The Japanese did not succeed, however, in bringing further troop transports from Dashiqiao to Yingkou, as the pioneer detachment assembled from the squadron pioneers had in the meantime destroyed the railway line in question with pyroxylin demolition charges.

It is characteristic of the Russian intelligence and reconnaissance service that at the beginning of the engagement the strength of the enemy [[p86]] in Yingkou was not even approximately known. Weak Japanese forces had ensconced themselves in the small houses lying north of the railway station, from where they offered stubborn resistance to the twelve Russian squadrons slowly advancing.

Toward evening these Japanese detachments withdrew to the station, where the fighting continued until around 10 or 11 o'clock at night, when the Russians took possession of it. The demolition work that was now to be carried out was begun, without, however, any particular damage being inflicted on the Japanese.
