### With the Second East Siberian Mortar Battery.

At daybreak we left Xiaoyangxintun—a name hitherto completely unknown in military history—and walked to the battery, where the horses were just being harnessed. “Another glass of vodka, comrade?” the captain invited me into his dugout. “No vodka for me, thank you, but a glass of tea would be most gratefully accepted.” It was a small, narrow space, partly dug into the earth, its inner walls neatly scraped down and hung with color prints and photographs. In one corner hung a guitar with a blue silk ribbon, between whose dainty bows the picture of a girl was pinned. In the other corner, an icon of the Mother of God with a little red lamp before it.

My head had grown heavy from the clamorous night. The senior lieutenant showed me stereoscopic photographs he had taken while serving with a battery to which he had previously belonged. They showed the dead laid out in rows, or men mutilated by shell hits, whose contorted faces seemed to come alive beneath the stereoscope lenses.

“Before going into action, one shouldn’t look at these things!” the captain said, taking the pictures away from us. “It gives you a hollow feeling rising up from the stomach!” He had hit upon the right words: a hollow feeling rising up from the stomach—how often had I felt this at the sight of the wounded, without being able to define the unease I experienced.

A non-commissioned officer came to report that the commander of the [[p207]] battery—I did not know him yet—had ridden ahead, and that the captain should bring up the battery.

An hour later the mortars were in position to set Nanshanpu, on the opposite bank of the Shahe, on fire. At first the affair seemed like target practice; the ground was frozen bone-hard, so that the impact of the high-explosive shells was difficult to detect; on the enemy’s side nothing stirred. Later on, however, they too began to fire from across the river, and that was uncomfortable. Anyone who could press himself against an earthen parapet had little to fear, and the ear soon grew accustomed to the whistling and whining in the air—not so, however, the officers and the crews serving the guns. The infantry officer and the cavalryman are swept forward in the tumult of battle—forwards or backwards, as circumstances dictate; they have no time to reflect, often barely to recognize the danger. Not so the artillery officer. He can determine to the minute when the enemy projectile will strike his battery; he sees the Shimose shells exploding closer and closer in front of his guns, and all the while he must calmly observe their effect, determine sight corrections, and keep the enemy target in view. And how much more devastating is the effect of a hit in the battery than in the ranks of the other arms! How much more disheartening are the severe, almost invariably fatal wounds caused by shell splinters compared to the clean perforations of infantry bullets. I believe *that in modern combat, of all officers, those of the artillery require the greatest moral fortitude* to be equal to their arduous duties under enemy fire.

The battery was fortunate; all the shells the Japanese sent flew many meters above our heads, throwing up the dirt far to the rear.

Around midday the men of the first echelon hauled up ammunition, but also reported that near the horses of two ammunition wagons, which stood unharnessed further to the rear with their drivers, a shell had struck, injuring two of the animals and killing one man. This was [[p208]] the brother of a gunner serving here in the battery; they brought him a prayer book and a crumpled letter they had found in the fallen man’s pocket.

Again a shell whirred over our heads; the heavy shells sing quite differently from their lighter sisters, the slender shrapnels. This time the men crossed themselves, for it had passed just over their heads.

The telephone connecting us with the observation post reported that Nanshanpu was beginning to burn.

The battery stood behind a sopka on which stood an enclosed redoubt that, incorporated into the line of positions, formed one of its main strongpoints. I had accepted all the more readily the invitation of its commander to drink a glass of tea in his secure dugouts, as the battery officers had asked me to leave the battery, since Japanese shells were expected to strike at any moment. The cold, too, had become biting as I lay motionless behind the earthen embankment, so that inwardly I was glad to crawl on all fours with some semblance of dignity out of this—at the very least not comfortable—situation.
