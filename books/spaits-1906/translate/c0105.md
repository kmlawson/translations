The total loss which the bank had suffered during the hasty evacuation of Mukden amounted to no more than some [[p347]] thirty thousand rubles. The wagons on which the silver bullion had been loaded had gotten stuck in the swamp near Puhe—everything else slowly turned up in Harbin.

Colonel Kovanko, the head of the Russian aeronautical section, had also witnessed the confusion at the Puhe in the morning hours of 10 March. Among the wagon trains, as he told me, the provision wagons of the army high command had also fallen victim to the panic during the night. Crates of champagne bottles, tin cans, zwieback, and the like, which had rolled from the overturned wagons, lay scattered here across the field. All the misery was not without its comic interludes: Cossacks struggling to uncork champagne bottles, then bravely raising the painstakingly opened bottle to their lips to pour the sparkling drink down their throats, only for it to come foaming back out through their mouth and nose! Others had better luck with cognac bottles, while tin cans that were finally pried open with sabres and contained nothing other than condensed cream were tossed aside in disappointment and disgust!

Naturally, this blockage here also had a delaying effect on the departure of the subsequent wagon trains from Mukden. *Thus it happened that the military attachés' baggage trains came under fire from Japanese artillery at 10 a.m. near Dawa, ten versts north of Mukden (they had progressed no farther than that in twelve hours!)*

The attachés' wagons, on which their bulkier baggage had been loaded, scattered in all directions as the Japanese shrapnel rained down; as luck would have it, I found them again later.

The attachés themselves were by this time already close to Tieling, where their train, which had left Mukden several days earlier, was awaiting them. Yet not all of them were together this time; some, including Szeptycki, as we know, had remained forward with the troops. Of the Americans, Surgeon Howard and Captain Judson of the Engineers had remained behind in Japanese captivity in Mukden, while Major [[p348]] Macomb, like myself, had looked for the train in Tieling and was said to have ridden right past it, but, failing to hear Lieutenant Colonel Rostagno's shouts, had soon lost himself again in the general turmoil.

The plain, roughly four versts wide, between the Mandarin Road and the railway was densely blanketed with soldiers. They were either columns hoping to make faster progress off the road, or once again those individual travelers tramping north quietly and peacefully, as though out for a stroll! Of panic there was really nothing left to be seen; the waves of excitement that the hasty evacuation of Mukden had stirred up during the night were already smoothed over, and everyone and everything went their own way, singly, in packs, in columns, just as it happened.

“Where is the road to Harbin?” a Jewish reservist asked Colonel Kovanko. — “What do you want in Harbin?” — “Everyone is heading for Harbin, so I’m going too!” — “And where is your rifle?” — “I lost it.” — “And did you lose your cartridge belt too?” — “What good are cartridges to me without a rifle? I threw them away.” — “And what are you carrying in that sack on your back?” With that, the soldier threw the sack to the ground and ran off. In the sack, however, were found more than two dozen pocket watches!

Among the columns marching north, I identified north of Tieling on 13 March troops of the 61st Division, the 3rd Division in its entirety, further troops of the 35th, 4th, 22nd, and 55th Divisions, two batteries, a military bridging train, the 4th Siberian Landsturm Battalion, several machine guns, and mortars.
