## Fighting with the Second Siberian Corps.

### Kandolisan.

On February 20, the fire on the positions was particularly heavy; it lasted through the entire night. The Chinese, too, began sending their women to the rear in whole cartloads. That was always a sure sign that the Japanese were planning an attack.

The next day, on a cold, bright morning, my patron, who had helped me so much during these often difficult days, escorted me to the military road leading to Shandalinsan to the Third Siberian Corps; this was the immediate destination, from which I was to proceed further to the extreme left flank to a Cossack regiment. “God keep you and take care of yourself—come back to Mukden again soon; in a fortnight or so, I might be there too!”—and then I rode on alone into the sunny day, into the mountains whose northern slopes shimmered in white snow, while the southern exposures, thanks to the warm midday sun, displayed the monotonous brown that gives the entire country its dull coloration. From the Shahe valley, the cannons rumbled; beside me hummed and chimed the telegraph wire, the surest signpost to the next headquarters.

My destination lay far away, far over on the army’s extreme left wing. I was to report to the commander of the *Second Chita Cossack Regiment*. As to where it currently was, I would learn more precise details only at General Rennenkampf’s headquarters.

I felt so easy and free; at last I could go wherever I wanted, bounded by neither time nor space; as far as the rations in my saddlebags and the [[p202]] Japanese bullets permitted, the way was open to me in every direction.

As for the rations, by the way, that was not to be taken so literally. In this war, the Russian officer had to live quite frugally, and often the barest necessities were lacking; but what he had, he was always glad to share with strangers. Without this *hospitality, which was nowhere lacking*, it would also have been impossible to make any headway. Yet just as courteous as the Russian is as a private individual, just as much vexation and difficulty arise the moment one has dealings with the cumbersome administrative apparatus, sunk in perpetual disorder and ignorance. Toward this “sloppiness,” the Russian displays a patience bordering on lethargy. The officer takes it for granted that the commissariat does not bother in the least with procuring his subsistence, and he pays the Chinese heavy sums for the adulterated food and drink brought in via Shanghai–Xinmin. There are no Russian merchants here; they are too indolent to pick up the money lying, so to speak, in the street—though perhaps the railway may also be to blame, which, it is said, carries no private freight from Russia, or at the very least assumes no guarantee for it.

The road was deserted and lonely, so much so that I thought I had lost my way. At last, I met several horsemen.

“Am I on the right road to Shandalinsan?” I asked them—pointing out the route I had taken on my map.

“Just don’t ride by the map, sir!” the soldiers said almost simultaneously—“put that stuff in your pocket, or you’ll certainly get lost!”

Well, the Russian four-verst map hardly deserved such mistrust—rather, perhaps, those who had once led these good fellows “by the map”!

The road I was riding was, in fact, the right one; it ran a few kilometers north of the positions of the Second Siberian Corps, above whose lines little silver clouds [[p203]] of bursting shrapnel hovered high in the air, while massive geysers of churned-up earth, standing out sharply against the blue sky, betrayed the effect of the shimose.

From time to time I halted to listen to the rumble of the artillery and the crackle of rifle fire. Over there things seemed to be getting serious—yet what was stopping me from going over to have a look? Was I not at last—at long last free—could I not go adventuring wherever I pleased?

As if to assure myself fully of my freedom of movement, I turned off immediately cross-country to the south, toward the heights of the Second Corps.

Sound and distance are sometimes deceptive. After nearly an hour’s ride, I realized that the Russian positions lay yet another ridge further to the south.

The sun stood high at noon; the villages through which I rode were devastated and deserted, with no one to be seen far and wide, so that I was glad when I spotted a group of horsemen and headed toward them.

It was an artillery colonel, a veterinary surgeon, and a few soldiers. With the amiability of Russian officers that has already been highlighted so often, the colonel invited me to accompany him—he was on an inspection ride. Whatever he might be inspecting, I thought to myself, here too superiors will surely be welcomed with a good meal; and so I rode along.

In a quiet valley, well away from shrapnel and shimose, a mortar ammunition park led an idyllic existence. With the good artillerymen, anyone is in good hands; and so the temptation was great when they suggested I abandon my further wartime travels in order to listen here, in peace and quiet, to the rattle of musketry and other battle clamor drifting over from the redoubts, all over a well-spread table. During the day, from a not too distant sopka, one could comfortably watch one’s own battery and a Japanese one duel back and forth. Toward evening, the wounded were carried past our fanza to the division hospital; they told us what had occurred in the positions during the day.
