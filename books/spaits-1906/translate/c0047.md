“As you see, a great deal is forbidden with us—actually everything, strictly speaking,” the colonel remarked jokingly. “You, however, as a soldier, Captain, will surely know even without these regulations what is permitted and what is inadmissible in war. I am convinced that we will remain good friends—and now we have finished with official business. Now give me the pleasure of having breakfast with me.” That was indeed a most collegial reception, which could not have been better back home!

Lieutenant Colonel Bazarov of the General Staff had also come over for breakfast, with reports and private letters from the attachés, whose correspondence was likewise subject to censorship. “And where are your war correspondents, Colonel?” “Oh, please don’t get me started on the correspondents!” Colonel Pestics sighed dolefully. “Right now they are all sitting here in Mukden, and that is just the way I like it; as soon as one of them steps outside, there is invariably trouble. The earful I got again today from the Army High Command on account of Little—you know about it, don’t you, Lieutenant Colonel Bazarov?” “Certainly, Colonel—though you should have taken a firmer line back then, in my opinion.” “It simply will not do for a correspondent who is granted permission to accompany General Mishchenko on his raid to slip off in the dark to the Japanese in Yingkou just so he can dispatch his cables.” “You gentlemen must make allowances for the irresistible urge that draws the newspaperman even through enemy lines to the telegraph office—that I can well understand; but how did this gentleman get back into the Russian camp—why did he not rather stay with the Japanese?” “Why not with the Japanese?” all the censors together with their chief now interrupted me at once. “Why not with the Japanese? Because nowhere in the whole world do correspondents have it so good as with us! With the Japanese they are kept neatly tucked away—but with us—I ask you, look [[p169]] at our two Englishmen, one of whom you met today; we ourselves, that is Baron van der Höven, who is assigned to the English press, dictates all their telegrams to them, for the fellows cannot speak even a single word to anyone, and when they ride out of Mukden, they lose their way at the next fork in the road; it is we ourselves—the gentlemen of the censorship—who dictate everything to them; could one be any kinder? “To your health, Captain, and forgive me that today, in your civilian clothes, I too took you for a military illiterate; you are one of the few officers who come here on their own initiative to look into conditions among us; mostly they are people who in their previous occupations *have never even come into contact with the military profession*, and now they want to see, understand, and properly judge [[p170]] things in wartime—these are the sources from which the civilized world draws its news! It would never occur to us soldiers to pass judgment on things we have never learned, that we do not understand; yet here come correspondents from all continents, some of whom have never even sat on a horse, and want to correct commanders in the field. *The Germans alone*, one must give them credit, their newspapers have sent only militarily competent people here. There was Colonel Gaedke and Lieutenant von Schwarz, and at present there is still Lieutenant von Ulrich, who is taking part in the entire campaign with General Orlov . . .” “Engaged by General Orlov, so to speak, to immortalize his deeds for posterity,” one of the Russian officers put in rather maliciously. “Binder-Kriegelstein is an officer too.” “Certainly, he even attended the war academy with us,” I added, “and then continued his service in the German army.”
