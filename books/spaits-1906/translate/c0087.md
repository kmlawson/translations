Slowly we retraced the same path by which we had come. Exhausted by the exertions of the last few days, the horses dragged their hind legs sluggishly and stiffly under the heavily packed Cossack saddles. Two were wounded; these were led along by the reins. In the village that the sotnias had left that morning, everyone was already asleep; the Cossacks banged and clamoured at the heavy wooden gates until the Chinese opened them. We found our fanza just [[p296]] as we had left it; we intended to stay here only an hour or two, after which we were to move on again. The Chinese had placed a lantern of coloured paper on the low table. Leaning against the walls in silence, smoking their little opium pipes, they watched us as we sat even more silently around the large iron kettle, from which Yesaul Belyaev filled our glasses from time to time.

The order from the regimental command stated that the sotnias were to withdraw to Yingpan as quickly as possible in order to rejoin the rest of the regiment's sotnias there. That amounted to saying that the Eastern Corps, and with it perhaps the First Army, had been thrown back!

Lieutenant Colonel Slatin urged our departure; we had to try to get past Zabei so that the Japanese coming from Mamagou would not cut off our route! “Better early tomorrow before sunrise than now in the dark of night,” the two chiefs countered, “our horses

[Illustration: Chinese beggar.] [[p297]] are tired and the men have eaten hardly anything today.” So it was decided to spend the night here. We had posted no outposts, yet no one feared that the enemy might take us by surprise. “What do you suppose would happen to you if we were suddenly surrounded by the Japanese?” one of the officers asked, pointing at me. “They might even take you for a spy and hang you!”

“I have my papers with me, after all,” I replied. “Nonsense! Papers! Who looks at papers in wartime! The Japanese would certainly hang you—still better than if Honghuzi take us by surprise, for then all our heads would be adorning the alleys of Nanjiahe tomorrow!”

Jesting thus, we emptied a few more cups of tea, and then the officers threw themselves down on the straw pallet. Through the window of the fanza pierced the glow of a flickering blaze. I went outside into the courtyard, where the Cossacks had stoked a roaring fire over which a plump little pig was roasting. Leaning against the doorpost, I gazed into the leaping flames and listened to the soft singing of the soldiers camped around the fire. Someone tapped me gently on the shoulder; it was our host. He gestured for me to follow and led me to his wives’ room. Astonished, I followed him and entered the dimly lit room—it was empty. Madame, along with all her friends and servants, was gone! “You may sleep here undisturbed until tomorrow morning,” seemed to be the message conveyed by the gesture of the Chinese man, who had prepared a warm bed of soft blankets for me in the corner.

Yet I was not alone here—from the corner of the fanza, behind a paper screen, came faint groaning. There the Chinese man had prepared pallets for our wounded from yesterday; the feldsher of the Amur Cossacks had dressed their wounds.

*9 March.*

We had marched off from Zabei early in the morning—though without the Amur Cossack sotnia. Its commander was sleeping too soundly! “Post sentries on the sopkas around the village, and if the enemy comes, wake me!” he had, [[p298]] as we took our leave of him, ordered his *starshik* (senior non-commissioned officer), and then pulled his coat back over his head. A few weeks later I met one of the officers from this sotnia. They had not been ambushed at the time, but that very morning, just as we were, they were cut off by the Japanese, and were only able to find their own army again after wandering for weeks through the Korean mountains. Thus, through the sleepiness of its commander, this sotnia was crippled for the remainder of the campaign.

At a walk, we fell back toward Zabei. On the way, the Cossacks brought in a Chinese man from the next village. The interpreter translated that Japanese troops in the next valley to the west were on the march toward Zabei. Was this true or not? As usual, we had sent out no patrols. But since the retreat had begun, the Cossacks, too, had grown indifferent and cared little about the enemy now. In retreat they are entirely different soldiers from when they are advancing.

“Why don’t we trot?” I asked the commander. — “Don’t you know that all the horses’ backs are saddle-sore?” was the reply; “after all, their saddles haven’t been taken off for four months!” We continued on at the most deliberate walk. Just before Zabei, a rider from the vanguard approaches, calling out from afar: “Over there,” and points with his arm toward the village, “the Japanese are advancing!” How many? Where they are going? No one even asks about that anymore.
