## Fighting on the Eastern Flank.

### To the Chita Cossacks.

On the night leading into March 4, the Japanese had once again pushed back the left wing of the Eastern Detachment by a contour line. Our Chinese even claimed to have seen Japanese on the mountains near Xiaoshangou, thus almost in the rear of headquarters. East of the Eastern Detachment were still the separated groups of Generals Lyubavin, Maslov, and Danilov, likewise subordinated to General Rennenkampf; there was imminent danger that they would be completely cut off from the Eastern Detachment.

“If you still want to get across to the Chita Cossacks, then make haste,” Rennenkampf told me. “Today you will still find the staff in Xidihezi—where it will be tomorrow, I might not even know myself. This afternoon a platoon of Argun Cossacks is crossing the mountains to General Lyubavin; your best course is to ride along with them!”

The officer leading the Cossacks was known to me from Mukden: he wore the Cross of St. George and seemed to be an able man. We also had a Chinese guide with us to show us the way over the mountains. Along a steep bridle path we climbed from Machuntan up toward the heights. When we reached the top, that abominable wind was once again sweeping across the stony plateau, a wind that in this country never seems to run out of breath. General Lyubavin was not far off—at the Tongshiling Pass, whose deeply depressed saddle lay about seven versts ahead of us. A wild, rugged region. From Lyubavin’s batteries we could distinctly count the guns, and we soon discovered their opponent on the heights to the south. Our Chinese guide urged haste. Once again we went down [[p263]] a stony passage and up another, through scrub and thorn hedges, the only vegetation covering these bare mountains.

We must already have been close to the Tongshiling Pass; we could distinguish the reports of the guns, with only a small deciduous wood, eking out a sparse, solitary existence here, concealing the view from us. Suddenly one of the horses—we were leading them dismounted by the reins—leaps to the side, dragging along the Cossack, who stumbles over a stone and falls full length. Immediately afterward a second bullet smacks against the yellow sandstone, shattering it into dust. We

[Illustration: Landscape west of the Tongshiling Pass.]

do not know where the shots are coming from, and turn back. Now we see that our thirty riders, whom their commander had paid little heed to during the march, had dissolved into a long line, the tail end of which was just descending from the opposite height. Yet over there, too, bullets seemed to be striking; about three or four of the men abandoned their horses and ran toward the small wood in which we were still standing. Where are they firing from? The wind howled from the east, carrying over to us only the dull boom of the artillery from the Tongshiling. Blood ran from the wounded nag’s right shoulder; it could go no farther; we left [[p264]] it behind and ran, as fast as we could make headway on the stony ground with our tired horses, down into the next valley, which we would have had to cross anyway. Now, however, we stayed down on the valley floor until we reached a tributary valley of the Hun River, where in a small village—Tongshigou, if I noted it down correctly in the haste—we met General Lyubavin. We had lost two of the horses. Toward evening, their riders went back to fetch the saddles, which are, after all, the property of the Cossacks.

No one cared any longer who had fired the shots or where they had come from. “Honghuzi!” was the simple answer to that.

General Lyubavin, who had the reputation of being a cavalry general, was not a very dashing cavalry figure. Short, fat, and somewhat greasy. The officer had a large, thick roll to deliver to him, which he now carefully opened before us. “At last they are sending us maps,” he said, already pleased, but soon his face flushed crimson with righteous anger, and with a fury I would never have suspected in the genial gentleman, he slammed the whole bundle onto the table, sending the map sheets flying across the floor of the fanza. Then he went out. We picked up the sheets; there were a well-counted thirty maps of—Sandepu, where a month earlier the great battle had taken place, seventy versts from here on the opposite flank!

Now only one stage separated me from the Second Chita Regiment, with which I was to see out the remainder of the campaign. This last stretch I rode in the pleasant company of a Guards officer, a yesaul and commander of the 2nd Sotnia of the said regiment, which was tasked with maintaining the flying post between Tongshigou and General Danilov—northeast of the latter was my destination for today: Changdahezi, the station of the regimental staff. We might have been riding for barely half an hour when two Cossacks came toward us. “Where are you heading?” “To Yaogou, to the 3rd Sotnia, Prince Alexander Dolgorukov. We come from General Danilov and are carrying the orders for tomorrow.” “We are going to attack Qingjiapuzi,” the second added proudly. “And [[p265]] the regiment?” I asked. “That I don’t know,” the Cossack replied, “I haven’t been there.” “Do you know what,” I said to my companion, “I will ride with these two Cossacks to the 3rd Sotnia. I am free, I can go where I please, my propusk is made out for the Second Chita Regiment, and besides, I carry a letter of recommendation from General Rennenkampf with me.” “In that case, though, you still have a good stretch of road ahead of you today—Yaogou is far, you won’t get there before nightfall, and tomorrow morning things will move right along.” “Then I would have a favor to ask of you, Yesaul; would you permit one of your men to take the heavy bag from my horse’s saddle, and my camera will also be unnecessary to me. Could both remain with the regimental staff until I happen to arrive there?” “With pleasure, but make sure to stuff your pockets thoroughly with chocolate!”—“Do you have good food there?” he interjected, asking one of the Cossacks. “Oh, food, sir, there’d be enough of that, it’s only the ‘little water’ that’s missing!” The repacking was quickly finished; I had no map of the area I was now entering—yet worth ten times more than a map was the natural sense of direction of the Cossack, who, like a homing pigeon, always found the right way straight ahead across unfamiliar valleys and heights.
