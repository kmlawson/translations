## Manchuria Station.

[Illustration: Manchuria station.]

A station scene, colourful and chaotic, such as I have hardly ever seen. The buffet hall wooden and spacious, poorly lit, crammed to the very last crevice with people and trunks. Foul-smelling Chinese, perfumed ladies, gendarmes, porters, generals, all in a wild jumble. Where to put my trunks? Who will watch to ensure they are not stolen from me? When does the train to Harbin leave? etc., questions to which no one can give me an answer. To make matters even worse, two Cossack chieftains are brawling because the staff refuse to give them any more vodka. Left alone, a person would be lost in [[p57]] this turmoil. Unpleasant as the Russian official may be, whether stationmaster or postmaster, the Russians are just as charming as travelling companions. They apologise for all the indignities one has to endure from the authorities, and endeavour through their amiability to make amends for the poor impression made on us by railway and postal officials. With the help of a Red Cross doctor and an officer, I finally manage to get my belongings into safety; as to everything else, complete uncertainty reigns. We are already in the staging zone of the active army; there, for a private individual, all reckoning comes to an end!

Characteristic of the Asian stations was that nobody knew anything. The considerable delays of the trains render any timetable illusory; one arrives—and waits until things move on. So for the time being one has leisure here to look around at one's surroundings. The female element consists mostly of families of Russian officers travelling from Vladivostok or Harbin to Russia. Beside me a darling little girl with a Chinese bonnet on her blond head; she is crying because the dark Cossack chieftain wanted to take her into his arms. Heated argument between chieftain and mama. How little homogeneous the Russian officer corps is! Elegant, intelligent physiognomies, then again people who with us would never be made non-commissioned officers. In this wooden shack, the magnificent Chinese vases that flaunt themselves as centrepieces alongside artificial palm trees have a comical effect. Waiters with greasy hands and white ties. A table, festively set, is being reserved. Fresh guests pour in again. Those can only be Germans! Tall, strapping figures, monocle clamped in eye, impeccably white caps of Prussian cut with the Red Cross upon them. The two chieftains are requested by their comrades to make themselves scarce for a while, because the "Germanic" Red Cross has just arrived. I was never an admirer of Prussia, but in these surroundings the personnel of the German hospital train, soon gathered in full strength at the reserved table, seemed to me like a greeting from the civilized world. These are men, these are women who, wherever they go, cannot fail to command respect. [[p58]] After a modest supper, their chief issues orders. The German hospital train will proceed tomorrow at 8:40—and it departed not a second later! We? The stationmaster thinks tomorrow at noon, the station commandant says tomorrow evening, etc. Each something different. Fortunately, we are allowed to spend the night in a railway carriage. But first we want to have a drink. We are speaking German, and a thoroughly tipsy Livonian sits down with us. He is a railway engineer and tells some choice stories. A stationmaster was recently crushed by a locomotive. Out of boredom he had been drinking vodka. Then, so as not to miss the train, he had lain down to sleep with his ear to the rails. The vodka, however, was stronger than his guardian angel, and the locomotive ran over him! To this opportune acquaintance I owe magnificent photographs that our new friend placed at my disposal the next day: scenes of wartime life. Will they reach their destination unconfiscated, I wonder?

There was, after all, one small reminder of home. At a merchant's shop where I bought provisions for the onward journey, I found a whole spool of red-white-

[Illustration: Russian and Chinese station in Manchuria.] [[p59]] green ribbon. Unfortunately he was a Buryat, and so the origin of our tricolour remains unknown to me. Our train did not leave at 2 o'clock as was initially stated; it is now supposed to leave at 10 o'clock in the evening. Why there are no longer any second-class carriages here, I do not understand. We shall be travelling with a transport of reserve horses. Four days on the hard benches of third class will have little appeal. On the other hand, the trip costs nothing! A peculiar zone tariff.
