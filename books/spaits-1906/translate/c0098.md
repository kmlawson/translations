#### March 9.

March 9 began with a hurricane-like *dust storm*. The fine brown dust-sand covering the vast plain from the Hunhe valley northwards beyond Mukden was swirled aloft in thick, massive clouds. In places, the force of the wind forced men to cling to the nearest trees, walls, or whatever else could be found; the dust settled in thick ridges on the eyelids and prevented seeing. Trains were still running on the railway line, at least to evacuate the hospitals; yet only the roar of the locomotive and the shrill sound of the steam whistle gave away the approach of a train—everything was shrouded in an opaque, indeed almost impenetrable yellowish-brown cloud of earth.

At 8 o'clock in the morning, the first Japanese shrapnel rounds burst over the station north of Mukden. The trains had to keep running in order to transport at least the most indispensable part of the war materiel. In the preceding days, large quantities of artillery ammunition had been detonated in deep trenches near Mukden, evidently that for the heavy guns. South of Mukden, for several days already, [[p324]] tall clouds of smoke had been rising from the immense *firewood dumps* that had been stacked near Gajiazi and had to be burned. For March 8, as already mentioned, orders had been issued whose execution was intended to place a strong reserve—approximately *eighty battalions*—under General *Zarubaev* at Dawa at the disposal of Army Headquarters in the course of that day.

In its execution, the attack of March 9, which General Kuropatkin directed in person, fell far short of its conception. For the reasons discussed earlier, barely more than some twenty battalions were at the direct disposal of the Commander-in-Chief on the morning of March 9, who had arrived with his suite at Cuertun during the early hours of the forenoon. At that time, Japanese artillery shells were still falling near Army Headquarters, so that it was compelled to disperse over a wider area—in the manner of a swarm—in order to present a less favorable target. To provide a linear sketch of the fighting west of Mukden for the ninth would be difficult, as far as the situation could then be surveyed from the Russian side. The units, which had already been heavily intermingled since the beginning of the fighting through the continuous combining of new formations, had become even more mixed and disrupted under enemy pressure over the past day. Thus, for example, on the forenoon of March 9, detachments of the 217th Regiment, belonging to the 6th Siberian Corps, were retreating across the railway north of Mukden, its men saying, "We are scattered," while almost at the same time a composite battalion of the 54th Division, belonging to the 5th Siberian Corps, was advancing westward not far to the south, the individual companies in single-rank line at intervals of about 300 paces from one another.

Coordination among the individual groups seemed to have been little regulated, so that the battle on the western front dissolved into a series of *isolated engagements*, the general tendency of which was that the individual groups, seeking to gain ground toward the west, attacked the villages situated immediately west of the places mentioned at the outset. To [[p325]] all this was now added the dreadful dust storm, which made any overall view, and perhaps also unified direction within the groups, impossible.

The Commander-in-Chief personally led his approximately thirty battalions against the enemy, and this *attack, directed uniformly toward Cuertun*, gained ground to the west; the *enemy here was far weaker* than expected and fell back.

The enemy artillery fire grew noticeably weaker toward noon; the Imperial Tombs had been spared altogether by the Japanese—seemingly intentionally. Without encountering significant resistance, General Kuropatkin's offensive group advanced from Cuertun via Yuhuantun toward the southwest, and the attacks of the other groups were likewise attended by success. These local successes already caused the fortunes of the day to tilt in favor of the Russians.
