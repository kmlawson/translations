At two o'clock in the morning, General Maslov arrived, to whom we were henceforth subordinated along with the staff and the third and fifth ¹) sotnias, while the second, fourth, and sixth sotnias of the regiment were assigned to General Lyubavin in Tsanta, west of General Danilov's group.

We received orders to march to Wangao, and around three o'clock in the morning arrived at a village that was densely packed with troops.

We waited here for an hour; men thrown together from the most diverse regiments crowded around the fires in the narrow village lanes. Some had been standing here since yesterday, waiting for orders. Many did not know from whom they would receive orders, for the organization of the troops had changed almost every day of late.

The regiment resumed its march to the northeast at four o'clock in the morning.

On the extreme left wing, General Lyubavin had to secure the flank and wing of the army with nine squadrons. He knew that the Japanese at Inpan could not be strong, and attempted to push south with his brigade—but in vain! The decision had already fallen in the west.

On March 10, we on the extreme left wing were still far from knowing that Mukden had already fallen the previous evening. Even the generals only suspected that over in the west a heavy blow must have struck [[p335]] the army, but where the army high command might be at this time remained in doubt—though it was certain that there would never be any going "straight on to Liaoyang"!

At Shanzalintsu I left the regiment in order to learn something more definite in Tieling. Should it no longer be possible for me to return, I was at least to write to the officers with exact information about the past events on the western wing; they wanted at least to know the cause of the general retreat. Esaul Dolgorukov sent three of his best Cossacks with me. To spare my horse, which after much difficulty had finally allowed itself to be shod, I also bought the horse of a wounded man through whose knee a bullet had passed the day before.

[Illustration: Brief rest with General Maslov's brigade.]

My route led from Shanzalintsu to General Maslov at Maabentsa. I found his troops resting on the retreat at that place. From here the way continued over the [[p336]] Fuantun Pass, which was occupied by General Danilov's 23rd Rifle Regiment. In the evening, I arrived at Sainpan, at General Rennenkampf's headquarters.

Even Szeptycki could tell me nothing definite about the loss of Mukden at that time—one was simply always and everywhere in the dark about what had happened just a few versts away!

In the evening, in the general staff section, the casualty lists of the subordinate troops were compiled. The figures I heard here spoke terrible truths. Forty—thirty—sixteen—yes, even *twelve* men was the remaining strength of the companies!

"Which company has only twelve men left?" I asked the staff captain who was reading out the regimental reports. . . . "The 13th Company of the 283rd Regiment!"

After dinner, I chatted with Szeptycki for a while about the events of the last few days; Szeptycki was full of praise for Rennenkampf and the troops under his command. "I believe," he said, "we would do our fatherland a disservice if we told people at home that this army is bad! Relying too much on its weakness could perhaps lead to bitter disappointment for us in the future!"—There is certainly much truth in these words. In the Russian people lies unmistakably the seed of an excellent—if not the best—army; someone would only have to come along and make that seed sprout!

The next morning, long before the sun rose, I was already on my way so as to reach Tieling before noon. I had no map and veered too far to the south, back in the direction of Fuantun, whereby I became witness to an infantry engagement one last time. It was General Eck's division that was indirectly covering the retreat of the Third Siberian Corps as it fell back toward the northwest here. Yet it too was soon forced to withdraw to the steep northern banks of the Fanhe River. The Japanese did not press, and the columns marched in the best of order. Without our even having heard enemy artillery fire, [[p337]] little white clouds were suddenly visible above us, whose rocket-like puffs only too soon revealed themselves as exploding shrapnel. To be sure, they were only stray shots, but as ill luck would have it, they too found their mark! The picture on page 311 shows the effect of a cone of dispersion that had struck the retreating column along its length.

On the northern bank of the Fanhe, along which I rode past the Third and Second Siberian Corps, entrenchments were hastily thrown up. In the eastern sector assigned to the First Army, the river formed a very favorable defensive line. While the valley plain extends broadly to the south, the northern flanking heights push close up to it and, through the sheer steepness of their slopes, already form a formidable obstacle in their own right.
