And we Europeans, much-tested tacticians, still delight in that fine spectacle of maneuvers when our reserves advance into the front battle line *tambour battant*, in lockstep and neatly dressed! We think of everything on such occasions except two things: first, that [[p238]] we have no men who would walk into this storm of bullets—which is what it is in reality—for us; and second, that if they did, nothing would be left of them after a few minutes!

In such a case, one must simply push forward a *living wall*, as the Japanese do—sacrificing battalions, even whole regiments, as a living wall, behind whose corpses the actual battle line only then establishes itself; *yet we have no such living walls.*

Toward evening the fire began to slacken on both sides. A brief lull in the fighting occurred, during which the severely wounded and the dead were carried out of the trench. To replace them, half a battalion was brought up, which, like the first, was led into the position under cover of communication trenches.

That same day I rode on. The Russians had so far lost two thousand men in the sector of the 3rd Siberian Corps, but they held out bravely on the three heights still left to them; the Japanese were only able to seize these when the 3rd Corps received the order to retreat from the 1st Army Command.

It was not thanks to the higher command that this stubborn resistance was offered, but solely and exclusively to the brave Russian soldiers, whose virtues and failings I intend to describe in greater detail.

## The Russian Soldier, His Clothing and Food.

“General, sir, our Little Father sends me to report most obediently that our men have been in action for five days; scarcely more than three men remain for each gun.” So reported the old artillery sergeant, who had come late at night from the Gaotuling Pass to Panjiazi to see the artillery brigade commander, General Schwerin.

“Tell your colonel that your men must hold out and wait. God will help. Go!”

“We must hold out and wait,” repeated the old non-commissioned officer, from whose shaggy mustache glittering icicles hung in long cones. “God will help!” With that he saluted and walked slowly, his head bowed, out of the low, smoky dugout into the dark night.

How few words had been spoken, and yet in them lay an infinite wealth of faith, hope, and resignation. But one had to have heard the voice in which the old man had carefully, deliberately repeated each word—the simple, convinced tone that sees help already in the solace of patience and God alone.

Just as he had come, he made his way back, heavily yet carefully. The thick fur coat and the heavy felt boots permit no brisk striding. It is a hard road in the dark night. He walked it today for the first time; he had never been in this region before, yet he did not lose his way; without a map—he cannot read one anyway, he cannot read at all—guided only by that innate sense of direction peculiar to the children of the vast steppes. The way back is easier. Far from high up [[p240]] over the mountains, the howling and whistling begins again; six white flashes shoot up into the black sky—those are bursting shrapnel shells that the Japanese are sending at the Russian batteries in salvos. Six, and another six, and in such rapid succession eight salvos. “That is eight batteries against our four,” the artillery sergeant mutters. Well, what does it matter; after all, the general said: “Hold out and wait.” The old man thinks no further—he is not really old at all, but only looks so gray and weathered; he goes on his way, repeating his general’s words so as not to forget them: “Hold out and wait!”

Wounded men are carried past him. They lie beneath thick woolen blankets and do not stir; perhaps they are already dead.

Vasili Stepanovich—such is our artillery sergeant’s name—lets them be carried past him without concern; he must keep his memory together so that not a single word escapes him of what he has to report to Little Father Polkovnik ¹).

Now he reaches the mountain slope along whose ridge his batteries stand. A white light, with a red cross upon it, points out the way. Here, close against the slope in the courtyard of a ruined temple, is the advanced dressing station. He steps inside to ask for the colonel. Wailing and groaning. Against the wall, right beside the gate, the returned stretcher-bearers have set their canvas litters. Ugly red bloodstains can be seen on them; some litters are as if soaked through with blood. These are stiff as leather, for the blood is frozen.
