Colonel Zakrzewski had likewise woken up. As we shook hands, he told me that when leaving I should not forget to cross myself before the icon of the Mother of God on the regimental standard, which was leaning against the wall outside and beside which a Cossack stood guard.

*March 6.*

On the way to Xujiazi, where Lieutenant Colonel Slatin's two sotnias were stationed, he explained his mission to me. The forces confronting Dolgorukov's detachment were to be attacked from the south, and in addition Lieutenant Colonel Slatin was to determine how far east the Japanese flank extended.

As far as I had seen on the previous day, Prince Dolgorukov had not remained facing the enemy at all, but had withdrawn to Mamagou, which, however, was not yet known to General Danilov at the time he issued the above order.

Far more important than supporting Esaul Dolgorukov's sotnias, however, was the second part of the mission—ascertaining the easternmost Japanese advance column.

For modern cavalry on reconnaissance duty, combat can only be a means to an end! The main thing is that it approach the enemy, gain insight into his dispositions, and report what has been observed to the rear as quickly as possible so that the necessary measures can still be taken there in good time. If this insight into the enemy's dispositions can be obtained without fighting, so much the better, for combat means *loss of time*, whereas the reporting service aims at *gain of time*.

From this point of view, it must be considered inappropriate that Lieutenant Colonel Slatin's reconnaissance detachment, in addition to the vital task of "ascertaining [[p284]] the easternmost Japanese column," was also assigned a secondary task—a combat engagement to achieve a completely inconsequential success—which could not fail to distract its attention from its most important activity: reconnaissance.

That among the Cossacks reconnaissance activity was something unknown altogether, so that no attention could be distracted from it in the first place, was something I was soon to see!

Around 7 a.m. we arrived in Xujiazi, where the officers of the sotnias were awaiting us. Commander of the 4th Sotnia was Esaul Belyaev; he had been garrisoned on the Galician border many years before, and had now been serving with Cossack regiments in Siberia and Manchuria for eight years—which one could tell just by looking at him.

Commander of the 5th Sotnia was Esaul Pavel Skoropadsky; I had made the acquaintance of this charming Guard officer a month earlier in Mukden, and only regretted that he was not present here at the time, but was on leave in Harbin to visit his wife.

Besides the commander, two more officers were assigned to each sotnia. The sotnias were around a hundred horsemen strong and carried no baggage train with them apart from a pack horse with ammunition crates.

The regimental doctor was bored at headquarters, while we did not have even a feldsher at our disposal.

At 8 a.m. we set out. In order to bypass the Japanese flank, we had to swing far to the east—our destination for that day was Dapu, several dozen versts east of Dexinda. There were Russian troops neither south nor east of us, and yet we marched quite carefree into the fine, bright morning, toward the gloriously shining sun that already stood high in the east. It felt so good to see cheerful spring light again after the long, dreary winter days—even if it was bitterly cold.

The two sotnias had sent ahead a platoon as vanguard, which rode four hundred paces ahead of us—that was all we did for our security—and so it remained later on!

[[p285]]

There were no reconnaissance patrols. No one was nervous.

Thus, toward midday, we arrived at Bapanlou, where we took a one-hour rest in the courtyard of a Chinese, during which no security posts were posted. When the march resumed, another platoon went ahead as vanguard. From then on our route led south along the valley, whose river line we had to cross several times.

The area was lonely and deserted; fewer settlements than on the plain, but here they were far better built than there. If we met Chinese along the way, they came up to us and readily answered our questions. Their last words were always: "Give me rubles!"

It was already evening when, eight kilometers north of Dapu, we met a platoon of retreating Argun Cossacks—probably from the half-sotnia that had spent the night before in Dapu—though we did not ask them about it. In passing, the commander told us that an infantry column with artillery had marched into that village in the afternoon—its strength he did not know! In an hour at most, therefore, we were bound to run into the enemy—and glad of the beautiful evening descending upon us, we marched on. Even now no reconnaissance patrols were sent out, and no one became nervous. Soon afterward we encountered an infantry company marching back. It was the one that had been attached to Prince Dolgorukov the day before. It marched past us without saluting, and neither did anyone among us ask where it was coming from or whether it knew anything of the enemy!
