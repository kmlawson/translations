Now we are already so very close to the war—yet it is simply impossible to gain even an approximate picture of the events of the last few days from all the confused talk. Even the outbreak of the war, so I am told, became known in Harbin through private reports four days after the first bombardment of Port Arthur—and officially only after another four days! The postal and telegraph services leave much to be desired in the great empire of the Tsar! The foreigner at least is left defenseless against the arbitrariness of the officials in question. More than once it happened to me that they refused to accept a letter, the Herr *nachalnik* simply declaring that he was under no obligation to know where Hungary was! Here in Harbin, the main staging station of the war's rear, a tiny little post office is operating; hitherto it has been so crammed full every day that I have not yet managed to reach the counter. Some regulation of the inn-

All the inns here are bad, but I believe I must especially warn travellers coming to Harbin against the "Hotel Ariante."

The description of my journey to the Russian headquarters would be incomplete were I not to mention the final leg from Harbin to Mukden. Immediately after my arrival, I had registered with the station commandant for the mail train and, three days later, stood on the platform at the appointed hour. I was already rejoicing at the small crowd when I learned that, once again as an exception, the train would depart four hours later. Hitherto there had been so little difference between the rule and the exception, and besides, I am already so well practiced at waiting, that I would not mention these few hours at all had I not whiled them away in pleasant company. A gentleman who knows Budapest and is travelling to his wife, who lives there. To speak of home is a pleasure one only comes to know in a foreign land.

Courteous, like all the Russian officers I had met so far, the station commandant had intended a seat in second class for me and called my name first during the assignment; unfortunately, at that time I was still standing before the closed ticket window, which the cashier opened only after prolonged knocking and shouting from those waiting. Meanwhile, all the seats had been distributed; only a few in third class were still free, and these were just then taken by storm. Anyone left behind here must travel in a *teplushka*. The men shout, the women plead, everyone shoves, squeezes, and pushes toward the narrow carriage door, in which, to make matters worse, a suitcase is wedged. Modesty achieves nothing here! After a brief struggle, I am inside the carriage. No sooner have they forced their way in than people climb like monkeys toward the ceiling; the berths are arranged in three tiers, one above the other, and whoever is at the very top can at least no longer be trampled on. I managed to secure a spot on the *bel étage*, where I immediately stretched out full length as a sign of taking possession. My new acquaintance pushes my suitcases in after me—a handshake: [[p67]] “Give my regards to Andrássy Street!” And we were already rolling.

It was quite cozy. Beside me stood soldiers. I could only see their heads; they were eating something that smelled of garlic. Spurred boots dangled down from the upper tier. In one corner, singing soon began, and the chorus joined in. Where people sing, there settle down in peace, for evil men have no songs, I thought to myself, placed a few handkerchiefs over my mouth and nose as an air filter, and tried to fall asleep. The Cossacks were from the Don. They sang a song that resembled the “Körösi kis leány” down to the very last note.

[Illustration: Large military ramp at Harbin station.]

At first the familiar melody delighted me: I tried to make out the lyrics from the medley of voices, but they were completely different from the Hungarian.

With sleep, however, things would no longer go quite right this time. I was excited. After all, we were now heading straight for Mukden. I thought back to that hour when I departed from Vienna. How confident I had been then, how lightly I had estimated all those obstacles that might be thrown in my path along the way. What would I have done if in Petersburg they had flatly refused my request and not granted permission to proceed to the headquarters of the Russian Manchurian Army? I believe I would have gone *all the same*; at least I would have tried it, after all. Now everything was happily [[p68]] behind me. The plank on which I lay was hard, right next to me were the door and the cold window—smoke, fumes, and exhalations to the point of suffocation—but beneath me the wheels hummed and rolled once again, straight toward Mukden. What will it be like there? Will I find our two military attachés? Is the army still near Mukden, or is it perhaps already in victorious advance upon Port Arthur?
