No order, no guiding hand to *employ the good and willing material, available in abundance, expediently and sparingly of strength*. And so the poor nurses wander back to the railway station that very evening. They ask the stationmaster when the next train will leave for them. “Wait!” he too tells them, and the girls, often barely eighteen years old, sit down around a round table in the smoky refreshment room, which has not been aired out since the onset of winter, rest their heads on their arms, and sleep sitting up until morning. Thus they lie, sit, and stand in the waiting room for another day or two until a train takes them along. If all goes well, crammed into a third-class Pullman, the journey takes ten days back to Harbin, where in the hospital wards the groaning and rattling sufferers already await their care. Yet it was not only the hospitals; almost every regiment had its nurses. Although these women were probably more exposed to the dangers and rigours of the weather than their colleagues in the institutions, this arrangement was nevertheless more advantageous, as it was permanent and the nurse felt more at home here than in the constant transfer from hospital to hospital.

Later, in Gongzhuling, I made the acquaintance of Princess O., who had served through the entire campaign as a nurse with a regiment. After the Battle of Mukden, the few belongings she carried with her had been stolen by Caucasian Cossacks. “Our Cossacks are worse than the Chinese brigands,” she said indignantly; “I myself saw pieces of my linen in the hands of the thieves, but my regiment was gone and there was no one to help me recover my property!” The poor woman had [[p364]] nothing left except what she wore on her back, and the campaign was far from over.

At that time, I also met nurses who were returning from the St. George Hospital, which had fallen into Japanese hands at Mukden.

“How conscientious the Japanese doctors are in comparison to ours! When the Japanese doctor took over our ward, he asked for the number and names of the Russian wounded in it. ‘We do not know the names,’ our doctor answered, ‘there are exactly a hundred of them.’ After a good while the Japanese returned; he had noted down the names and regiments of the captive patients—‘but there are not a hundred, only ninety-nine.’—‘Well, yes, I told you *a hundred*,’ our doctor remarked. ‘One hundred—and ninety-nine, Staff Surgeon, is a great difference,’ the Japanese said solemnly—‘especially when it is a question of *human beings*!’

“Such lessons must we, the bearers of culture”—the nurse remarked ironically—“receive from the Asians! What is one human being to our doctors, what are hundreds to them! For we well know that when the first transports of wounded arrived from Mukden in Harbin, our doctors had those who had died along the way laid at dawn in the doorways of the nearest houses, in order to spare themselves drawing up the official death reports.”

The truth of this account, incidentally, was later confirmed to me by German merchants in Harbin; this was said to have occurred on the morning of 12 March.

Imagine now a feeling woman’s heart that had to witness all this misery!

The nurses were young and the war lasted seventeen months. Whether this long period passed for every one of them entirely without piquant memories, I am inclined to doubt—and yet, why not? Certainly the novelist would have found in the hospitals and field infirmaries plenty of tender material for short love stories. For instance, in a hospital not far from Mukden there was a young doctor to whom a nurse was devoted. His superior, who was likewise infatuated with the beautiful nurse, [[p365]] sent the doctor back to his regiment in the positions so that he might remain cock of the walk alone. A few months later, after Mukden—the regiment had stood near the Imperial Tombs during the final days of the battle—we met him enveloped in dust on the retreat. Bringing up the rear was the doctor—and beside him on horseback the beautiful nurse!

Even General Sakharov, the commander-in-chief’s chief of the general staff, had found time to fall in love with a lady of the Red Cross and to marry her—three days before the Battle of Liaoyang!

Among the thousands who wore the red cross, there were certainly some for whom this emblem of mercy served merely as a passe-partout to the theatre of war.

Thus there was a strikingly large number of officers’ wives—including those of senior officers and generals—who had followed their husbands here to the theatre of war.

While in Mukden hardly any other women were to be found apart from those truly engaged in caring for the wounded, the Harbin station and the lower port city offered a rich assortment of the gentle sex—of all categories. Yet how

[Illustration: Sisters of the Cross and doctors of the Red Cross.] [[p366]] could it be otherwise! Manchuria had already been permanently inhabited by Europeans before the war; Vladivostok, Port Arthur, Dalny, and Yingkou were large port and commercial cities whose inhabitants were gradually sent to the rear by the Russian high command as the Japanese advanced—what wonder that dubious rabble of both sexes had accumulated back in the rear staging areas! Now, there were people who feasted their eyes only on these marsh flowers and were blind to the high, noble calling that the Russian woman was fulfilling in the theatre of war. There were also those who believed they saw something wicked in the fact that the nurses carried out their service, which brought them into such close relations with men, mostly without the protective moral wings of mothers, aunts, or matrons! But these gentlemen forgot that the Russian woman had long since broken free from the petty-bourgeois views of our female education, and that here of all places the fertile seed of women’s emancipation, which seeks to qualify woman as man’s fully equal comrade and supporter, had borne its most consummate fruit!
