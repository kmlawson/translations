## Afterword.

Mighty Russia was cut off from the Pacific Ocean. Its cherished dream, which the labor of many centuries had brought so close to realization—to rule freely over a warm sea in the Far East—had been cruelly shattered. Not a ship and not a man could pass beyond the iron ring that the Japanese had drawn in a wide arc from Vladivostok along the coast and then up the Tumen toward Jilin, Kujujan, and in the west into Mongolia, around the shaken symbols of Russian power.

Yet my own favorite plan, to return home across the cheerful blue sea and still see Japan and India, had likewise come to naught.

Disheartened, I had returned from the Gobi Desert to Transbaikalia; all the exertions and privations there had been in vain. The difficulties of travelling on from Urga via Kalgan to Beijing exceeded my means, and slowly, day and night, the jolting tarantass made its way toward Lake Baikal.

It was a May night with snow and ice. The carriage halted before the post station to change horses; I peeled myself out of my furs and stepped into the small, cramped room. An Orthodox priest sat here with his five children, who had curled up together on the floor and fallen asleep.

So we sat here for a good while by the dim candlelight—taciturn and drowsy.

Slowly the priest's postilion came in through the door, bowed and crossed himself before the icon lamp of the Virgin in the corner, and announced to his master that the tarantass was ready. When the door opened, cold air had rushed in; we [[p370]] shivered. It seemed as though the priest had not heard the driver; motionless, his head resting on his right arm, he looked at his children. The other man remained standing and looked at the priest. At last, weary and slow, the latter turned around and said: “Wait!”—“I shall wait!” the simple man repeated deliberately, and walked with dragging steps out of the room—into the cold night to his horses.

Wait!—I shall wait!—Did it not seem to me as if I were hearing the general and the sergeant of Padzyaza once again!

*Wait, and wait again and again*—that is the great watchword here in Russia, turning days into months and months into years!

The entire war had begun under the auspices of *waiting*; indeed, at its outset, Commander-in-Chief Kuropatkin had demanded *patience*, *patience again, and a third time patience* from the Russian fatherland. Perhaps, without knowing it, he had thereby translated into Russian the well-known words of Montecuccoli, who demanded money, money, and yet again money for waging war! For money—power—is there; the Russians need only *time*—but *a great deal of time*—in order, despite all the sluggishness and corruption of the obsolete autocratic system, to bring this power to bear. They have entirely different views on the value of *time* from us fast-living Europeans, and the gigantic dimensions of their empire make the *value of space* seem far less there than among us, who must live on a narrower plot of earth, yet have been able, free and unhampered by the whims of an autocratic government, to develop intellectually and economically.

War, as the supreme exertion of a nation's capacity, is at the same time the best yardstick of its efficiency; it is the bloody harvest festival of the good—or bad—seed sown in the people over decades. One must not seek the explanation for the Russian defeats purely in military considerations. These alone would leave many questions unanswered: it is in the people themselves, in [[p371]] their religious and state institutions, in the national flaws that have embodied themselves over centuries into a specifically “Russian system,” and in bureaucratic malpractice that one must seek the primal sources of those evils, which naturally carry over into the army as well and prove the untenability of this system there.

Whoever travels for days and weeks through the endless plains of the Russian Empire, whoever waits there for weeks and months for the simplest matters to be attended to, whoever is lied to there deliberately or unintentionally at least once every day, soon comes to the realization that time, space, and truth play a vastly different role in the great Tsarist Empire than among the peoples of the rest of Europe, where corruption—the mother of lies—is held in check by the system of public control. Rigid autocracy had sealed the empire off from the rapid historical development of its western neighbors; the despotic rule of some two hundred families, who, with the aid of an ossified and corrupt officialdom, must keep the people in apathy and indolence for the sake of their own self-preservation, has hampered the free intellectual development of the indi-
