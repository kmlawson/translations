## Riga.

*Early December 1904.*

The detour to Riga would hardly have squared with my impatience to reach Mukden as quickly as possible, had I not had dear relatives there. From them I hoped to receive many a useful piece of advice and an unvarnished account of the mood in Russia regarding the war at that time.

For at home it had been so difficult to form an accurate judgment of the course of the war from the newspapers. Aware that our entire press was anti-Russian, I was firmly convinced that the Russian cause had to be faring much better than could be assumed from the endless calamitous reports that were trumpeted to the world distorted and full of malicious glee.

My optimism suffered its first jolt here. I spoke with men whose word I could believe—among them wholesale suppliers to the navy and the railways. Corruption was said to be most blatant in the purchasing and acceptance commissions of the navy. The name of an Admiral M. was mentioned to me, who had issued bribery schedules arranged in downright tabular form for the various deliveries! In return, however, the contractor was permitted to deliver only fifteen thousand pieces instead of thirty thousand, and to split the remainder with the head of the acceptance commission.

Another gentleman had supplied railway frogs to a line in the Urals, I believe for four rubles apiece.

A year later, he bought back the very same goods from the officials for a few kopecks, only to sell them back to the government [[p16]] another year later at the original price! Railway construction in Russia must indeed be expensive—or seen another way: how immensely rich this country must be to be able to build so many railways under such a system!

However much was stolen in Russian railway construction, Russia’s rail network, including its Asian possessions, is the most magnificent in any country. Nowhere do we find conceptions so gigantic and at the same time so purposeful as here. The construction of its monumental railways across Siberia, Transbaikalia, and Manchuria to the ocean, and of that from Orenburg to Samarkand toward the Afghan and Persian borders, are not only technical masterpieces that moreover reveal a strong, resolute policy, but Russia has also rendered a great service to the entire civilized world through these projects, bringing both Europe and America closer to the previously closed interior of Asia. This was always one of those considerations that heightened my sympathy for Russia.

Riga is a splendid city, so completely different from other Russian cities. Its inhabitants are mostly Germans. They all sincerely longed for the ultimate victory of Russian arms—most believed in it, too; they all had a stake in the events in the Far East.

Few parts of the tsarist empire were struck as painfully by the casualty lists after the first engagements as the Baltic provinces; not only is a large part of the naval crews drawn from them, but, following old traditions, the nobility of these lands has its second sons serve in the army or as naval officers. The conspicuously large number of German names that became well known among Russian army and naval commanders in the course of the war bears eloquent witness to this. Midshipman von Schlippe, son of the Marshal of the Nobility of Volhynia, whom I visited, is one of the seven officers who survived the catastrophe of the battleship *Petropavlovsk*; his account, coming from an authentic source, will certainly still command interest today.

“An unfavorable factor,” von Schlippe recounted, “for the Port Arthur fleet was the narrow harbor entrance, which at [[p17]] that time was passable for larger ships for barely two hours before and after high tide. Our bold Admiral Makarov sought to make up for this disadvantage by personally taking command of small squadrons and going out to meet the superior enemy, with the intention of drawing the Japanese onto Russian mines through feigned maneuvers. The adversary, for his part, tried to obstruct our wide-open roadstead with mines, which, however, mostly failed thanks to the vigilance of our coastal searchlights and torpedo craft; a great many Japanese boats were ‘bagged’ in the process, and their mines rendered harmless.

“On the night of March 30–31—it was around the time of the Russian Easter—our torpedo craft had once again clashed with Japanese minelayers. The latter, six ships in all, succeeded in cutting off and surrounding the torpedo boat *Strashny*. Admiral Makarov immediately launched an attack on the Japanese with those ships that were initially able to clear the harbor entrance, but he could no longer prevent the *Strashny* from being sent to the bottom before our eyes, whereupon the enemy, declining battle with us, escaped our fire thanks to their high speed. Soon, however, they had joined up with significant forces and confronted us with superior numbers. Already 15 miles from the harbor, the Russian side had to take into account the risk of being cut off from port, from which no further reinforcements could be expected due to the onset of low tide. Admiral Makarov ordered a retreat, by which, however, he simultaneously attempted to lure the enemy onto our mines. The Japanese did not follow us this time, and soon only the smoke from their funnels betrayed their presence. Unmolested, we steamed back to Port Arthur: first four cruisers, then *Pallada* and *Petropavlovsk*.”
