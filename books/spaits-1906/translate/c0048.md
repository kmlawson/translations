“How is it, though, that apart from the official French military attachés, there are no other French officers in the Russian camp? Given the well-known sympathies of your ally, I would have thought that several French officers would surely be serving in your ranks—much like your coreligionists, the brave Montenegrins? One fine day, a small band of the latter, armed to the teeth, arrived in Mukden to support the Russian-Manchurian army.” — “We did have one French officer,” the colonel explained to me. “After much pleading, he was granted permission to take part in the raid on Yingkou in the ranks of a dragoon regiment. We were all very fond of him, and he was burning with the desire to ride into battle. To take part in a great cavalry engagement was his fondest wish. But the poor fellow had to die without having seen anything of the war; on the first day of the advance, a few shots were fired at the squadron with which the Frenchman was riding—they were probably honghuzi. The first and only man to fall on that occasion was the French officer. You can imagine that this incident did little to encourage our gentlemen war correspondents to risk their expensive hides on similar occasions.” “Why do you say expensive?” “Oh! These people, [[p171]] especially the English and Americans, receive magnificent pay—often purely for being here. At present we have two who would like nothing better than for us to expel them from here. If they left here voluntarily, their newspaper would no longer be obliged to pay them; it would be quite another matter if they were deported *ex officio*, just as we did recently with an Italian correspondent. That gentleman had attempted to forward his dispatches via the Chinese post without first submitting them to censorship.” “Must all letters, even private ones, be submitted to censorship?” “Everything that goes abroad; no Russian post office within the army’s area of operations will accept a letter addressed abroad that does not bear the black censorship stamp; the Chinese post, which runs from here via Xinmintun to Peking and Shanghai, is also bound by this regulation.” “And letters to Russia?” “Those do not need a censorship stamp.” “Then the entire complicated censorship apparatus is actually superfluous,” I ventured to object; “one only needs to have an acquaintance in Russia to forward the letters!” “As far as censorship is concerned, we don't care all that much about letters anyway,” the colonel explained. “By the time they reach Europe, they have long since been overtaken by events; the only things that matter to us are the telegrams. But the Italian also tried to make his way to Peking via Xinmintun, whereupon he was picked up by our border guard patrols.” “Colonel, I must confess frankly that I have that same plan! Xinmintun is a solid day’s journey from here by carriage, and from Xinmintun to Peking is 36 hours by train—to be so close to Peking and not be allowed to see it?” “No, Rittmeister, abandon that idea; there is only one way back, and that is the one by which you came. Even the American military attachés who traveled home from here were not permitted to take that route, for it leads not only to China—but also to Japan!” “Then, Colonel, I drink to the health of the Russian army and to a very swift and victorious advance on Port Arthur—by that route you will surely let me cross over into China?” “By that one, yes! And you [[p172]] will see that we shall soon take that southward route, for God will help us. And now to work, gentlemen. Bring me the latest report from *Novoye Vremya* [*New Times*], Lieutenant Fehrmann—you see, that is a good newspaper; it has honest correspondents who always write nothing but the truth!”

[Illustration: Staff Rittmeister Baron van der Höven as I met him on his return journey from the Gobi Desert.]

Good old colonel, if only you knew that across all of Europe, *Novoye Vremya* is known as nothing other than the Russian *rag of lies*!

Staff Rittmeister Baron van der Höven was a very well-educated man. He had been educated more for diplomatic than for military service, had mastered several languages, including Chinese, and had already lived in [[p173]] East Asia for several years beforehand. I was pleased that he knew Budapest, and in the course of our conversation we even discovered a mutual friend, the Hungarian explorer Count Jenő Zichy, whose name I was later to hear mentioned often in the Caucasus as well. With Höven, one could speak quite candidly about conditions in Russia. The next day he took me to Mukden, where we first paid a visit to the two American correspondents. Together with the American military attachés who had remained with the army, they occupied a handsome, spacious *fangzi*, had a good cook, and smoked excellent long cigars wrapped in tin foil. They could hardly have seen much here in Mukden, but that was not the point at all. The main thing was writing and telegraphing. But whoever was out at the front line, no matter how much he saw there, could never send timely reports to his newspaper. On the one hand, writing there was exceedingly difficult in the first place, for frozen fingers are of little use for hammering out feature articles; on the other hand, there was rarely a post office or telegraph station there, and these were not permitted to transmit correspondents' dispatches any further than back to the censors in Mukden. For newspapers that set greater store by the speed rather than the reliability of their telegrams, it was therefore of little value to have their reporters somewhere on the front line, which after all would not always have enhanced the military value of those reports either.
