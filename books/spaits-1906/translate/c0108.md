### The Military Significance of Harbin.

The pressure of the pursuing army had eased near Kaiyuan. The Japanese pursuit was somewhat more vigorous this time than after the preceding battles—yet a lack of cavalry made exploiting the success impossible this time as well. Anyone who saw the Russian Second and Third Armies in retreat during the days from 10 to 14 March had to admit to himself that here an intact modern enemy cavalry division would have sufficed to dictate unconditional peace to the Russians! It was precisely here that the absence of powerful *battle cavalry* proved its indisputable right to exist!

Just as after all its failures, this time too the Russian army came to a halt wherever enemy pressure ceased.

Since mid-March, the Russian army had been stationed with army headquarters one station south of Gongzhuling, the First and Second Armies pushed forward to Sipingjie and Maimakai, and the Third Army in reserve behind the First and Second. Of fresh troops from Europe, the IV Army Corps and two rifle brigades had arrived. General Linevich’s somewhat naive demand to mobilise six army corps in Europe and send them to the theatre of war by echelon traffic—that is, one train after another without return trains—which would have required in all no fewer than 1,000 trains of 40 wagons each, was flatly rejected by St. Petersburg and earned the new commander-in-chief [[p358]] a very sarcastic remark from his supreme warlord. Apart from replacement transports and some troops from the Caucasus, few military forces were en route to the east. Of the lines of advance available to the enemy, three came into consideration: the direct route along the railway to Harbin; that via Jilin to Ninguta, which cuts Harbin off from Vladivostok; and that via Boduna to Qiqihar, which cuts Harbin off from its previous line of communications to the west. To be sure, the Songhua cuts across the three aforementioned lines of attack, but its southern bank dominates the northern, and its course bends southwards near Jilin, so that stubborn resistance with the forces available at the time was out of the question.

The evacuation of Harbin was only a question of time—provided that the Japanese continued their offensive. But what then? From where and with what to feed the army? Besides the many thousands of wounded who were in and around Harbin, removing the army’s supply

[Illustration: Kuanchengzi, city gate.] [[p359]] provisions, calculated to last for months, would have been just as difficult as in Mukden, although this time preparations for a possible evacuation of the city—such as burning the war materiel that could not be transported away, the timely evacuation of the bank, the removal of private individuals, etc.—had already been made.

Yet what was to become of the army without Harbin? Mukden as a place had only a moral value, but it was quite different with Harbin, which had the only seven steam mills supplying the army with flour, where the only large railway repair shops were established, and which, as the junction of the railway to Vladivostok and the south, was of vital strategic importance! And yet everything pointed to the fact that they were determined to evacuate Harbin!

Indeed, some regiments—such as the Kuanchengzi Frontier Guard Regiment, with which I stayed for some time after the battle—had sent their heavy baggage to Hailar, on the border of Transbaikalia! Indeed, Colonel Thurn-Taxis, commander of the frontier guard regiment in Gongzhuling, had told me himself that all preparations had already been made to blow up the barracks there.

What the German Prince Heinrich was still doing in the theatre of war at this time, when everyone was already thinking of the end of the campaign, was inexplicable to all. At the end of April, he had arrived in Harbin by a detour across the Gobi, since the Japanese would not let him pass through their outposts from Beijing!
