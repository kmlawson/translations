“Is work carried on here every day?” — “Every day from 3 to 7 in the evening, with the exception of Thursdays, when the school children come to us.” — “And who donates the materials for all this?” — “For the most part the Tsarina from her private funds, and partly from the monetary donations received here. Princess Golitsyna performs her duties here, overseeing the receipt and acknowledging of incoming sums.”

“About how many ladies would you say work in the Winter Palace every day?” — “At present about a thousand, but in the summer, when the Empress was here, there may well have been twice as many.” — On the second floor I find several rooms crammed with books and periodicals, old and new, Russian, German, French, just as they arrive and as people [[p27]] bring them in from the street. Reading material for the wounded in the field hospitals. Adjoining this is a room filled with small parcels wrapped in colorful cloth. “Dlya soldat”—“for the soldier” is the simple address. These are gifts from humble folk, which are collected and forwarded postage-free. The Christmas presents already departed last month; every soldier receives a tin box containing sugar, tea, chocolate, tobacco, a pipe, a sewing kit, some linen, smoked meat, and a piece of soap. The entire country contributed to this gift, and in the end every donor enclosed a postcard with their address, asking the recipient to write a few words about how and where he spent Christmas Eve. Roederer are plentifully represented—icing the noble beverage over in Mukden will not be difficult. To my astonishment, I hear that after France, *England* ranks next as the most generous benefactor. Twenty-four large crates from a Miss B. arrived this week, in addition [[p28]] to considerable shipments of port wine. Germany likewise does not lag behind. Via a small spiral staircase we reach the interior of the imperial theater—yet one would scarcely recognize it as such. Rows of seats, boxes, stage, everything covered with crates, bales, or goods not yet packed. It serves as a depot from which loading onto the trains takes place. Two freight trains weekly handle the dispatch of these colossal quantities; transit takes two months, as freight trains must still give way to troop transports. Russia also seems to reciprocate the expressions of sympathy from its neighbors. Whatever cannot be produced domestically is ordered from them; thus I am shown a shipment of woolen shirts from Berlin valued at 26,000 marks. The packing slips affixed to the containers indicate packaging in complete sets, which will greatly simplify distribution on the spot.

“God grant,” says my companion, “that all these gifts may still reach those for whom they are intended.” I must confess that on leaving the Winter Palace, my sympathy for Russia had once again been strongly reinforced.

Outside, thousands of lights flickered through the thick fog; a warm wind from the sea had melted the snow, and foul mud covered the streets. The bright jingling of sleigh bells had fallen silent; grave and taciturn, people went their way. Flags fluttered from every roof; Petersburg was adorning itself for the morrow, for the Tsar’s name day.

My stay in Petersburg was beginning to drag out unpleasantly. Here began my training in patience.—I could not accustom myself so quickly to this *disregard of time* à la russe.

I had delivered my letters of recommendation with more or less success; the people were all courteous and hospitable. Yet all my efforts would have been in vain had our ambassador, His Excellency Baron Lexa von Aehrenthal, not taken an interest in me.

“So you have come after all, even though I had word sent to you that your plan would hardly be feasible!” — [[p29]] “Excellency, I am firmly resolved to go over; if not otherwise, then as a medical orderly!” “Well, perhaps it can be managed by another route after all,” His Excellency remarked; “you did mention that you feel a journalistic vein within you; something might best be done with that.” “Certainly, Excellency,” I replied, happy at the glimmer of hope that seemed to shine from the ambassador’s answer; Lieutenant Colonel Prince Alexander Volkonsky had also advised me in his letters that this was the only possible way.

“I am certain, Excellency,” I continued, “that by working as a journalist I can render many a service to the Russian cause. It is even a principal motive of my journey to refute, on the basis of firsthand observation and my professional expertise, all those spiteful lies that a prejudiced press has disseminated throughout the world about the Russian army and its leaders.”
