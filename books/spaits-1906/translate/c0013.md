"The Japanese bullets—they have the smallest caliber—are almost too humane," the doctor told me, "mostly they pass clean through, the wound heals in a few weeks—but the worst is yet to come! We had a man here,

[Illustration: Station building on the Transbaikal line.] [[p49]] the bullet had entered at the back of his head and come out through the nasal bone; after a few weeks he was cured and asked to be allowed to continue serving as a medical orderly. After several months, without any previous morbid symptoms, the man faints; two days later he was dead." The doctor wanted to show me several more "fine" cases, a shattered jaw, an excised shoulder blade, splintered bones in a knee—I thanked him; I felt an urge to get out into the cold air.

[Illustration: Baikal Station, the summer resort of Irkutsk.]

This was my first foretaste of war. If there were any means, I thought, of making comprehensible in a single spot, in a single image or sound, all the misery, all the pain suffered in just one day across the hundreds upon hundreds of hospitals of Russia and Japan, how ghastly it would have to be! To those good, brave lads up there in the ward, with their honest, simple peasant faces, it would surely be a matter of utter indifference whether Russia has an ice-free port on the Pacific Ocean or not!

Back at the hotel, I heard the cheerful clinking of glasses. Officers departing for the front were celebrating a merry farewell dinner with pretty Orpheum girls.

I have my seven-day provisions for the journey to Harbin in my rucksack; now we can cross the Angara again and head further east!

## Transbaikal.

More than two weeks on the road, and the journey presses relentlessly onward. I have grown as accustomed to it as the miller to the rumble of his mill, and at night I wake up whenever we come to a halt. Then, from time to time, I also hear the impatient stamping of horses from the railcars on the siding; still transports of train troops, whom we pass. On the whole we make rather slow progress; fifteen to twenty versts an hour, but one must be content even with that.

When we boarded in Irkutsk, our carriage offered the most unbelievable spectacle of filth; fortunately, a colonel-auditor later raised quite a stir, summoned the station master, gendarmes, and whatever other officials there were, after which it was even swept out, and the supposedly broken hot-air heating recovered as well. The lighting has likewise grown worse; yet travel is still quite comfortable. Below, the wheels hum and drone, the carriage sways in an ever-monotonous rhythm; a candle illuminates the worn compartment, from whose walls the wires of a former electric system hang down mournfully. Outside, however, light glimmers and floods in magnificent effects. In bright sunshine we greeted Lake Baikal, and late into the night we were still rolling along its shores. Now there were massive slabs of ice piled atop one another, now broad expanses swept bare by the gale, in which bright moonlight was reflected in a single tone yet with a thousand different shadows—a wondrous silver carpet spread out at the feet of mighty Alps! I regretted not having been able to admire this magnificent panorama in its entirety. How wonderful it would have been to cross the lake with one of the two icebreakers, [[p52]] the *Baikal* or *Angara*, or by sledge! Until recently, the Circum-Baikal Railway was reserved solely for military trains: passenger trains stopped at Baikal Station, from where their passengers made the crossing over the lake in two and four hours, respectively. At Tankhoi, on the eastern shore, another train awaited them. The icebreakers also take on freight wagons. These are pushed into the interior of the giant vessels from high ramps. If one crosses the lake by sledge, an inn set up halfway across warms and refreshes the traveler. The temperature at Lake Baikal is usually very low and sometimes drops well below −40° R; nevertheless, the southern part of the deep lake freezes over only during prolonged, very severe cold. From the train I could clearly see a dark ribbon of open water—the wake of an icebreaker’s latest passage—even though we had twenty-five degrees of frost. For weeks now, passenger trains have been departing once daily direct from Irkutsk to Manchuria. The line of the Circum-Baikal Railway strikes me as a masterpiece of engineering. Cut entirely into the rock, it hugs the lake closely, bridges several small rivers, and passes through forty-eight tunnels, though admittedly short ones. All this was constructed in a relatively

[Illustration: Baikal icebreaker.] [[p53]] short time, for the most part during the war itself, which somewhat excuses the primitive appointment of the stations.

Late in the evening we reached Tankhoi, but for a long time I was still able to follow the ever-changing picture of the glittering ice sheet and its picturesque shores, until at last the moon disappeared behind the dark forests of the mountains. All around, everything seems dead—frozen stiff; only our train puffs and rattles through the silent solitude. From time to time a campfire flickers, in whose glow stands a soldier. His tall black papakha pulled down over his ears, rifle at the order, he lets the train pass. When his relief arrives, he crawls back into the dugout, the entrance of which peeks out behind through the snow.
