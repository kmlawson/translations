Baron Aehrenthal did not let the matter rest with the gracious promise of his support, but had the kindness to [[p30]] request his secretary of legation, Count Trauttmansdorff, to proceed that very same day to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs on my behalf.

If I now thought that I would be underway in a few days, this proved only my complete ignorance of that complicated clockwork known as Russian bureaucratism. First of all, I had to gain clarity on the meaning of the word “*zawdra*.” In German, “*zawdra*” would mean nothing more than “tomorrow.” Unsuspecting, I clung to this literal translation and for two weeks presented myself trustingly day after day at the office of the Great General Staff, where the friendly Captain Nikolski assured me most definitely each time that General Kuropatkin’s telegraphic permission would be here zawdra, that I would most certainly receive my propusk zawdra, and could depart zawdra. In the Russian reckoning of time, the difference in dates is probably the slightest distinction; one could most easily grow accustomed to that; the harder thing for us fast-living Western and Central Europeans lies in the far more protracted assessment of the value of time in general.

If the Russian says “*sejcsasz*” = “immediately,” he means at best the next day; but if he says “*zawdra*,” it is the brief designation for something dragging out quite indefinitely, at best from a week upwards.

Yet waiting here in Petersburg at the same time as myself were many others. The Hôtel de France was teeming with merchants and industrial magnates from all over the world. One had ships to sell to the Russian government, another cannons, a third tinned provisions, a fourth macaroni, and so on. They were all waiting for the “zawdra” on which their deal was to be concluded. What a life there was in that hotel, a babel of languages—a squandering of money in the hope of future gain! They all hoped—only two groups were certain: the Germans and the French. As I heard, these were said to have owed the conclusion of many an advantageous contract above all to the active support of their military attachés.

In the great industrial competition of our time, in which the marketing of war materiel plays a leading role, a well-sounding name and good outward manners are not enough for a military attaché. Even an exclusively military education, however thorough, does not suffice on its own—*beyond* this, he must have knowledge and understanding that cannot, to be sure, be acquired in the salons of the upper ten thousand.

For Hungary, which offered cartridges and tinned provisions for sale, the presence of the *Hungarian commercial attaché von Podhorszky* was of the greatest advantage, while the *Austrian firm of Skoda* was supported to the utmost by the joint ambassador.

At that time, my observations on the occasion of my daily visits to the War Ministry and the Great General Staff were by no means uninteresting. How many people of all social classes were there, inquiring about the fate of their nearest and dearest in the theatre of war! What anxious hope, grief, and pain could be read in their faces! How many officers shot to cripples did I already see in the anterooms back then; most had come to plead for an increase in their meager pension.

At last—who could describe my delight—my “zawdra” too had seen the dawn. From General Adjutant [[p32]] Kuropatkin an affirmative answer had arrived, a propusk was issued to me, my passport visaed valid for Mukden—nothing stood in the way of my journey to the Russian headquarters any longer. To have requested, waited for, and obtained all this in spite of all the unfavorable prognoses given to me could only enliven my joy, my self-confidence, and my faith in the future all the more.
