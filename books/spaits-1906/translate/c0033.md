On the front wall of the room there is invariably a domestic altar with candlesticks and cups in which thin yellow incense sticks are usually kept. On the walls I often found very beautiful ink drawings, such as the one included in this book.

The windows are large and take up almost the entire front wall. Set into the window frame is an outwardly opening wooden lattice, which often displays elaborate carvings. Over this lattice is pasted thick white parchment, into which a tiny glass peek-window is also cut. The parchment would have kept things relatively warm, but it had mostly been damaged by the soldiers, so that the cold air blew through its cracks.

Few of the original inhabitants had remained in the villages. Almost all had moved into Mukden, which around this time harboured some thirty thousand or even more such displaced villagers. For the most part, the Chinese had been expelled by the Russians from the area of the troops and headquarters—a measure that was entirely justified given these people's aptitude for espionage, but which struck the poor Manchus, whom the entire war did not concern in the least, quite bitterly.

Sometimes such a poor devil would sneak out of Mukden back to his village and was glad if he still found his four stakes on the old spot. He could rest easy if a flag was flying over his roof. If it was the Red Cross flag, then a divisional field hospital was housed here, as these were often pushed forward disproportionately far toward the firing line [[p120]]. This also frequently resulted in their suffering from enemy fire, *for which the Japanese could not be blamed*.

The Slavic tricolour signified a staging post. These were mostly occupied by doctors and officers who temporarily had no assignment, that is, whose previous official assignment had ended and who, as a result of the sluggish administration, *waited* for weeks for a new duty posting.

[Illustration: Mongolian felt yurt.]

The Mongolian felt yurt, which has also become naturalised in Manchuria, could be used to good advantage for housing smaller detachments. It keeps much warmer than a tent, even though the latter was always pitched with double walls.

The higher headquarters were marked by yellow flags with inscriptions, and by lanterns at night.

Order and cleanliness distinguished their sectors, just as strict camp discipline seemed to prevail in the other billeting areas as well.

Nowhere was there abundance, let alone luxury, yet everywhere in this zone of the field army the needs of both enlisted men and officers were satisfied.

[[p121]]

The Russian is accustomed to eating well and plentifully, and he did not need to renounce this habit here either. The cooking at the higher staffs was even excellent; on the other hand, they too lacked the comfort of proper cutlery. The Russian is impractical in this regard—portable officers' messes, such as belong to the field kit of every staff in our army and in other armies, were entirely absent there, and it was not uncommon for roast meat with gravy to be served on a tin coaster. This may well also be a consequence of Russia's underdeveloped industry, which is wholly dependent on foreign countries for the procurement of practical travel goods.

Of drinking bouts or even orgies, of which the imagination of journalists so often knew how to report, there was not a trace. For the non-Russian, however, it was unpleasant that vodka and tea were the sole substitutes for beer and wine.

I had already come to know tea during the journey out, but even more so on the theatre of war itself, as an excellent, refreshing, and health-preserving universal beverage, to which far too little attention is unfortunately paid in the other European armies—indeed, there are even regions, such as in Hungary or Austria, where this drink is completely unknown to the common soldier.—

It is no exaggeration to say that the Russian Manchurian Army owes its excellent state of health to tea.

The source of all infectious diseases usually lies in unboiled, bad water.

The very fact that the Russian, accustomed to tea, never drinks ordinary water, but even to quench his thirst drinks only boiled water—that is, tea—provides a universal preventive measure for the army's state of health.

How could the Russian soldier have survived the forty-day transport from Moscow to Mukden had his friend, the *chaynik*—tea kettle—not stood by his side? How many living bacilli would he not have swallowed on the way across all of Asia had they not been rendered harmless in the *kipyatok*—[[p122]] boiling-water cauldrons—at the stations and halts?

And now on the theatre of war itself! What was the poor devil supposed to drink here? *Vodka was strictly forbidden to the enlisted men;* it could be sold to them neither in inns nor at railway stations. *Chin*, the Chinese liquor, a hideous drink that continues to ferment in the stomach for a week after one has slept off the hangover; wine was out of the question, beer too expensive, and unboiled water, finally, the surest breeding ground for every possible disease.
