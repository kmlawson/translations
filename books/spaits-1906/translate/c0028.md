And so it was, only the “tomorrow” was a “Russian tomorrow”, a *zavtra*—consequently, the entire Japanese left wing was attacked in a unified manner not on the 25th, but only on the 26th.

On the 25th, the “rolling up” of the Japanese front was continued to the extent that the *Mishchenko Group* succeeded in taking possession of Qitaizi, Mamakai, and the ferry at Beidagu, while the *Center Group*, after strenuous fighting, seized the village of Heigoutai on the right bank of the Hun River. The enemy—who could not have been very strong—had entrenched himself along the edge of the village and, as the Russian artillery fired almost exclusively with shrapnel and not with high-explosive shells, held out until evening. With the onset of darkness, the *First Corps (Stackelberg’s Corps*, which since the beginning of the campaign had already lost nearly *one hundred percent* of its original strength!) stormed into the village, where a desperate house-to-house fight lasted until the early morning hours. The actions on these two days, conducted with only fractions of the total force available, resembled mere demonstrations to such a degree that the Japanese—perhaps expecting a [[p100]] main attack against their center—are likely to have shifted their reserves toward Sandepu only on the 26th.

What *precious time* the Russians had again *allowed to slip away* here! It truly seemed as though the Eastern Group and the entire Third and First Manchurian Armies had frozen to the ground during this time!

Always and in all battles the same Russian principle: one flank group begins; once it has accomplished its mission, the next follows, and so on—everything else looks on and waits! But if one of these groups deployed *one after another* encounters insurmountable resistance, the whole enterprise stalls—the individual corps become entangled in actions separated from one another in time and space, the ultimate result of which is always and everywhere retreat!

The bravery and above all the eagerness for battle of the Russian troops during the fighting around Sandepu recalls the days of the heroes of antiquity.

On the 26th, the Japanese were pushed back from all their positions; their efforts had not succeeded in retaking Heigoutai; on the contrary, they were driven back to the Sandepu–Malan-Sumapu line.

Nowhere did the Russians wait for their own artillery to prepare the attack; everywhere they charged—*by battalions*—against the Japanese, who, although always well covered, were numerically significantly weaker and were forced to yield the field each time after bitter close combat.

By the evening hours, the First Siberian and Eighth European Corps had left well over a thousand dead on the field.

Terrible was the plight of the wounded. The temperature had fallen far below –20° R. The few stretcher-bearers were insufficient to transport to the rear all those whose faces and hands were streaming with blood, or who were writhing in agony with a bullet in the belly. Those who were unhurt gave no thought to the dying, but rushed forward, for at last victory seemed to beckon to the Russians—the new, fresh troops from Europe wanted to [[p101]] show that the soldiers of old Russia could still fight and conquer—with their baptism of fire, the tide had to turn! Thus the Eighth Corps stormed on across the frozen fields of Heigoutai toward Sandepu—a handful of snow had to serve many a man as food and drink that day.

Whoever collapsed, struck down by an enemy bullet, simply remained lying there—until the bitter frost came over him and pressed the gaping wounds together with icy fingers; the blood ceased to trickle, the pain froze into lifelessness. Yet even those who reached the advanced dressing station were not yet safe. There were indeed enough doctors there to dress their wounds, but on the two-wheeled cart or on the Chinese *arba* that was to carry them to the main dressing station, the shivering chill came over them once more, penetrating the thick furs and the Chinese camel-hair blankets with which the poor wretches had been covered. Nothing helped here except movement, and so one saw men who could scarcely walk dragging themselves along painfully beside the ambulance wagon. Better that the wound should bleed and burn—the wounded man wished only not to freeze to death.

The Russians had been lying before Sandepu since afternoon. The village lies toward the south on a gently rising height, which the Japanese had skillfully prepared for tiered fire. The Japanese artillery stood close beside the village. The villages on both sides of Sandepu were burning; these the Russian artillery had taken under fire—Sandepu itself had not, as was said, owing to an error in designating the targets to be shelled on the map!

Here, as in other instances, the Japanese were able to assemble their reserves under cover in the villages, since these were never shelled by the Russians with high-explosive shells, but only with shrapnel; likewise the Japanese could also venture to place their artillery immediately adjacent to settlements. How devastating, by contrast, was the effect of the Japanese Shimose shell in the Russian ranks, and particularly in their batteries! Where the Japanese shell struck, a dense, broad cloud of earth, two storeys high, rose into the air and, lingering aloft for several minutes, shrouded all the horror it had wrought. It was not only the shell fragments, but the earth itself, thrown up as though by a mine, that tore limbs from torsos and severely injured the faces of even those standing farther off. To the whiz of bullets, to that howling and whistling high up in the air, and to the spattering of dirt where rifle bullets or shrapnel balls strike, one becomes accustomed fairly quickly in war, and even the sight of men wounded by these projectiles is bearable. Terrifying, on the other hand, was the effect of the shell on our nerves; each time it was as though a powder mill had blown into the air—and then the many mangled limbs lying about, and bodies crushed by the earth into a black mush!
