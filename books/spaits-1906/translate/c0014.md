The following evening we crossed the Selenga; splendid moonlight once again; the high bridge superstructure cast its long shadows across the sheet of ice, over which the wind sent fine snow trickling like slender rivulets. From there the journey proceeds very slowly; barely ten versts an hour. We reach a wide plateau, which forms a watershed between the Pacific Ocean and the Arctic Ocean. The nights are becoming bitterly cold; in Zavod it was thirty-five degrees below [[p54]] [Illustration: Circum-Baikal Railway.]

zero. From there the country becomes monotonous again: the broad valley plain of a tributary of the Shilka, heavily ravaged forest to the right and left; alongside the railway, a road that seems heavily traveled, the old Siberian trakt. The further east we go, the less snow; last night we passed Chita, and from then on there is scarcely any white to be seen on the mountain slopes.

We know virtually nothing about the war. There are no newspapers, and at the stations one learns little. Yesterday in Chita there were again a few telegrams: Vladivostok must be evacuated by the civilian population, only artisans are permitted to remain there. It was further reported that in Hungary the military is said to have fired on the workers!

How modest the people here are with regard to “news”! At the stations, soldiers sometimes come up to us and ask for finished newspapers. Recently one caught hold of a newspaper page from Tomsk—long out of date—which was then read aloud in front of the carriage. From the [[p55]] window, one saw nothing but a circle of black Cossack caps pressed tightly around the man with the newspaper.

This morning there was finally a splendid subject for a snapshot. About ten Cossacks were standing around a band of honghuzi in front of Karymskaya station. Real, genuine honghuzi, life-sized, one of them even bound! By the time I came with the camera, they had already boarded a third-class carriage along with their escorts. During the journey we paid the band a visit; to my regret it turned out that they were merely Chinese being escorted to the next guard post on account of some brawl or other. Repulsively ugly, but powerfully built figures, almost as disgusting as the numerous Buryats who are now seen in ever greater numbers at the stations.

Tomorrow morning we arrive at Manchuria station. From there we travel on the Chinese railway; hopefully it will be better than the one I am on now. The valley has meanwhile grown narrower, bare mountains on both sides with steep slopes and gullies. We are approaching the Khingan. Hopefully from tomorrow on there will also be more of military interest to see.
