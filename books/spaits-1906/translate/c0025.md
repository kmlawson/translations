### The Retreat.

The engagements of the preceding day had alerted the Japanese, and it was to be expected that they would attack the detachment from the east within a very short time. As General Mishchenko, for the reasons mentioned above, had no intention for the time being of committing to a more serious engagement, he was anxious to cross over to the right bank of the Liaohe as soon as possible—if possible, still south of the mouth of the Hunhe.

Owing to skirmishes dragging on late into the night, the detachment had only found rest in the early morning hours. Consequently, the *departure* on the 13th did not begin until around midday.

[[p89]]

The result of the prior river reconnaissance showed that the ice cover at the southernmost crossing point was already very weak, so that it seemed advisable to assign the artillery of the southern column to the center column.

The Liaohe was reached at 6 o'clock in the evening. The left bank is steep and high here; ramps had to be cut into the hard-frozen ground and the ice sheet itself artificially reinforced, labors which were significantly hampered by the falling darkness. The river crossing lasted six hours, so that the troops once again only came to rest late after midnight.

Nothing was to be seen of the enemy on this day—a further sign that at *Yingkou* one had faced only completely inferior forces.

*14 January.*

The Russians had remained in camp until 8 a.m. No reconnaissance troops had been left behind on the left bank; thus Japanese artillery was able to appear on the opposite bank of the river at the aforementioned hour and open fire on the withdrawing Russian columns. This time the Japanese shot very poorly; for the time being, their fire had no effect at all.

To cover the retreat, two Cossack regiments remained behind with the artillery, whose combined attack soon succeeded in silencing the Japanese batteries and compelling them, as well as the weak Japanese infantry, to retreat.

On the Russian side, thirty men had fallen on this day.

The further retreat of the Russians was not disturbed by the enemy. The transport of the wounded to the rear, in which the Finnish ambulances proved very practical, permitted only a limited marching speed.

With the army, there was already concern for the fate of the detachment, and General Kosakovsky was sent with two Caucasian regiments [[p90]] toward the south to effect a possible disengagement. Kosakovsky advanced to the vicinity of Dawan, where, upon receiving news that General Mishchenko was already approaching safe and sound, he turned back and rejoined the army. Up to the beginning and during the Battle of Sandepu, the Mishchenko group remained on the army's western wing.
