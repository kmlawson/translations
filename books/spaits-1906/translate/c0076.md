Szeptycki was likewise here. He had ridden over with Rennenkampf from the right to the left wing, where he witnessed the critical situation at the Dalin Pass.

[[p260]]

Surprised in front by the enemy, Alekseyev had evacuated the pass, but the retreat was already threatened as well. The troops began crowding back along the narrow road, where a few versts further to the rear trains of all kinds were jammed together. Rennenkampf had arrived just in time to avert a worse disaster. General Alekseyev was sent away, several battalion commanders were relieved of their command, and a bullet was promised to anyone who dared move from his post. The heights north of the road were occupied. The very same troops who had given way under their former leaders stood firm under Rennenkampf and held their own against the numerically superior Japanese. The losses, however, were terrible; some rifle companies had dwindled to twenty or thirty men, and almost every battery had lost several guns in the fighting.

General Rennenkampf greeted me most cordially and personally led me into the general staff section opposite, commending me there to the care of Lieutenant-Colonel Baron Korff.

At staff headquarters, whose chief, Colonel Gurko, was the son of the famous general, there was still complete confidence at the time, and high hopes were placed in General Linevich, who surely would soon attempt an offensive thrust with his strong reserves. That the decisive struggle of the battle was no longer being fought here on the left wing, but over by the Liaohe, was not learned by the Eastern Corps until the night of 4 March! Although exhausted, I was unable to fall asleep, being far too agitated by the shifting impressions of the day. Around midnight the officer on duty was awakened. A telegram was brought—he woke his neighbours to the right and left and read in a low voice:

“Japanese attacking from the west; battle underway for three days seven versts southwest of Mukden.” What, then, were the losses for, the bloody sacrifices of the rifle regiments—I heard the officers exclaim indignantly—if we are to be beaten on the right wing after all!

Beaten? There was nothing about that in the telegram! “Were we winning, they would have told us sooner, not three days later!” That is the Russian principle!

[[p261]]

*At a time when details about the fighting around Mukden were perhaps already known in Europe, we, a hundred-odd kilometres to the east of it, still knew nothing at all.*

That I met Binder-Kriegelstein here once again—and unfortunately for the last time—I have already mentioned earlier.

In the morning, before I rode on, I also made the acquaintance of the “town commandant” at Rennenkampf’s headquarters.

He was a retired Cossack chieftain—his greatest pleasure seemed to be beheading Chinese, whom he hated in their entirety. Anyone stopped in the mountains was searched to see whether he had any “flags” hidden in his garments. If such were found on him, it was taken as proof that he had been signalling to the Japanese over on the other side. He was brought to Machuntan, where the executioner was already waiting for him. Cossacks had volunteered for these executions, which our Cossack chieftain directed with visible relish—save that in handling the sharp sabre they fell far short of their Chinese colleagues. Often the Cossack had already swung for a blow three or more times, and the spy’s head was still hanging from his torso.

With a letter of recommendation to Colonel Zakrzewski—commander of the 2nd Chita Cossack Regiment—which Lieutenant-General Rennenkampf had given me, I was to ride on in the afternoon.
