The greatest importance must unquestionably be attached to any new sighting device that facilitates aiming for the marksman and, above all, prevents that so dangerous overshooting of the enemy by offering the man the ability to align the front sight quickly and reliably level.

Numerous inventors have already occupied themselves with designing such a sight; yet these projects, often quite ingenious, suffered for the most part from the defect of being too complicated, and therefore entirely unusable for military service.

The Royal Hungarian Hussar Captain Domobran Kokotović has now earned the credit of solving this problem in splendid fashion. The *universal front sight* designed by him must, on account of its *simplicity* and immense advantages, be described as a truly battle-worthy sighting device which, particularly against modern combat targets, brings about an enormous improvement in hit accuracy.

The new sight has admittedly not yet had an opportunity to prove its merits in actual combat; in numerous foreign armies, however, practical trials are currently under way, the results of which to date fully confirm the universal front sight's high utility in the field.

Money for vodka—that tempted many a man to sell his only fur coat, his boots, even his army-issue trousers. To be sure, the number of these men derelict in their duty was not large, but it was precisely these scoundrels who loitered around the stations and staging posts [[p249]] and tried to arouse the pity of travellers and strangers. Not infrequently do we find among Russian soldiers a propensity for theft. This, too, seems to be a consequence of his faulty upbringing. In a country where a Grand Duke Sergei embezzles the felt blankets donated to the wounded, and where, despite all piety, it is customary to steal the jewels from altarpieces, where officials live largely by theft—for the autocratic system permits no oversight of the public purse—it can hardly come as a surprise if the muzhik, too, steals without seeing anything particularly evil in doing so! Yet in this regard as well, much may have been painted in blacker colours than it really was. I travelled and marched with Russian soldiers, bivouacked with them—*nothing was ever stolen from me by these people.* During the retreat from Mukden, I entrusted my horse to a Cossack of Prince Dolgorukov’s sotnia so that he might take his chances and try to deliver it to Captain Count Szeptycki at General Rennenkampf’s headquarters. The man could be certain of never seeing me again in his life; in the appalling chaos of that time, any check on whether the horse had perished during the retreat or not would have been impossible—and yet the man faithfully delivered my property. I had bad experiences, on the other hand, with the Caucasians. The people from these regions are not only thieves, but—*if it can be done without any particular danger*—robbers as well. During the retreat from Mukden, Caucasian Cossacks plundered worse than the Honghuzi; it was Caucasian Cossacks under General Kozakovsky who attempted to loot the wagons of the Russo-Chinese Bank at Puhe, and it was mostly Caucasians whom General Linevich had executed by firing squad in Tieling immediately after taking supreme command of the army. Yet in resourcefulness and resilience the Caucasian is far superior to the Russian, and far cleaner in his appearance and dress as well.

When judging the clothing of the Russian soldier in the field, one must keep in mind above all a fact that will perhaps surprise the reader: the Russian [[p250]] cannot tolerate the cold or, better said, he fears the cold more than the southerner does, and strives and knows how to protect himself against it better than the latter. “They are no cold weather people,” an American quite rightly remarked of them. It seems as though we still had something left to draw upon from the rays of the sun stored within us, while the Russian has already surrendered his little bit of warmth to the perpetual frost and winter. He spends the harsh winters mostly in an overheated room whose windows are never opened. The fear of fresh air goes so far that even the dining car of the Trans-Siberian express train—which, it may be noted, served as a dormitory for the waiters—was never aired out. Washing or even bathing outdoors during the cold months—as the Japanese did—is something the Russian shrinks from, which is again a sign of a lack of willpower.
