In the village are quartered the headquarters troops belonging to the army high command—Caucasians—and the supply trains. Kuropatkin maintains strict discipline; meticulous order reigns in his headquarters as well as in those of the other staffs. Further on the road to Kouloutsi we ride through several villages. A sad sight. The roofs and walls of the low mud buildings seem to have suffered from fire. The inhabitants are gone, having moved to Mukden. Next to the villages, earthen huts, called *zemlyanki*, have been dug into the ground, offering the troops far warmer shelter than the wretched *fanzas*.

The roads are excellent, and faster than we would have given the little horses credit for, we were in Kouloutsi, where we warmly greeted Szeptycki. He complained that he had already been sitting in his cramped *fanza* for weeks and that things simply would not get moving up at the front. We told him that the day before, General Rennenkampf had passed through Mukden to take over the cavalry regiments of the wounded General Mishchenko over on the right wing. Rennenkampf, whom I had the honour of being introduced to, is a magnificent figure, a genuine cavalry general, and one of those whom one certainly cannot deny quickness of decision and energy in execution. Heedless of danger, he, like his entire staff, has already been wounded repeatedly. Until now he was on the extreme left wing in the mountains, which he was now glad, as he said, to be out of. Yet the plain has its drawbacks too. If the thaw sets in, which this year is likely to happen earlier than usual, one gets miserably stuck in the mud. If the offensive does not come soon, it will hardly be possible down there to make any headway later in the sodden ground. Rennenkampf, however, is certainly the man who knows how to overcome such difficulties.

The day before in the dining car, Rennenkampf had also mentioned Szeptycki in the most laudatory terms, adding several flattering remarks about the Austro-Hungarian cavalry. It was likely also thanks to his high esteem for our cavalry that he received me so exceedingly warmly and granted me permission to attach myself to one of his Cossack regiments.

Captain Szeptycki had a guest with him, the writer and journalist Binder von Kriegelstein, who had served as an artillery first lieutenant in the Austro-Hungarian and later also in the German army. At that time he was writing for the *Berliner Lokalanzeiger* [Berlin Local Advertiser] and for other German newspapers.

Both were delighted at the news that Rennenkampf was preparing for a raid against Liaoyang. Szeptycki, who had always felt very much at ease with the dashing cavalry general, immediately decided to join him on the extreme right wing, and Binder likewise hoped to be able to take part in the raid.

Life here in the centre must have been quite tedious during the long lull in operations, which had now already lasted for four months. An ineffective cannonade around midday, isolated night-time enterprises by a few raiding parties that were mostly unsuccessful, an occasional shifting or relieving of troops in the positions—such was roughly the character of Russian activity in the centre and on the left wing during the winter months. That very afternoon we rode away from Kouloutsi. Binder-Kriegelstein had taken a few more photographs of us. The picture shows the outer wall of the *fanza* in which Szeptycki lived. It was one of the better ones, and its roof as well as the doorposts had still been spared by the soldiers. The neighbouring huts looked desolate. Whatever was made of wood had long since gone into the cooking fires of the muzhiks, leaving only the bare grey mud walls with the large, gaping square windows, from whose corners there still hung now and then a piece of parchment paper, which in these parts serves in place of windowpanes. Those villages in which higher commands or staffs had been quartered could consider themselves fortunate. There [[p118]] the houses remained undamaged for the time being and were even put into better repair.

In every larger Manchurian settlement there are one or more spacious buildings: a temple with a garden and adjoining priest's quarters, a liquor distillery, pawnshops, or the farmstead of a wealthier Chinese. Each of these buildings is surrounded by a thick, high stone wall, usually fitted with loopholes and flanking towers for defence. The large wooden arched gate of the outer wall, always painted with the

[Illustration: Manchurian fanzas.]

obligatory good and evil god, leads into a smaller forecourt, in which stands a low domestic altar with burning incense; further back in the main courtyard stands the dwelling house—the *fanza*. From the outside it looks quite cosy. Single-storey, the roof thatched with sorghum stalks, wooden wattle walls smeared with mud. The painted door leads into an anteroom, which usually also serves as a kitchen. On either side of it lies a spacious room. The heating is distinctive. The stove is replaced by the *kang*, which extends about two metres wide and half a metre high along both side walls. It is [[p119]] made of solid clay and is usually heated from the anteroom, so that the room is free of smoke and pleasantly warmed. Overnight, however, the *kang* cools down, so that in the morning hours we usually suffered keenly from the cold. The surface of the *kang* is covered with clean, shiny straw mats. It is upon this that the family life of the Chinese takes place. Here the women crouch beside a low square wooden table and embroider or weave, here children are suckled, opium is smoked, here the whole clan sleeps all jumbled together; erotic modesty is foreign to the Chinese.
