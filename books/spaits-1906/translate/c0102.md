## The Retreat of the Third and Second Armies.

### Tieling.

As if at a single stroke, the military character of the scene now changed. While in the mountains with the First Army there had everywhere been calm and relative order, even among the retreating troops, the entire plain, as far as it stretched before me from the heights, resembled a broad highway upon which swarms of ants struggled forward side by side and over one another in long black columns. At one moment horsemen galloped past me in a wild pack; at another, artillery, ammunition wagons, and other vehicles came rattling from the south, pressing on northward, here along the road, there across country; then again I passed bivouacking troops; and so it went all the way to Tieling. By our notions, one might have thought it was quarterly moving day, for all the good Chinese stood on the streets ready to drive on, with all their belongings packed onto carts. Here, for the first time, I saw soldiers looting. They appeared to be Caucasians raiding the wine shop of a German merchant. I had no time to concern myself with this, ordered my four Cossacks—one more had joined us from Sainpan—to form up around me, and rode toward the railway station, where I hoped to find the military attachés' train. The confusion here was unprecedented. I could recognize Kuropatkin’s train by the sentries posted around it in a wide arc; asking or searching here would have been futile! The sun had barely begun to shine a little warmer, and already the entire ground was turned into deep morass. In a long file, [[p339]] artillery teams moved past me—surely they must have had to abandon their guns and ammunition wagons! Slowly we wound our way toward the station through charred campfires, filth, and Chinese carts—it was with promises of the station that I had kept reassuring my Cossacks throughout the journey, telling them that there they would find shchi, tea, and gaoliang for the horses!

Yet what boundless confusion we found here, what repulsive scenes, such as I had never before witnessed in the war, were playing out in front of the station building and inside the buffet room! The glass cabinet in which the various beverages were usually stacked had been smashed to pieces; what it had contained could be guessed from the red, bloated faces of the officers, who lay about heavily intoxicated on tables, chairs—and on the floor as well—some in deep sleep, others howling and shouting. *Yet let us not forget, before this revolting sight, that these were men who had perhaps spent eight days in the open and fighting, who had scarcely partaken of food or drink during that time, who had greedily thrown themselves upon the first refreshment to be had, and who now could not but fall victim all too quickly to alcohol!*

[Illustration: The tower east of Tieling.]

[[p340]]

Where is the station commandant? A useless question; no one knows! At last I find him. He had taken off the red armband that identified him as such—so as not to be asked so many questions!

Where are the military attachés? He does not know; perhaps the general will know! The latter advises me to ride to Gongzhuling. How far might that be from here? He does not know—I should just keep following the railway, and eventually I will get there!—Any chance of eating, drinking, or feeding the horses was out of the question.

I want to ride on along the railway, but this is impossible. Hundreds of carts block my way, begging soldiers press close around me, off the road troops are encamped or waiting. Dead horses and fallen cattle lie on the ground, abandoned rifles, cartridge belts—even instruments from regimental bands were to be found there!

To make faster headway, I branch off to the west and reach the Mandarin Road again, which I had already crossed once that morning. Here I now encountered the entire train of carts and riders, still bearing the traces of that panic which last night, at the crossing of the Puhe, had dissolved its last semblance of order.
