“Yet *Russian cavalry* is also better than what you see of it here in the theatre of war! For almost my entire service I was stationed on the German frontier with Russian army dragoons. Only at the beginning of the campaign did I take command of my present Cossack regiment, but I cannot reconcile myself to this Cossack horse, and *change* the Cossacks—that I cannot do either! You, Captain, will certainly see much here that will not please you. The Cossacks would, in and of themselves, be quite excellent material for the ranks, [[p280]] enduring, daring, and gifted with an admirable, instinctive sense of direction. They have deficient notions of right and wrong when it comes to mine and thine. Their obsolete organisation as well as insufficient military training during active service in the first line are to blame for their *lacking any military value that can be taken seriously*. The second-line officers are on leave for most of their service; in the meantime, they hold posts in the civil administration. In the districts of Amur,

[Illustration: Cossack patrol.]

Argun, and Nerchinsk, the officers for the second-line regiments came at the outbreak of the war from the Guards and the army cavalry, which, considering the quality of some of the original Cossack officers—you will get to know them!—could only be beneficial. Yet even among the newly arrived officers, the military education and cavalry proficiency are *not always without exception* such that they could help the Cossacks overcome their deficient training.

[[p281]]

The Cossacks bring their horses, clothing, and sabres themselves; only their rifles and ammunition are issued to them by the regiment. Like the men, their horses are excellent in their way; there can hardly be a tougher, less demanding animal than these little Siberians. Straw and a little green fodder scraped up from beneath the snow is their nourishment; they spend the freezing nights in the open, with nothing on their backs but the fully packed saddle, which the Cossack never removes from the beast. How often have I been vexed by this, yet our officers have no eye for such things. As a rider, the Cossack certainly shows a certain dash, but absolutely no understanding; senseless, purposeless running of horses into the ground, preferably at a fast trot, he considers a cavalry feat. Knowledge of horses, care, and love for the animal are lacking in all of them. As long as a nag can still hobble on three legs, it is considered fit for service, and since the poor four-legged beasts cannot scream, they are blithely ridden on despite galled backs. In spite of all this, the good beasts still hold out, even when they can barely drag their hind legs behind them. *They are no good for a charge*; they are too slow for that and have too little weight. Yet by virtue of their endurance, they would render excellent service in reconnaissance; but for that, their riders—officers included—are once again *too poorly trained!*”

During his description of the Cossacks, the colonel had become so animated that only now did he notice Lieutenant-Colonel Slatin, who had already been standing beside us for some time.

“While I explain his assignment to the lieutenant-colonel and discuss a few other matters with him, you, Captain, can sleep for a few hours,” said the colonel, pointing to a bed of gaoliang straw in the corner. Even during supper I could barely keep my eyes open, having been in the saddle almost uninterruptedly since early morning. The bed was soon prepared; as always, the tunic served as a pillow, the greatcoat as a blanket. Over by the table they sat bent over the map, tracing the lines of march; similar, ugly-sounding names of Manchurian streams and villages, which the adjutant read out in a monotonous voice from [[p282]] General Danilov’s orders. With it, the dim, flickering light of a clamped pine splinter, soporific even for someone less weary than I! And yet the deep, nerve-soothing sleep refused to come.

The images of the past days had affected me too powerfully and in too rapid succession; at the time I could take in only the glaring colours and deepest shadows; the eye was never granted time to examine intermediate tones and nuances more closely.

Yet the images are not blurred for all that; after some time they emerge again in their unfaded colouring from the back room of memory before the mind’s eye; most often when our body, tired and weary, has yielded full dominion over our being to the spirit, which is still busily preoccupied with the recent past. From the right ear to the left, and then back to the right, the sleeper tosses, pressing his eyes tightly shut as if to bind the dark god of sleep, yet in bright sunlight familiar figures already rise up in motley succession behind his lids. There I must look once more into the eyes of the Japanese soldier, who then thrusts the bomb into his abdomen; they carry past me the bleeding torso from which the Shimose tore away head and back; then Cossacks run forward again, faster and faster, raising their arms, tumbling and pitching headfirst into the brown furrows, one after another, ever wilder—then comes dark, quiet night—suddenly a jolt through my entire body, from crown to toe—my grey broke free of me with a sudden leap, and I am still lying in the furrow—Manjiapuzi, Ximapuzi, and on to Haoweizhen, the adjutant reads in an unvarying, monotone voice. Sleep, sleep! You have only a few hours left, then you must move on again. And again I try to sleep, for I am so tired!—Why did the colonel even want to give me a carbine? So we will be in action tomorrow.—Probably tomorrow night, he said.—I wonder if there are letters for me at the Mukden post office? Really I ought to write too, just a [[p283]] few lines home—but there will still be time for that tomorrow before riding off—now sleep, at last! Yes, at last I fell asleep, heavy and leaden, until Lieutenant-Colonel Slatin woke me at dawn.
