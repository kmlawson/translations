Even during major manoeuvres, and all the more so in war, it is difficult to gain an overview of tactical situations. The enemy's long-range fire makes it impossible for the spectator to approach the firing line, so that only in quite exceptional cases, and only in the mountains, is it possible to observe troops in combat in relative safety. If it is already difficult to *see* anything, *understanding* is all the harder! Even an educated officer requires much practice to grasp quickly and correctly what he sees in the field. Today we mostly have “empty battlefields,” in which the combatants lie well-concealed behind cover and smokeless powder hardly betrays their presence. For the most part, the location of the foremost lines can only be guessed from the position of the artillery and the advance [[p174]] of the reserves, and among these, distinguishing friendly from hostile troops at greater distances is once again difficult. It requires a thorough knowledge of army organisation to deduce the strength and branch of service of the troops at the front from the markings and types of the rearward ammunition wagons, medical facilities, and train transport wagons. How, then, is the layman, amid the overwhelming spectacle of battle and under the deafening impression of roaring guns and howling bullets, to find his bearings or even be capable of critically judging the course of the engagement? Such an evaluation will never be possible *immediately* at all, but only after mature reflection and careful comparison of the facts as they clarify over time. Yet our sensation-hungry reading public demands immediate news! *Yesterday's original news from East Asia!* The correspondent must report, come what may; and so he grasps at any sensational detail lying by the wayside, which the censor, being utterly inconsequential, allows to pass; back home in the editorial office, the brief dispatch is pieced out at front and back according to the taste and editorial slant of the newspaper in question, and appears the next morning in the paper as an “Original Telegram from Our Special Correspondent.” *Not the press*—but solely the impatient public was to blame for those newspaper lies served up to them at their own request by both sides during the war—for quite fanciful reports often came from the Japanese side as well! This was also roughly the view of the two Americans, who endeavoured to cater fully to the tastes and demands of their readers back home in the sense described above.

From the Americans we went on to the French writer Naudeau. He lived in that cosy temple quarter with a priest, the only place in Mukden where one could live cleanly and without being robbed. For only two days earlier, two horses had been led out of the stable during the night from Binder-Kriegelstein; horse theft had become a regular trade here, at the weekly Mukden [[p175]] horse market almost nothing but stolen nags were sold, and Binder even found one of his own there again.

Curiously enough, all those correspondents of whom I speak here had gathered here during the subsequent fighting around Mukden and, together with the two American military attachés, Staff Surgeon Howard and Engineer Captain Judson, had fallen into Japanese captivity.

The censorship officers were from time to time also entrusted with intelligence duties. Thus, prior to the Battle of Mukden, Staff Captain Baron Höven was sent to Xinminting to ascertain whether there was any Japanese advance along the Chinese railway. Naudeau accompanied him on this journey; he later attempted to return to Mukden, in doing so encountered General Bürger's Russian brigade, and with it was forced northward for two days. Naudeau was certainly witness to interesting fighting on this occasion. Binder-Kriegelstein had stayed in Mukden for only a few days and had gone in Szeptycki's company to General Rennenkampf. I met him at the end of February in Matsundan, on the eastern wing at the general's head-

[Illustration: Chinese troops of General Ma near Xinminting.] [[p176]] quarters, near which he had rented a fanza from an old Chinese woman. By Rennenkampf himself he was treated with every courtesy; the staff officers were less friendly toward him. One morning I informed him that a dispatch had arrived at headquarters during the night, according to which serious fighting with the Japanese had already begun southwest of Mukden. “Then it will probably be best if I ride into Mukden and secure my suitcases and Szeptycki’s, which we left there in a fanza,” he said. In Mukden he fell into Japanese captivity along with the other correspondents and was taken to Japan, where, while cleaning his revolver, he was fatally struck by a bullet.
