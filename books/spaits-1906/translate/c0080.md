Regular volleys now flashed from the mountain, to which our Cossacks replied with lively skirmish fire; each man fired on his own, for there was no fire control among them. I was lying rather far off, near the village, and could observe well. Up to this point only our third sotnia had been under fire, but now the Argun Sotnia also began to shoot, having skillfully moved forward along the hollow and caught the enemy on the Pereval in a crossfire from there. "Hurrah!" now rang out from the third sotnia—almost simultaneously the men leaped up and rushed forward—yet only a few paces! I could clearly see how, after the very first steps, two men flung their arms wide, dropped their rifles, and collapsed with heads thrust forward. The Japanese seemed to have been waiting for this moment; a terrible rapid fire rattled down from the heights. As was later determined, no more than seven men from the third sotnia were left lying, seriously and lightly wounded. The range was still too great for rapid fire, but the moral effect was unmistakable. The sotnia had advanced no more than fifty paces, while individual Cossacks, faced with the bullets striking thickly around them, had already earlier thrown themselves down into the sheltering furrows.

I had never before seen combat from so close. My heart pounded with excitement; my thoughts, my feelings, my nerves, everything in me seemed to vibrate; something inside me cheered when the fire of our men, who had now slowly gathered again in a line along the field ridge, intensified into a deafening rapid fire, and I grew anxious [[p274]] whenever it began to die down here or there. And that is how it is with the men as well—the more shooting there is to their right and left, the safer they feel!

My grey had used these gripping moments to tear himself free with a sudden jerk, and was now galloping after the other horses, which I likewise saw running to the rear in wild flight! Bullets had surely struck among the held horses; they had bolted up and were now—more sensible than the humans—seeking a safe spot farther to the rear. Our two squadrons now remained lying in the position described above for almost two hours! Indeed, in war things move far more slowly than on maneuvers! The Argun Sotnia did manage to advance across one more fold in the ground, but for the time being no one thought of advancing any further. If the cavalryman is to advance to the attack with his carbine, then give him a bayonet to go with it as well—it has little practical value in combat, to be sure, but all the greater moral value!

The ordinary Russian always regards the fire fight as merely a preparation for finally charging with the "shtuk," as he calls the bayonet.

At last—I noted the time on my watch immediately—there was a crack from the mountain above, just above the Japanese. Our infantry company! A lively hurrah from our Cossacks, who now, without waiting for another command, considered their work as infantry at an end and crawled back to their horses. The runaway bunch that my grey had joined was back again—the horses here are just as phlegmatic as the people!

Dolgorukov had assigned me a Cossack that morning as an orderly—he was now standing before me with my horse, and pointing up at the heights, he drew my attention to the fact that the Japanese were retreating.

These sons of nature have enviably good eyes! I had always fancied that I had good eyesight, but to my annoyance I had to admit that with field glasses I often could not find what the Cossacks spotted with the naked eye. But here there was no more time to take out the glasses; already we were off [[p275]] at a gallop—that is, as fast as the short-legged Cossack horses could manage—toward the Pereval.

The fire of our infantry seemed to have taken the enemy completely by surprise, for I did not hear a single bullet whistle over our heads.

How magnificent this charge would have been on swift, powerful horses, over level ground, with a few fine obstacles along the way—just as a cavalryman is accustomed to dream of battle! Here the ground was wretched, hard-frozen plowed field, where the little horses soon lost any desire to gallop. From the heights of the pass only sporadic shots rang out; our infantry on the opposite height was still firing after the retreating Japanese. The Argun Sotnia had been closer to the pass and had reached the crest on the right ahead of us; when we reached the enemy's abandoned position half a minute later, the only sign of them was a few wounded horses running down the hollow of the saddle, limping and bleeding.

But the Argun Cossacks had made a good catch! "Yapontsy, Yapontsy!" Prince Dolgorukov called to me. He had personally led the Argun men and had been one of the first on the Pereval, where his Cossacks succeeded in cutting off two Japanese who had been slow to mount. Now the two little yellow men stood among the jubilant Cossacks. They readily surrendered their weapons. When cartridges were also demanded of them, they pointed to the empty casings lying on the ground. They were said to be two officers of the 7th Japanese Dragoon Regiment. I had barely seen them when Cossacks came riding up and reported that very close on our right flank, two Japanese infantry companies were advancing.
