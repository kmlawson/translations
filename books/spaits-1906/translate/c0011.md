## Irkutsk.

A cold, dark night. Outside the railcar, a balalaika and dancing soldiers. Is it joy that they are going off to war, or are they dancing to keep from freezing? We are in Irkutsk. Things will hardly proceed as smoothly and comfortably as before. Above all, how are we to get into the city, across to the other bank of the Angara? In summer, a pontoon bridge leads across the torrential river; it was taken down with the onset of the drifting ice. If the Angara freezes over in severe cold, the matter is once again quite simple. It is less favorable, however, when ice is drifting. Even before reaching Irkutsk, we were told that six people had drowned the day before while crossing. Most reassuring! For the time being, we sit in the small, smoky buffet room waiting for morning, which in these parts dawns very late in winter. From time to time, someone brings news. One moment it is said that the river is frozen over; the cold is at thirty degrees again, after all. Then boatmen appear, offering to take us across immediately for a large sum of money. Another advises waiting for morning to take the steamboat, which might still be running. And so, yawning and shivering, one empties glass after glass of chai, until another batch of passengers arrives from Lake Baikal. Now, however, the small room is packed to suffocating. A motley crowd: officers, Chinese, children, Sisters of the Cross, figures bundled up in tall *tachas*—the shapeless Siberian fur coat—and among them a most peculiar trio; at first glance, it brings to mind Nestroy’s merry brothers in *Lumpazivagabundus* [*The Rogues' Trio*]. In threadbare black overcoats, light little hats on their heads, and without galoshes, they are easily recognized as foreigners. Perhaps German journeymen wanting to head west again. I sit [[p43]] down near them to hear what they are saying. They are railing against the stationmaster, and they may not be so wrong about that. We were twenty hours late getting to Irkutsk. As my ticket from Petersburg to Manchuria Station is only valid for twenty days, I asked the *nachalnik*—as these gentlemen are called in Russian—to certify the delay. He calmly refuses; in wartime, he says, he is not allowed to do such a thing! What that has to do with the war is not clear to me, but one must comply.—The man was simply too lazy to dip his pen into the inkwell!

[Illustration: Irkutsk.]

Morning finally comes. We drive down to the Angara. To my astonishment, the coachman speaks German—I still know this jargon from Galicia.—“How did you come to be here?”—“I was sent away, that is to say exiled, eleven years ago, sir.”—“Did you murder someone?”—“No, sir, only for counterfeit money, sir.”

Down at the icy bank, a struggle with various people who want to haul our trunks into their boats. They lie, saying that the launch will not be running, yet it is already whistling for departure. A wretched, small, very high-built screw steamer. A narrow, icy plank leads steeply over the deep water [[p44]] up to the deck, and people push and shove across it. Things are beginning to get Asiatic, I think to myself. From above, no river is to be seen, nothing but dense fog. With one foot on the railing and the other on the engine, our captain stands, a splendid, daring fellow. With his left hand he points the way to the helmsman; with his right he pulls the steam whistle from time to time. Sometimes he has the engine stopped or even put into reverse when ice floes that are too massive block our way. Thus we wind our way successfully through fog and ice to the other bank. A jolt that sends the trunks rolling across the deck, and we have landed. Only now do I see that the sides of our little vessel are made of nothing but thin wood! “That such a thing is even permitted!” I say, forgetting once again that I am already in Asia.

As I walked through the wide streets with their clean, mostly single-story, though also two- and three-story houses, I had the distinct feeling of being in one of our provincial towns between the Tisza and the Danube. Only the tall, magnificent domed structures of the numerous churches lend the Russian character. The older houses are all of wood, including the elegant “Hotel Metropole.” These buildings are the only typical ones. Gateways, window cornices, and the seldom-missing, usually tasteful veranda all display a mixture of Russian-Chinese ornamentation. A stone triumphal arch in a similar style commemorates the pioneer of the Amur region, Count Muravyov. Besides many inns, there are three large hotels, all of which are packed full. Mostly officers, both healthy and sick; nor is the eternal feminine lacking, making its presence felt in the local *cafés-chantants*. Prices for lodgings and provisions are enormous.

Already along the line over the past few days, the traffic of troop and hospital transports had grown brisker. We had encountered the first major transport of wounded at Achinsk, and it was a very large one indeed; the men were well accommodated, and their care seemed to be irreproachable.
