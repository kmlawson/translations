When one considers how difficult it is with us, even during peacetime exercises, to keep the men from drinking tainted water, the advantage for the state of health of an army whose men are accustomed from home never to drink water, but always tea, becomes clearly apparent.

A fifteen-minute rest was enough for an individual horseman to untie his chaynik from the stirrup and quickly brew himself a warming drink. Most of the men carried compressed tea—they called it Cossack tea—with them; they almost never took sugar with it—the addition of rum is unknown to the Russian tea drinker. “When I drink tea, I drink tea—when I drink spirits, I drink spirits!” the Russian is accustomed to say when expected to spoil the aroma of his favorite drink with liquor—that comes separately later!

Thus, in the *fangzi* of the general staff sections, too, the samovar bubbled all day long—alongside it, there was chatting, smoking of papirosy, and working. Nowhere in the offices were the time and place of duty and relaxation sharply separated from one another—as is indeed hardly easily possible in war anyway. The daily routine lacked that strict formality to which we are accustomed, but on the other hand there was also no trace of that nervousness which has become ingrained among us and which, in a serious emergency, will scarcely be sustainable over the long run.

Another habit peculiar to the Russians, and one they did not want to abandon in war, is staying up late into the night—even when there is no reason whatsoever to do so—and the consequent *sleeping in late until the early hours before noon*—even when it would have been necessary to rise earlier.

Even the men in the zemlyankas rarely crawled out of them before 8 o’clock in the morning. Mind you, though, all that is said here applies only to the period when operations had come to a standstill!

The zemlyankas were spacious, fitted out for fifty to a hundred men, and had earthen stoves. They were dug out by unit, mostly near the villages, in regular lanes—work that had to be finished back in autumn, since later the ground froze as hard as bone.

In the morning, or rather before noon, the battalions assembled in front of their quarters, greeting their commander with a ringing “Pozdravie zhelayem!” Then they drilled. Mostly in close order, less often extended-order combat,

[[p124]]

and the latter mostly in the manner of “assembling individually to the front”—as we were frequently able to observe during such exercises.

Once work is finished, the troops march in with fresh, cheerful singing. Nowhere have I heard soldier songs as melodious as among the Russians, whose metallic voices are as pure as the hearts of these good fellows.

When night falls, the soldiers step outside their zemlyankas, take the papakha from their heads, and sing their evening prayer. Inside the earthen hut, the large kettle with boiling water bubbles, for which they have been given double rations of tea and sugar. Then they can sleep, or perhaps ponder—how long the war will still last. Ponder, though, thank goodness they do not yet do! The zemlyanka is warm, the food good—far better than at home—and they must die sooner or later anyway, if God wills it so.

Comfortable as it was here in the rear, it was by no means so forward on the actual battle line of the positions. These foremost lines, sometimes no more than a few hundred paces from the Japanese outposts, consisted partly of section trenches with covered shelters for entire battalions, partly of entrenchments—here termed redoubts. Four to five hundred paces behind the infantry cover stood the batteries, mostly positioned behind undulations of the terrain for indirect firing.

From the left wing of the Second to beyond the center of the Third Army, drawn back quite far from the foremost line, ran a *mighty artillery line consisting of approximately three hundred heavy fortress guns*, which had been brought here and installed with great effort. They formed a formidable rampart against any potential Japanese breakthrough attempt.

In these infantry and artillery positions, duty was extremely grueling. The biting cold and the never-resting enemy made life quite miserable for the garrison troops. The skirmishing—the Russians called it “perestrelka” (firing back and forth)—went on here all day long.

Relief troops attempting by day to move into [[p125]] the position were immediately brought under fire by the Japanese; even individual horsemen coming from the nearest village were shot at; the Russians, for their part, favored taking Japanese entrenching parties as their targets.

In the zemlyankas, tales were told of daring feats by this or that Russian soldier; one was said to have succeeded in bringing home a plump little pig that was roaming between the opposing battle lines; on another occasion, three men were said to have wagered that they could capture a herd of horses which a Japanese patrol had led to the Shahe to water, and so on.
