Now we took our stand before the church door—we had not yet forgotten this custom from Europe—to gaze at the pious beauties, who, as good Christians, allowed us to look into their narrow, slanted eyes far more unselfconsciously than did their heathen sisters. The Chinese woman feels a certain shyness before the European; she avoids him if possible, yet she never veils her face, nor is she so uncivil as to turn her back to us and face the wall upon meeting, as is the custom among Mohammedan women.

The colourful floral ornaments and decorative hairpins that Buddhist women as well as the female followers of Confucius wear in their hair, and the garish white powder and red rouge with which they paint their lips and cheeks, are absent in the Christian Chinese woman, who has also renounced colourful silks even for her Sunday dress. Yet for all these modesties she has been richly rewarded ever since birth. While girls loyal to the old faith and old custom mince along on artificially bound feet scarcely a span in length, keeping their balance with a stick, the Christian woman steps out freely; her foot has been spared the dreadful custom of crippling.

Girls and women, men and boys passed by us, laughing and joking. “The mission counts over four hundred converts,” the priests told us, “but it will likely be a long time before we can rebuild the church, for the jealousy of the Russian priests alone would prevent us. And if the Russians should have to leave here one day, we will be all the more at the mercy of the mandarins' arbitrary rule. Thus we can only trust in the help that comes to us from above.”

In Mukden there was much to see. It was a pity that the way into the city was so long and inconvenient. One usually took a rickshaw, a two-wheeled vehicle drawn by a Chinese, long rows of which stood before the railway station. They were able to weave their way through the throng of pedestrians, [[p184]] carts, and riders far more skilfully than was possible on horseback. The road to the western gate leads past Mongolian temples and ceremonial archways; all are shaded by large trees with wide-spreading branches that have not lost their green even during the winter. There, behind the grey walls of the temple courtyards, the lamas lead their contemplative existence. On the street is the most colourful life. Chinese two-wheeled carts, with four to five horses and donkeys harnessed partly abreast, partly in tandem, columns of *dvukolkas*, the Russian light army cart, laden with provisions of all kinds, between them

an elegant troika now and then, officers and Chinese on horseback. My companion this time is an officer of the Russian censorship, who wants to show me the eve of tomorrow's Chinese New Year—and something of Mukden's nightlife. The wretched jog-trot of our two rickshaws finally brings us to the baked-clay city gate, before which stands a Chinese guard. To him the foreigner must present his *propusk*. All at once, a purely Chinese street reveals itself. From the curving roofs hang golden dragons, colourful birds, and various kinds of wooden monsters. In front of each house stands a tall post with carved cross-arms protruding from it, bearing gold or red [[p185]] characters—Chinese signboards. In front of the houses or in their courtyards stand small altars with incense; the shops were closed in honour of the New Year festival. The third New Year that I have witnessed in a short span of time! Everyone wears holiday attire, the dignitaries dressed in silk; black and blue seem to be the favourite colours. Right in the middle of a crossroads stands a tall wooden pagoda; one has to drive completely around it, for ropes are stretched across its gateways; they are probably too sacred for ordinary mortals to be permitted to pass beneath them. Constantly something new, constantly something strange! All the angular figures that we have seen at home on teapots, fans, and Chinese folding screens have come to life here; we know these long queues, gold-embroidered caps, silk robes, painted women with tiny feet, all these images of dragons and birds from pictures and books, and yet here everything is new to us—unfamiliar. The Chinese seem so good-natured and gentle, curious as children and always helpful towards the foreigner. How can there be so much cruelty in these good people? Whoever wishes can convince himself of it; almost daily one can attend the revolting spectacle of a penal court session with mild torture.

The rickshaw finally came to a halt in front of a mud shack above whose unpromising entrance was written “Nowa Gostinica”. Along with “Dalni Wostok”—a dive of similar quality—it was the most reputable inn. The door leads into the dining hall, which, never ventilated according to Russian custom, reveals by its very atmosphere the culinary pleasures offered for sale here. But the company that gathers here during the day is likewise hardly inviting to linger in; so we prefer to stroll around the streets until evening. First, we pay a visit to the Russo-Chinese Bank. It is housed in a Chinese building constructed of solid stone. At the counters stand Russian officers and officials depositing the savings they have made during the war. Mr Frisk, a German, receives us, as indeed most of the officials of this large institution are Germans. We met again several times later on.
