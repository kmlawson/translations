### The Advance.

*9 January.*

Formation of columns according to the sketch.

*The left column*, consisting of the Tomsk Cossack Division, the Caucasian Cossack Brigade, and a horse artillery battery, was the strongest. To it fell the task of reconnoitring towards the [[p80]] Yingkou–Liaoyang railway line and destroying it if possible. That this railway destruction could not have been meant very seriously is evident from the fact that the Cossack regiments are neither trained nor equipped for such work,¹) while the demolition detachment was attached to the centre column.

Nothing was to be seen of the Japanese on this day. All the more frequently, however, the vanguards encountered detachments of Chunchuses, who opened fire on the approaching column from the villages.

Each time, the Cossacks attempted to attack the enemy entrenched at the edge of the village with the *lava*—that is, a dispersed tactical formation on horseback. Here, *the powerlessness of the mounted soldier against the prepared rifleman showed itself every time*—all the more so here, as the small horses were not fast enough to cover the exposed ground at a rapid pace. Each time the *lava* attack was repulsed, whereupon the Cossacks dismounted and drove off the bandit gangs with a few shots.

In order not to become entangled in time-consuming village fighting, the settlements were always bypassed.

Bivouac near Dawan.

*10 January.*

The columns set out at 7 o'clock in the morning. This late departure time is somewhat explainable by the late daybreak, the extreme cold (20–25° R.), and unfamiliarity with the terrain to be traversed cross-country.

Around 11 o'clock in the morning, the centre column ran into a Japanese half-company that had entrenched itself in a rifle trench west of Qilijie.

During this opening engagement, the two flank columns had not intervened, although they would have been in a position to cut off the Japanese half-company by a simple wheeling movement.

*Cooperation* between individual groups in combat, particularly the kind arising from the *independent initiative* of one commander or another, is something we almost never find among the Russians.

On the one hand, they considered each other so strong that they believed they could dispense with assistance—the *perpetual reliance on numerical strength!*—on the other hand, they find nothing improper in the *worthless squandering of time*, even when it is as precious as during the execution of a raid. From this arises the lack of energy on the part of the flank column commanders, which failed to awaken even when they saw the centre column, *more than an entire division strong*, held up by *a single Japanese half-company*!

One must understand the nature of the Russians, their soul and train of thought, to be able to explain how it came to pass that these so *splendid* men could achieve so incredibly few successes. A half-company halts the march of a cavalry corps!

The fight against the half-battalion on the left bank of the Hunhe again lasted until evening, at which time the Japanese withdrew towards the railway.

The columns went into camp at 8 o'clock in the evening.

During the engagement, reconnaissance towards Haicheng had been continued, and the patrols reported that the railway bridge there was occupied by infantry and artillery.

11 January.

During the advance, a report reached General Mishchenko that the Japanese were fortifying Niuzhuang.

The Japanese offered their first resistance west of the Taizihe. The reports as well as the private accounts of [[p83]] the engagement that developed here are contradictory. According to one account, Niuzhuang was said to have been occupied only by a Japanese telegraph detachment; according to another, the struggle for the village is said to have lasted until towards evening, with the fire of the batteries attached to the centre and left columns reportedly concentrated on the place.
