### The Evacuation of Mukden.

I have already mentioned that the evacuation of Mukden had begun in complete secrecy as early as 2 March. Yet that from the very beginning of the battle there was a lack of any energetic resolve to hold the city under all circumstances was amply demonstrated by the hasty removal of the three hundred fortress guns, which were evacuated to Harbin for safety at the first Japanese attacks. Concerning the destruction of the ammunition belonging to these guns, which could not be loaded so quickly and safely, Major Schönmayer recounted that a huge pit was dug near Mukden station and filled with fuel; into this were placed the dismantled projectiles. This terrible concert was soon followed by a mournful yet dreadfully beautiful illumination: the seemingly endless timber yards stacked at Gajiazi for the operation of the Russian railways were set on fire with naphtha from 3 to 5 March—a burning torch, mightier perhaps than any that had ever blazed on earth, visible across the entire battlefield by night. Far to the east, the soldiers watched in astonishment as dense showers of sparks sprayed up into the sky through the black night; the billowy black smoke was carried by the west wind as far as the mountains to the First Army. But the Japanese, too, saw the pillar of fire—to them it was a bonfire that their enemy had kindled as a sign of his weakness.

Even though the evacuation of Mukden thus seemed a settled matter already at the start of the battle, the order for it was issued only at the eleventh hour, when the enemy broke through the army's centre on the evening of 9 March.

The military attachés had already left the train in the first days of March, having been quartered first in a house near the railway station and later in a *fangzi* in the interior of the city of Mukden. In the courtyard of this same *fangzi*, part of the baggage train belonging to army headquarters was also camped. Not far from here was the Russo-Chinese Bank, which during the battle had received no fewer than thirteen orders—at one moment to pack up and depart, at the next to unpack and remain.

At 10 o'clock in the evening, the order to march reached the attachés.

Of the four exits from Mukden—whose city wall has a high tower facing each cardinal direction, and whose angled gateways and small intermediate gates formed an obstacle to movement even under normal circumstances—all, with the exception of the southern one, were soon jammed with wagons, troops, slaughter cattle, and whatever else sought to force its way hastily out into the open. [[p344]] When the military attachés reached the city gate, men were just then labouring to batter a breach in the mud wall, through which the crowd of shoving and shouting people then poured into the narrow alleys of the suburb, finally reaching the Mandarin Road from there towards midnight.

The censorship bureau left for Tieling on the last passenger train to depart Mukden on the evening of the 9th.
