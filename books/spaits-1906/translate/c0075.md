## Fighting with the Eastern Corps.

### To the Headquarters of General Rennenkampf.

The direct road from Bajiazi to Machuntan—the headquarters of General Rennenkampf—was already threatened by the Japanese on the evening of the 3rd, and since the higher commands changed their locations almost daily during this period, it was in any case advisable to ride first to the First Army Command at Shihouicheng to obtain more precise information about the Eastern Corps there.

Thousands of campfires burned in and around the village, which was covered by a reddish, shimmering cloud of smoke billowing high into the firmament—a dependable guide for a rider coming out of the mountains in the dark of night. No camp guard stopped me as I rode into Shihouicheng. The First Army Command was already sitting on packed trunks. The mood was depressed. Once again they were to retreat; part of the train had already been sent to Fushun on the Hun River.

I was still able to meet Rennenkampf in Machuntan the following day. Early in the morning I rode on. The path led to the southeast, deeper and deeper into the mountains, along the positions that were still stubbornly held by General Bachinsky and Prince Tumanov's foot Cossacks.

I met General Bachinsky in Xiluolingzi, a small place wedged between the steep walls of a side valley of the Hun River. Under his command were: two battalions of the 36th East Siberian Rifle Regiment, three battalions of the Kulikovsky Infantry Regiment (a battalion of which we saw over in Bajiazi!), and the Ninth Mountain Battery.

[[p258]]

As we were standing in front of the general's fanza, the battery rattled past—in the direction of Shihouicheng! Why was the battery being pulled out of the battle now, leaving their own infantry defenseless against the fire of the Japanese batteries?

Our battery had received orders from army command to withdraw to Bajiazi—to replace it, two field batteries were supposed to come to us from Shihouicheng! And so three batteries were marching back and forth behind the positions—thanks to the headlessness of a general staff section, while the troops in the positions were bleeding to death.

General Bachinsky's outermost outposts were on the Xidalin Pass that day; southwest of them, on the Gulingzi Pass, were those of Prince Tumanov—several companies of foot Cossacks.

Bending back in a hook to the north from the Bachinsky group as far as Beipingtai stood Colonel Aleyev with roughly a regiment.

General Bachinsky himself did not know exactly on March 3 whether he still belonged to General Rennenkampf's group or had already been subordinated to the Third Corps, with which a part of the troops organizationally under his command was already located.

Thus here too we see once again the battalion-by-battalion dissipation across a long, thin line that showed neither the character of a planned offensive nor the will for a stubborn defense—of group or general reserves nothing was to be seen here, and to the rear in Shihouicheng I had nowhere encountered stronger forces that could have been used as an "army reserve" to support these exposed forward groups.

My confidence in old Linevich's "demonstration of strength" began to fade more and more, the more opportunity I had to gain insight into the operational orders of his general staff section.

The further way to Rennenkampf led over hill and dale, cross-country; from hour to hour, "flying posts"—*letuchie*—were stationed. They each consisted of a Cossack officer and ten to fifteen horsemen; their task was to secure the [[p259]] communication among the commands. Everywhere I was warmly received, for each of the officers—there were also some among them who had previously served in the Guards—was glad to hear something new. Nor could I tell them very much, but for them, who had not left the mountains for more than five months and hardly received their letters from Mukden once a month, even what I had heard and seen in Petersburg before my departure was news. That barely twenty kilometers away as the crow flies bitter fighting was raging at the Gaotuling Pass—that they did not know!

Each of them was kind enough to escort me to the next post. But each of them also wanted to show off his prowess as a horseman on this occasion—by setting off at the most unreasonable hard trot and not pulling up until the next station.

With the first I went along; the subsequent ones I calmly let ride ahead and then explained to them that riding at a hard trot on the road was a cavalry vice that even our battalion adjutants were slowly beginning to abandon.

It must have been six o'clock in the evening when we reached Machuntan. The village lies in a narrow gorge; it seemed to me much more solidly built than the Manchurian villages I had seen so far. The walls were made of that dark grey stone which here, the further east one goes, replaces the brown loam. The houses enclosed large courtyards in which troops were bivouacking. On the roads was once again the familiar sight of ammunition and wounded transports. An autocannon was just being brought down from the mountain; it had shot off all its rounds—there was no more ammunition for it. Rennenkampf's headquarters was easy to recognize. A large temple compound with front and side courtyards—in the second courtyard a large fanza on a stone terrace. On the right lived the general with his chief of staff; on the left was the general staff section.
