On Wednesday evening we left the beautiful Ural landscapes behind us, and since then we have been travelling through the endless snowfields of Siberia. Snow, snow, and yet more snow, as far as the eye can see, up to the distant horizon where the glittering plain disappears into vague mists. Along the line, snow fences against drifts, now and then a stunted birch copse, a telegraph line leading inland—that is all that interrupts this lifeless monotony. From hour to hour a small station building, buried up to the windows in snow, solitary in the endless expanse.

[Illustration: Buryats.]

The settlements seem for the most part to lie far off the line. I would have imagined the traffic on the line itself to be livelier. Except for an ambulance train which we overtook beyond Ufa, and a transport of recruits coming towards us from Irkutsk, not much of a military nature has been seen so far. Large troop transports are said to have passed through here several weeks ago; [[p39]] people speak of more than a thousand wagons heading east in a single rotation.

A merry life prevailed at the station in Chelyabinsk. Siberian officers going on two months' convalescent leave, before returning to the front. They were quite weather-beaten, and their tall fur caps gave them a martial appearance. I thoroughly enjoyed watching them drink champagne, vodka, and tea wildly mixed together.

[Illustration: Siberian reservists.]

On Thursday we passed Kurgan; it recalls the history of the Decembrists and of Kotzebue, who likewise lived here.

Towards evening we reached Petropavlovsk, which lies right along the railway; there is not much good to say about it. Outside the station, many Tatars—we are already deep in Asia. A huge array of rolling stock had assembled on the sidings, perhaps seventy to a hundred axles in all.

[[p40]]

A short stroll in the open air does one a world of good, especially as it is quite pleasant outside. Since leaving the Urals, there has been no more than five to six degrees of frost; yesterday the express train from Irkutsk even brought word that they had two degrees of warmth there. People console me with the assurance that I shall have ample opportunity yet to get to know the Siberian winter.

This evening we reach the Ob, and then we will travel merrily towards the Russian New Year. How we shall establish the midnight hour, I do not quite know, but this will not dampen our high spirits. Just now there was a knock at my door. The colonel and my neighbour, the engineer, came to ask whether I would care to join in the collective New Year's celebration with the obligatory champagne punch, in which most of the passengers will join together tonight. How good it feels not to have to wander through the world all alone, but to find dear people everywhere, as I have been fortunate enough to do so far. Happy New Year, and joyful hope for the future, however enveloped in a blue haze it may lie before me!

The New Year's celebration was quite cosy. The plump blonde woman from second class, with the Red Cross on her arm, was the only lady. Most of the passengers had alighted towards morning at Taiga. I was rather disappointed by the great primeval forests I had hoped to see. At Mariinsk I awoke to find once again the scene of previous days: a plain with devastated, deforested woodland along the railway line.

Their esteemed colleague at Klyukvennaya does little credit to Russian postmasters. He was unmistakably still fatigued by the mental exertions of New Year's Day—to put it in plain German, too lazy to register my registered letter. First he claimed he could not find the register, then that he could not read my handwriting, then he insisted that he did not know and had no need to know where Budapest, together with Hungary, was located—and finally he claimed that my letter was insufficiently stamped. Outside, the second bell sounded.

I had bought a large sheet of stamps for several roubles, which I held out to him. He tore off the larger [[p41]] half—saying he would stick them on himself so that I would not be delayed—and I was glad that the ink-slinger at least made out the receipt for me.

Lodging a complaint about such things would only be an unnecessary waste of time—far worse things happen here that go unpunished. Where time loses its value, there can be neither punctuality nor order; in the chaos of slackness, the legal entitlement of the individual is lost.
