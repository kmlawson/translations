### The Retreat Along the Railway Line.

Of my two horses, one had become completely unfit for service, and for my good grey, too, the exertions of recent weeks had been too much; he was beginning to go noticeably lame in the right hind leg. The prospect of having to continue on foot with the entire baggage train along the Mandarin Road was hardly alluring, so with my Cossacks I struck out across the plain once more to reach the railway.

[[p349]]

Here too, now scattered, now densely packed and jumbled together, the masses pushed forward on both sides of the tracks. Whenever a train coming from the south pulled into a station or halt, enlisted men and officers rushed at it in wild haste to secure a place on the running board, up on the roof, or even on the wagon buffers; this was not out of fear of the enemy, who was following only slowly, but because it was more comfortable than marching. At Kaiyuan, the crammed train was just pulling in when I reached the station. It

[Illustration:]

was already late in the evening, and once again the roofs, the footboards, the open flatcars—in short, anywhere an individual could cling to—were covered with soldiers. How would these people spend the cold night? And how much worse for those who, straddling the iron buffers, between the cars or even out on the catwalk around the locomotive, had chosen their perilous positions?

At the station in Kaiyuan there was even greater disorder than in Tieling. Naturally, I no longer asked anyone anything. Here my Cossacks were finally able to dispatch their telegrams, which the colonel and Dolgorukov [[p350]] had entrusted to them; I dismissed them here and commended my lame grey to their care. The good fellows faithfully brought the fine beast back to Szeptycki.

But what now? One train after another pulls in—yet there is no hope of finding even the humblest little spot. Thus it grows darker and darker; all around me are drunks, sleeping men—and dead men too! Yes, dead men as well; they lie beside the wooden fence, laid out side by side like those covered with straw mats. Last night a train, such as those arriving and departing here, had been packed full of people from running board to roof. Whether from sudden braking or rapid starting, no one knew any longer; in short, several hundred people had fallen from the train, the wheels had passed over them, and twenty of them had died—they were lying here!

Fortunately, I became acquainted with an amiable border guard officer—van der Beiningen. We finally managed to tear the seal off a freight car—inside were flour sacks from the Tieling station. Quickly we crawled in and shut the door. The flour in the sacks was frozen, and that night it was still –16° R. Beiningen and I wrapped ourselves together in our blankets, huddled back to back; we thought it would be warmer that way—yet poor Beiningen shuddered so violently with the cold that I too was unable to sleep.

Four long days and nights we travelled in this way, often waiting ten to twelve hours at the halts.

Ever-changing though the scene was, it was always mournful! Troops upon troops filed past us; a transport of Honghuzi and captured Japanese were also marched past a halt. There was almost nothing to eat; Beiningen and I helped each other out with canned food.

On the third day we were finally able to purchase a bottle of rum for seven rubles and a loaf of bread for two.

Finally, at noon on the fourth day, our train stopped nine versts before Gongzhuling. And yet we had covered no more than 120 versts in four days!

[[p351]]

Behind us came Kuropatkin's train. What nameless anguish must have thrilled through the commander's soul when at night he looked out of his carriage window across the fields and everywhere, unceasingly, saw the campfires flickering, around which the beaten, scattered, and exhausted soldiers were slowly gathering once more. All the misery that reached him through the silent night from the hospital trains, from the ambulance columns, from those dark masses of the unfortunate army pressing northward—was he *alone* to blame for it?
