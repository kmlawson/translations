### The Retreat on the Mandarin Road.

In a broad line, three or even more wagons abreast, the baggage train pushed forward toward the north in the deeply rutted tracks of the Mandarin Road. Mounted men and infantry marched beside and between the wagons, among which were also the thirteen dvukolkas in which the Russo-Chinese Bank was packed. It would have been difficult to say where the head of this column began, for isolated stragglers—"individual travellers," as they were called—had already been moving north for several days, and many of Mukden's inhabitants had likewise already sent their families and belongings ahead toward Tieling during the day, so that the entire road was occupied by an unbroken, densely packed column in which every blockage made itself felt all the way to the rear.

At the time the wagons of the Russo-Chinese Bank approached the Pu River, an accident had occurred to the wagons in front while crossing the steep-banked river—there was no bridge—which brought the advancing column to a halt. Thus wagons and men now stood in the dark, cold night and waited, freezing and exhausted from fatigue, for the morning that would perhaps bring order to the chaos of men, animals, and wagons that were hopelessly tangled and jammed together here.

Yet suddenly, it might have been around three o'clock in the morning, rifles begin to rattle several thousand paces to the west from the fields, and already in the darkness one can also hear the clatter of heavy wagons racing in a pounding gallop across the ploughed fields toward their own column. Ever [[p345]] closer comes the rumbling din, already the snorting of the horses can be heard, when the men rip their rifles from their backs and fire—firing forward and firing to the right and to the left, for already the wild stampede is crashing over their own horses and wagons. It was a terrible impact when the heavy six-horse ammunition wagons slammed with full force into the tangle standing there! Yet their drivers had not seen the column on the Mandarin Road at all in the deep darkness of the night and lashed their exhausted horses all the more desperately, thus crushing men, wagons, horse teams—in short, everything that stood in their way. And those being run over here did not know whether Russians or Japanese were racing over their bodies! As long as there were cartridges in their rifle magazines, they fired away, even after the fleeing column had long since disappeared into the darkness of the night.

Mr Frisk, the official of the Russo-Chinese Bank in Mukden mentioned earlier, had been present during this scene. At the first onset his horse had reared and tumbled over, and when he picked himself up again, it had disappeared in the darkness. Frisk now leapt quickly onto a wagon so as not to be knocked down by the approaching unknown danger. But immediately thereafter the wild shooting back and forth began, so that he crawled under the wagon as fast as he could to be safe from the bullets. When all finally grew quiet, he sat down on the wagon again to await the Japanese here, who in the people's opinion were bound to arrive soon. The dawning day spread its grey glimmer over a horrifying scene. Dying horses, their bodies pierced by the wagon poles of the ammunition carts, dragged themselves miserably onward, their entrails trailing on the ground; here lay a cart, wheels in the air, there a wretched nag with crushed legs; over there the horse teams of several wagons that had collided in the nocturnal panic were jammed inextricably together, the drivers had run off, and the animals yanked and pulled at the traces without being able to get free of one another. Of the men, only a few remained. Some were lying on the ground; were they asleep or [[p346]] were they dead? nobody cared; most had abandoned their teams, and only a few loyal souls still kept watch beside them.

As it grew lighter, Frisk noticed a compact body of men cautiously approaching the bank's wagons; he took them for Japanese, but the guard that had held out beside the bank's wagons soon recognized that they were Caucasian Cossacks from General Kosakovsky's brigade. "Where are the wagons with the silver?" asked the leader of the Caucasians. "Why?" "You'll see in a moment," came the reply, and the mutinous horde was already beginning to rummage through the nearest wagon. At that, the guard opened fire. The bullets probably went only into the air, but the Cossacks thereupon preferred to loot the Red Cross wagons, where they at least encountered no resistance.

Frisk was soon relieved of his unpleasant company; one of his colleagues came riding along, and Frisk swung up behind him onto the little nag, which carried both gentlemen to Tieling—for bank officials, this night had certainly been eventful enough!
