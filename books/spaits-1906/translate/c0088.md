The horses were quickly turned, and we continued riding at a trot around the village. It went on like this for a while, when suddenly it began to sing and whistle overhead. The men already knew this music; “They’re firing!” one heard them shout, even before the crackle of Japanese infantry fire could be heard from the opposite mountainside. The enemy was shooting too high, the bullets striking ahead and to the flank of the sotnias. Without waiting for a command, following their own instinct for self-preservation, the riders turned once more to the right, toward a mountain spur that finally shielded the Cossacks from the bullets striking closer and closer. Down the valley from here, another Cossack detachment could be seen trotting back. It seemed to have been fighting, [[p299]] for its vanguard was just running back to the horse lines. The enemy had to be strong there, for across the broad valley plain, little clouds of sand swirled up close beside one another from the falling rounds. The soldier who in attack rushes forward willingly and undaunted across this hot ground hardly stands firm under fire in retreat, especially when he has *given up covering it with his own weapon*. Our two sotnias tried

now, trusting to luck, to reach the next valley to the east over the steep mountain ridge. Whether there were Japanese over there as well we could not know, for we had not sent patrols in that direction either. So we proceeded in single file up the steep slope, leading the tired horses behind us by the reins. Soon the column was strung out over several thousand paces—if the Japanese pursued, they could pick off every single Cossack.

[[p300]]

In this critical situation, we at the front were suddenly startled by several shots that had been fired within our own column! “Nichevo!” said the men following behind with a laugh, pointing to a covey of pheasants that had flushed on the mountainside! It was not even a quarter of an hour since we ourselves had been fired upon; we were still within range of the enemy rifles—and already the Cossacks were no longer thinking of the danger they had just endured and found time to hunt pheasants! As huntsmen, on the other hand, they deserve full praise! Three shots had been fired, and three pheasants lay hit by bullets despite the considerable distance. “They are poachers without exception, our Transbaikal Cossacks,” said Lieutenant Colonel Slatin, excusing his men.

After about an hour, the mountain had been climbed. A tourist’s heart would have leaped at this magnificent view! Towering in massive tiers behind one another appeared the mountains of the Khingan, whose snow-covered peaks, rising above each other, seemed to billow like silver waves in the radiance of the midday sun. But the Cossacks were thinking of no such comparisons; with exhausted horses whose flanks heaved up and down, they now stood before a steep precipice whose scree gave way with every step. Along the narrow ridge, over boulders and through thorn thickets, we now proceeded, searching for a better way down. Here the small Siberian and Mongolian horses proved their full worth; with any others, this route would have been utterly impossible.

Down in the valley, all was quiet. The Chinese claimed to have seen no Japanese here. We marched on for a short distance, but then the tired horses had to be given a rest. A halt was called in a small village. It had become late afternoon; if possible, the night was to be spent here. The Cossacks began looking around in the *fanzas*; soon the chickens were fluttering anxiously about the courtyards and the piglets began squealing in mortal terror.

Half an hour later, they were already scalding in the cooking pots. Yet today we were not to enjoy their catch; the officers were just about to help themselves to their midday meal when a man burst in with the report: “The Japanese are firing on our picket!”

[[p301]]

No one, to be sure, had heard any shots, but everyone ran to the horses, which were quite reluctant to let their heads be pulled from the fodder straw ... “Mount! Move out!” But at the picket all was quiet—in all, three or four shots had been fired!

Yingpan could no longer be reached by the detachment on that day. But I felt pressed to return to regimental headquarters. Here, with the two sotnias, there was likely not much more to experience; they had as little striking power left as they were *unwilling to sacrifice themselves for the sister arms, which in retreat becomes the duty of the cavalry!*

Yet with what shall I continue my narrative? Straight away with the end? With the depiction of that beaten army streaming back, which I encountered in the days that followed, in which the bonds of order and discipline were beginning to dissolve; which, resembling a masterless mob more than marching columns, moved northwards on both sides of the railway, on the Mandarin Road, alongside it, and cross-country, leaving thousands of stragglers, wounded and dead horses, and other broken-down detritus along the way—an easy prey for the swarms of Honghuzi pressing in from the flanks and rear?
