The *Combined Division of the 17th Army Corps* (the former general reserve), which had marched out from Mukden on March 3, was to cover the Sinminting road near Houzha; the 147th Infantry Regiment was to provide flank protection north of it near the Imperial Tombs; the 1st Siberian Corps remains in Mukden. *The Second Army Headquarters and the Army High Command fall back to the Mukden railway station.*

The *supply trains of the Second Army begin to withdraw northward*; they were thoroughly intermingled. March 4 had passed without any serious fighting on the army's western front, and can therefore, in view of the enemy's turning movements to the north, which were assuming an ever more threatening form, be described as a *lost day*.

The *Third Army* on the Sha River was still holding its positions. On the left wing, *Linevich*, despite his numerical superiority, had abandoned all thoughts of an offensive and had fallen back with his headquarters to Fushun on March 4. The *Rennenkampf* Corps held out in the mountains with heroic resistance. Led by a general like Rennenkampf, the Russian soldier performs miracles of bravery and self-sacrifice. The center of gravity of the situation on that day, however, lay unmistakably on the right wing. Here, having now united the Second Army and parts of the Third Army on the northern bank of the Hun River, the Second Army Headquarters resolved upon an *attack* for March 5. According to the

It is not difficult to recognize that this disposition suffers once again from the typical flaw already discussed: the flanking column is assigned an objective, and the other groups are not to begin their advance until this has been attained by the flanking group. Yet this time, too, the Japanese were not kind enough to fall back to the Dahuang—Yaojiatun line assigned to them by the Russian Second Army Headquarters—and the entire Russian attack consequently ground to a halt.

In just the same way, on August 2 of the previous year, no coordinated general attack came about because Major General Orlov suffered a reverse at the Yantai coal station; in October, the evacuation of the Banyupu position threw the whole problem of the attack into confusion, and here the *waiting for the capture* of the Japanese positions by the Eastern Army Group led to disastrous inactivity. In February of this year, as we have seen, the impregnability of Sandepu paralyzed the entire offensive.

At 10:00 a.m. on March 5, General Gerngross's attack group was not yet in the ordered area. The blame was once again shifted onto the poor 1st Siberian Corps, which was alleged to have received the disposition only at 12:00 midnight on March 4 and consequently to have arrived late at the assembly area; this, however, is improbable, since at that time the 1st Siberian Corps stood united in bivouac, roughly eight versts from the assigned position. It is more probable that operational reasons—namely, the Japanese offensive against Majiapu manifesting itself in the *south* on March 4—caused the execution of the at long last adopted *decision to attack to be postponed*. For this reason, the *Combined Division of the 10th Corps* also seems never to have passed under General Gerngross's command, but rather to have been retained as a reserve.
