The road to Mukden was long, and it would not have been advisable to ride alone after nightfall through the graveyards and settlements outside the city proper, where many a European had already been ambushed and robbed. Thus, one morning, a Russian merchant had been brought to Professor Oettingen, a Red Cross physician, after hunghutzes had dragged him into a fansa in one of these suburbs by night and tortured him in a horrifying manner with small curved razors and red-hot irons. They believed that the man had money lying around somewhere in Mukden, and wanted to force a confession from the poor devil, who had already given his tormentors the few rubles that were all he possessed anyway. To avoid such travel adventures, I preferred to quicken my pace in order to reach Mukden before dusk.

Across the Hunhe plain swept that abominable wind once more, obscuring with its brown clouds of dust even that bit of blue sky which on clear days was the only feast for the eyes in this broad monotony. The valleys, the hills, the fansas, and even the people who lived and walked here—everything was brown, bleak, and monotonous. No forest, not a [[p138]] tree as far as the eye could see, no chirping bird, no murmuring brook; nothing but dusty brown plain before me, bare brown mountain slopes to the east, and to the west a brown, foggy haze from which the tall iron framework of the Shajiatun railway bridge stood out in thick black strokes.

The wind whistles and howls, and vying with it, drunken soldiers howl and sing as they rattle toward me at a furious pace on empty carts from Mukden along the rock-hard frozen road. Perhaps they had delivered wounded men and seized the opportunity to buy a few bottles of *tchin* from the Chinese with pay saved up over months. They whip the small horses, brandish their rifles, and act bellicose, as if they intended to drive straight into the Japanese.

It was evening when I reached the Hunhe bridge; three long lines of wagons stood before it, patiently waiting until their turn to cross came. I had Lieutenant-Colonel von Csicserics's two-wheeled cart with me, and I had to fall in behind the last of the vehicles and wait until my turn arrived! It might well be midnight by then! But here chance intervened—that coincidence which befalls us so often in life, where far out in the wide world, amid unknown people never seen before, we come upon a friend dropped as if from heaven. It was the Russian colonel with whom I had traveled from Moscow to Irkutsk, the commander of transports for the Third Manchurian Army. His bundle of furs would have made him unrecognizable had I not known his friendly voice when he laughingly invited me to take a seat in his elegant troika, which in the next minute crossed the bridge ahead of the wagons waiting there.

The joy of seeing each other again loosened our tongues, and the colonel, too, came out with his view on the war. “We have our great, rich country, we have our glorious past and our history. Look at these fansas here,” and with that he pointed to several wretched Chinese huts, “to wage war over these fansas, is that not foolish, when one has a fatherland richer and mightier than all other European states! Over these wretched Chinese fansas!” He seemed [[p139]] to have had bad experiences in these fansas, so much did the mere sight of them irritate him. He was right, besides. Although his duties would not have taken him to Mukden, he nevertheless brought me to the military attachés' train, in whose dining car the gentlemen were just gathering for dinner.
