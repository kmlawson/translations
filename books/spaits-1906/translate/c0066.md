“We can’t stay here much longer,” said the colonel. “Either forward or back—just look at these chunks of iron they’ve been hurling into our camp for the last two days”—and with that he pointed to a collection of fragments from twelve-centimetre shells that his son had arranged against the wall. “I don’t even know which one it was that smashed my hip bone—I’m only sorry that I won’t get to see with my own eyes how Father Linevich finally shows those yellows over there what’s what!” Yes, that much was clear to me: Linevich enjoyed great trust; [[p227]] *and he was strong now, too—he must have had nearly eighty-three battalions—why then had he gone back to Shihhuicheng—why had he not come here to lead forward the attack all the troops were expecting?* Here again two regiments stood idle, letting themselves be bombarded by the Japanese!

The commander of the 2nd Rifle Brigade had kindly given me a mounted rifleman as an escort; at Fanshen, with the 3rd Siberian Division, I was to be provided with another. The general staff section there was just then busy packing up. One of the officers said: “We are falling back to a more favorable position.” Another was less diplomatic, admitting that the Japanese had taken three redoubts overnight and that several shells had already flown into the village during the morning. “One of the three *sopkas*,” I asked, “you took back again, didn’t you?” “Not at all,” came the reply. “It merely changed hands back and forth—in the end, the Japanese kept it after all.”

The warm tea had done a world of good; my new escort stood ready, and now we could head back out into the snow and wind.

Up hill and down dale the path led, often quite wretched. By a ruined temple, against whose front gateway a regimental standard leaned, several battalions were bivouacking. The men stood in clusters around the campfires, which the biting wind whipped flat along the ground. They were troops of the 72nd Siberian Division, easily recognized by their numbered shoulder straps. How did they come to be here? After all, they belonged to the 6th Corps, which stood in the center with the Third Army! “Where are you marching?” I asked a soldier needlessly. “To the position,” came the short reply. More than that he could not possibly know. It was just as well, in any case, to break the habit of asking. *“Ne mogu znat!”* (I cannot know!)—that was the ever-recurring, stereotypical reply. Often a convenient way out for mental indolence, but mostly the plain truth. Where one stood oneself today was still known; [[p228]] but who was to the left and to the right, to which general one actually belonged, where tomorrow’s supply train was to be sent—assuming it was not already lost altogether—changed not from day to day, but from hour to hour. For that reason, with the best will in the world, no one could possibly know. Thus General Zasulich had also believed the commander of the neighboring 3rd Siberian Corps to still be in Xiandalinshan, whereas he was already much farther south at Panjiazi,

north of the Gaotuling Pass, which had been fought over since February 25.

The sun was sinking toward the mountains, and Panjiazi was still far away. The headquarters of the 3rd Siberian Corps was indeed still in Xiandalinshan, but I hoped along this route to reach the Eastern Corps sooner.

It must have been around six o'clock in the evening when my guide turned south off the path we had been following. Over two more low passes—the Russians call them *pereval*—and we rode down into a narrow valley that led to Panjiazi.

[[p229]]

As if invisible portals had opened, the din of a distant struggle suddenly came sounding between the steep mountain walls, borne on the wind. As yet it resembled a slowly vibrating, dull rumble, which the thunder of battery volleys drowned out from time to time. On the narrow path running along the ice of a stream, soldiers marched closely packed; their heads were bent forward, for the wind drove the snow into their eyes. Now and then empty ammunition carts came galloping toward them from the opposite direction; then they had to make way and step onto the ice, which frequently broke through. Sullenly they would pull their soaked felt boots out of the water. Heavy two-wheeled *arbas* also came toward us, perhaps a hundred or even two hundred of them. Those lying on them were covered up over their heads with gray wool blankets. They came from the field hospital situated by a long wall at the entrance to the next village. Behind this wall people seemed to be digging, for from time to time the heads of iron picks showed above it. They are probably digging trenches again, I thought, and rode over to the wall to look across. Far from it! Graves were being dug here. Several had already been filled in; on these stood crosses of white wood without names. Beside them a priest was pacing up and down.
