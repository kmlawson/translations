We spent the night in the fine fanza in Nanjiahe. It was so large that Cossacks were also billeted together with us in the same room.

8 March.

Early in the morning, before our spies sent out the previous evening had returned, Lieutenant-Colonel Slatin sent the regimental commander the following report:

“Japanese infantry (approx. 500) in Hanjiapuzi and Tupdabusa. In the Kosadu valley there are still further Japanese.

On the line Gundjason—Matande and on the second line (?) Ulingon—Shidajiazi—Madansadu as well as in Totikotsen there is likewise Japanese infantry; their patrols have reached as far as the village of Liujiabuzi.

Spies were sent by me to Dapu, Qingbiapuzi, and to the south.”

Such was the wording of the report, without further commentary.

Personally, Lieutenant-Colonel Slatin was quite an excellent comrade. Educated, amiable, and quick of comprehension, he stood far above the intellectual level of the officers subordinate to him. In years he was younger than they, which perhaps had an adverse effect on his assertiveness toward them. Surely we would have been on our way today as well, had not the sluggishness of the two Cossack yesauls put on the brakes always and everywhere.

[[p292]]

While we thus spent the morning quite pointlessly in Nanjiahe, I used the time to inspect our host's fanza a little more closely. I noticed beautiful ink drawings pasted to the wall. I asked the Chinese man for one of them and offered him money for it, but in vain. The Amur Cossack chieftain happened to join us and asked me what I desired. “You want this drawing here? — Well, that is simple!” — and with that he pulled his knife from his pocket and cut the paper down from the wall for me. The host put a good face on it — knowing full well, after all, that he would not lose out in the final settlement with the Russians.¹)

Finally, towards noon, after a hearty breakfast, we set out to clear our way over the heights south of Chebaipo with united forces.

Around three o'clock in the afternoon we arrived at this village, where the spies sent ahead that morning were awaiting us. The passes were still occupied by the enemy, the heights further east too steep to make a wider detour in that direction — we had to attack the enemy.

Our two sotnias dismounted for the firefight; the Amur Cossacks remained in reserve. To the west of us, around General Danilov, heavy fighting seemed to be taking place; artillery salvos from friend and foe echoed over to us in ever more rapid succession. But soon things came alive on the pereval before us, against which the Cossacks had advanced in a skirmish line. Between the dark bushes, little white clouds rose up, soon followed by the sharp crack of rifles. Yet the enemy greeted us with only slow, scattered skirmish fire, as if wishing to encourage us to advance. In good time, Lieutenant-Colonel Slatin recognized the danger that could threaten us from the heights on our right flank. If these were occupied by the enemy, our retreat could be cut off from there if the dismounted men advanced further.

The sotnias advancing to the attack were ordered to halt. They fired several volleys against the pereval, but their effect could not be observed because of the tall brush covering the slopes. Individual horsemen now rode to the right toward the heights on which we likewise suspected Japanese.

Sending forward such reconnaissance patrols early in the morning, or at least at the beginning of the march, had been regularly neglected. Thus the detachment now had to wait another two to three hours until the horsemen had climbed the high mountain opposite and returned with the news that, apart from a few armed Chinese, nothing was to be seen of the enemy. The two sotnias were just about to follow the vanguard, which had approached the pass from the side through brush and young timber, when an order arrived from regimental headquarters to fall back immediately to Yingpan. Why fall back? This order came unexpectedly! Had not the clerk who returned yesterday from staff headquarters recounted that General Kuropatkin was already on the way to Liaoyang! From the south—that was General Danilov—our own guns were still booming—why fall back, then? Some officers thought the regiment might now be sent to the *right wing* to march with Kuropatkin to Liaoyang; others began to recount episodes from the fighting in August and October, in which the *general retreat* had always begun in a similar manner.

That the Japanese on the right wing were already on the Xinmintun road northwest of Mukden, and their own center had already been forced back to the Hun River, the sotnias here in the mountains could hardly know.
