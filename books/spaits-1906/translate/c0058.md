During the afternoon hours and the previous night, the Japanese had attacked the Russian positions south of Kandolisan [[p204]] at Wanyepuzi and Sunupudza, but had been repulsed with heavy losses. Many of the wounded who were led past us carried, in addition to their own, a Japanese bayonet that they had taken from a fallen opponent.

Yet the Russians, too, had attempted to take the offensive that night, again of course with only a small fraction of their strong forces. Thus, a small but daring band—the scout detachment of the First Siberian Reserve Division—had crossed the Shahe after nightfall,

taken by surprise the Japanese outposts, who on this bitterly cold night did not anticipate an attack by the Russians, and after a brief bayonet fight reached Nanshanpu—a village on the southern bank of the Shahe—entrenching itself in its northwestern half. At the same time, as mentioned earlier, the Japanese attack against the Russian positions had been repulsed, so that our scout detachment was caught between the Japanese forces falling back across the Shahe and those still holding Nanshanpu. Two of its men had succeeded in reaching their own positions [[p205]] via Sunupudza and reporting on the desperate situation of their cut-off detachment.

How much blood had flowed once again, how many had stood in mortal terror or were perhaps even now facing the strangler Death, who had already mercilessly drawn his circle around the small band on the other bank of the river, while we, barely an hour’s march away, enjoyed the bright sunshine. Heavy rapid fire from the mountains had indeed awakened us around one in the morning—yet the kang on which we slept was warmly heated, we were tired as well, so we turned over onto the other ear and went on sleeping until the sun stood high in the sky.

At the mortar ammunition park, besides the commander, three reserve officers and the veterinarian we already know were stationed. He was a good photographer and had brought along excellent large cameras.

Anything one might call duty I did not observe at this ammunition park, yet everything proceeded in the best of order. Empty ammunition wagons returned, full ones went up into the mountains, then an ammunition column from the army park arrived, everything received and directed by the long-bearded Ivan Ivanovich, the ordnance sergeant. Only once did an official discord ripple through this peaceful atmosphere. The cow, which was carried on the strength of the draft horses, had failed to yield the morning milk needed for our breakfast, and for some reason not entirely clear to me, Ivan Ivanovich was supposed to be held responsible for this. On that occasion a sharp reprimand was administered to him by his commander from bed. Whether it bore fruit I do not know, for the next morning already found me up with the mortars. In the evening, the officers from the battery had arrived; one of them had a magnificent voice. There was singing, guitar playing, and throats did not remain dry either. The officers had come to say goodbye, for the Second East Siberian Mortar Battery was to go into action tomorrow for the first time. For three months they had lain idle in the zemlyankas near Kandolisan, until at last, tired of idly looking on, they had asked General Zasulich, commander of the Second Siberian Corps, [[p206]] for permission to lead the battery into the positions from which their high-angle fire was to bring under bombardment the part of the elongated village of Nanshanpu occupied by the Japanese.

The joy at facing the enemy the next day was sincere and unconstrained—it grew ever wilder the closer morning approached.

Soon it was decided that I would go with them.
