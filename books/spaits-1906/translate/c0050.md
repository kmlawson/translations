And now a few words about the Russian war correspondents. Of these there was an immense number; they brought the relatively small number of foreign correspondents up to two hundred and sixty. In terms of quality, however, with few exceptions they were quite inferior. The *Novoye Vremya* [*New Times*] had an officer who had been slightly wounded at Liaoyang and who served, so to speak, as its official correspondent; in addition to him, it had a professional journalist in the theatre of war—both very pleasant, amiable companions who were equal to their task. As for the rest who went about under the title of Russian correspondents, they could hardly have had anything to do with the writing profession before the war. I remember finding myself one morning—it was on the return journey from Gongzhuling to Harbin—sharing a compartment, to my little delight, with an individual who was bristling with dirt and weeks of unwashedness—one could hardly credit his bulbous, greasy fingers with any acquaintance with a penholder—and yet the man identified himself as a correspondent for the *Birzhevye Vedomosti* [*Stock Exchange Gazette*] (Exchange Courier), a paper whose telegrams Hungarian newspapers in particular were fond of reprinting.

From such people, of course, a considered judgment, a report reflecting the situation as a whole, was not to be expected. Mostly they hung around station restaurants, staging posts, or brothels, and whatever bad things they saw there, they reported to their paper for lack of anything better. A drunken officer, a worthless reservist who had exchanged his government-issue clothes for a bottle of spirits, a few dissolute women whom he used to visit in their regular haunts—such were the subjects he knew how to write about, and from which he drew conclusions about the whole. And in this very respect there was [[p178]] hardly anything to reproach in the army. The *soldiers were superbly fed and clothed, their appearance was excellent*, and the fact that further back along the line of communication people sometimes drank more than thirst required, and that obscene scenes took place in brothels, could hardly be a yardstick for the ethical and moral worth of the army!

Such spiteful accounts flowed far more from Russian than from foreign pens, however. For the most part, people at home wished thereby to arouse pity and spur charitable giving, without considering how severely the reputation of the Russian army was damaged by it.

In addition to the censorship in Mukden, there were also censorship offices in the principal towns of the staging area and in Vladivostok. That the distribution of the material to be reviewed was poorly regulated is shown by the following example: [[p179]] About a month after the Battle of Mukden, I visited Colonel Pestics, who had relocated with the censorship to Gongzhuling.

Of my acquaintances, I met only Lieutenant Fehrmann; Staff Captain Baron van der Höven had been cut off by the Japanese at Xinmintun, Count Orloff was on leave, and a fourth officer, who had been tasked with reviewing the French reports, had been obliged to return to his regiment. To my astonishment, I saw letters from the firms of Tillmann and Albert in Vladivostok, addressed to Shanghai, being opened here in Gongzhuling, even though the route to Shanghai led via Harbin, Petrovsk-Zavod, and then through the Gobi Desert via Urga and Peking. Sending the letters down to Gongzhuling (when there were censorship offices in Vladivostok, Harbin, and the Manchuria border station) dated back to the time when Port Arthur still belonged to the Russians, and it now meant that this correspondence arrived at its destination ten to twenty days later, while for the field censorship itself it was an utterly unnecessary burden.

“That is nothing at all,” said Colonel Pestics, “just look at this.” They were three picture postcards—“to the victorious commanders Oyama and Kuroki”! One from Aarau, the second from Basel, the third from Vienna (Mariahilfergürtel 29). The writers congratulated the Japanese commanders on their victories and modestly asked, each and every one of them, for an autograph at the end.
