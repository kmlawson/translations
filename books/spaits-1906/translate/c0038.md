An unpleasant coincidence gave me an opportunity to see for myself the quality of the Russian veterinarians. Lieutenant Colonel Csicserics had an excellent horse. Since it had formerly belonged to the Spanish military attaché, Sanyi had christened it “Marquis.” One day it went lame, and the veterinarian summoned from headquarters simply ordered that it be shot immediately. In response to a few professional questions from us, which seemed to surprise him, he finally explained that in his whole life he had actually never had *anything to do with horses*, but that until the outbreak of the war only cows and dogs had been his patients! Poor Marquis later had, when

[Illustration: Russian remount commission.] [[p135]] Huangshan was evacuated by the Japanese, unfortunately to be shot after all, since until then no one had known how to treat him.

Procuring a good horse was quite a difficult matter at that time. All serviceable stock had been bought up by the Russian remount commissions, so that the Chinese dealers were only offering animals afflicted with every hippological defect.

The news that the Swedish military attaché, Captain Edlund, was departing for home and that his horses were for sale decided me to return to Mukden.

On the morning I rode away, the military attachés had put on their full-dress uniforms to attend a mournful ceremony—the field requiem for Grand Duke Sergei, who had been murdered in Moscow. His fate had caught up with him; no one shed a tear for him.

On the way back, I stopped in to see Major Runkel. I found him ready to travel. “Are you going to Mukden as well, Major?” “Thank God, a bit farther,” he replied with a laugh, “to Berlin! After all, I cannot accomplish anything useful here—by straightforward means—with this damned secrecy everywhere, so I asked to be relieved and will await the answer in Mukden.” We were standing in front of the fansa as a detachment marched past us. “Where are you marching to?” Major Runkel asked a non-commissioned officer. “To position,” was the brief reply. “I could have figured that out myself,” Runkel grumbled vexedly, “but you simply cannot get anything more out of these fellows—and one is supposed to write reports based on that!”

“Do you believe, Major, that there will hardly be a great decisive battle now?” “I believe,” was the reply, “*what all the other military attachés also foresee and what the Russian General Staff itself most ardently desires: that there will be another great mutual slaughter here on the Shahe, with each side leaving several thousand dead; neither will yield ground, and both, Japanese as well as Russians, will then have to see that things cannot go on like this, and will make peace.*”

[[p136]]

These were also roughly the words that Major Baron Tettau had spoken to me a few weeks earlier in Mukden, being very indignant at my expressed hope that the war would only now properly begin. “You surely don't imagine that the war will drag on for another year for your sake; as you can see, I am already preparing for the return journey,” he said, pointing to a guidebook, *Travers la Chine* [*Across China*], which he had borrowed from the French attaché, Captain Boucé, in order to put together an interesting route back. “One more big massacre, in which the Russians, *thanks to their current significant numerical superiority*, will apparently succeed in scoring a success, so that at least the honour of their arms is saved, and then we are going home!”

[Illustration: Distribution of rations at headquarters in Huangshan.]

So thought the German officers and nearly all the other foreign officers. Lieutenant Colonel von Csicserics, on the other hand, was of a different opinion. “Now,” he said, “would be the best, but also the last opportunity for Russia to conclude a relatively favourable peace. Now—this was still *before* the Battle of Mukden—the army stands numerically superior and un- [[p137]] *defeated* before the Japanese, and the Russian fleet, too, is approaching Japanese waters—still commanding respect today. These are numerical power factors which Japan would certainly have to take into account in a peace settlement; *but as far as I know the Russians, they will not make peace.* Given the military impotence they have displayed thus far, however, it cannot be assumed, even for the future, that they will ever achieve operational success. Where is all their numerical superiority today? Dissipated along a long, thin line; the Japanese can break through it wherever they please! And what follows then?” At this he used to point to the defile of Tieling north of Mukden marked on the map: “Then there will be a debacle here!” How accurate the assumption of Lieutenant Colonel von Csicserics was—of whom the Russian officers themselves said that he knew their army better than they did—events have confirmed.
