# General Mishchenko's Raid and the Battle of Sandepu.

## The Raid on Yingkou.

### Purpose and Plan.

The word "raid" recalls the dashing cavalry general Stuart, and when we hear of a raid by General Mishchenko, we suppose that he must have carried it out just as dashily, or perhaps even more so, than the brave American cavalry leader. For this time it was, after all, a Cossack raid, the first major undertaking of that Russian cavalry—now finally unleashed and so greatly feared in Europe—for whose sake so many permanent cavalry barracks were once built in the beautiful land of Galicia, which today are an even more permanent exile for young cavalry officers.

For the understanding of the non-military reader, let it be explained that a "raid" means an incursion planned on a larger scale by a relatively strong body of cavalry.

Its mission is to inflict damage on the enemy's rear and flank, and possibly to divert his attention from those operations planned against the opponent's front.

The principal element is that of surprise, so that the enemy has no opportunity to take countermeasures; furthermore, the raid must harmonize *in timing* with the main operations directed against the enemy. Thus, once again, the correct choice, or rather seizing, of the *moment* plays a prominent role.

The raid against Yingkou can only be regarded as a partial operation of that great offensive movement upon which the Russians were finally forced to decide after the fall of Port Arthur.

The Japanese had not delayed, immediately after the fall of Port Arthur, in beginning the dispatch of the forces now released here toward the north to their operational army. [[p74]] The task of General-Adjutant Mishchenko could now be to bypass the enemy's left flank, appear by surprise in his rear, and, by destroying the railway line, delay the further transport of the Port Arthur force group, divert the enemy's attention from the front through such a diversion in his rear, and thereby create more favorable conditions of attack for his own army.

Certainly each of these two tasks, which could also be accomplished simultaneously, was worthy of a total effort. Seldom will a cavalry commander find himself in a position to employ his forces so decisively on the further course of the main operations as on this occasion, and such an opportunity, which promised to bring the Russian cavalry its long-awaited laurels, was well suited to awaken full energy and drive—if such were at all to be awakened in him.

The right moment for such a diversion had probably already been somewhat missed again, as the Japanese by this time had already dispatched a good part of their siege army toward the north; nevertheless, the enemy's entire situation—above all his long, vulnerable line of communication to the sea—could still be considered promising.

The account of events that now follows is based on the precise data that I owe to the kindness of the Norwegian General Staff Captain Nyquist, who rode along on the raid at General Mishchenko's headquarters. *Not so the critique of it, for which I alone am responsible here.* Anyone who could not form such a critique for himself needed only to listen to the voices of several senior General Staff officers in Mukden, who discussed just as vehemently the planning of this raid as well as various purely private considerations which, as was so often the case in this war, exerted an ill influence on the execution of the operations.
