## Petersburg.

*December 1904.*

For a week now I have been in the capital of the tsarist empire, which stands under the sign of one of the bloodiest wars of all time—and behold! Nothing, absolutely nothing of the war to be felt! It may well also be the present standstill of operations that has smoothed the waves of agitation, yet they are said not to have run particularly high among the people even during the days of Liaoyang. The theatre of war is many thousands of miles away from the heart of the empire. This alone gives the war and its home front a different character from those cases where the capital is at the same time an operational objective of the enemy. The comparison of Petersburg today with Vienna at the time of the July days of sixty-six, or with Paris during the German advance, can serve as a factor in assessing the political and military situation. In the tsarist empire, *space and time play a different role than with us Western and Central Europeans.* What do the several hundred kilometres from the Yalu to Mukden—taken, of course, as a loss of ground—signify compared to the colossal dimensions of the empire, and what do one or two more years of war mean in the slowly rolling history of Russia! The Russian does not know the proverb “time is money”; yet if patience really brought roses, Providence would long ago have stuck a rose in the buttonhole of every Old Believer.

Everything here runs in its accustomed groove; calm and grave. Were it not for the bright jingling of bells from hundreds upon hundreds of little sledges—Petersburg's peculiar means of transport—ringing out merrily until late at night through the wide [[p24]] streets and across the endlessly vast squares, an ear deafened by the din of our great cities could scarcely believe itself to be in a city of a million people. But let us not content ourselves with strolling the streets; let us look deeper into the soul of the people and investigate what lies hidden behind this almost sluggish-seeming calm.

Already at the border I had noticed beside the ticket window those little collection boxes with the Red Cross; some for the bereaved, others for field ambulances and hospitals. Along the way, as well as here in Petersburg, I find on every tavern table, in every shop a collection point for charitable gifts intended for the orphans of fallen soldiers; neat little tin houses into whose chimney now a kopeck, now even a gold coin drops.

[Illustration: Winter Palace on the Neva.]

And now onward. Let us make our way into a salon of Petersburg high life. Bright girls' voices ring out to greet us; from the adjoining rooms we hear the whirr of sewing machines. The lady of the house, a long pair of shears in her right hand, stands at an enormous cutting table that has been placed in the middle of the state room. All around, blooming youth, at [[p25]] whose cradle it was never sung that they would wield the needle. There is joking, laughing—and sewing for the soldiers in the Far East. After the theatre, gentlemen in tailcoats appear; they too are enlisted in the service of charity and, to the amusement and mockery of the ladies, must try their hand at various menial tasks of the noble tailor's craft. Thus people chat and flirt, speaking of everything except the war; and yet many a piece of white linen that today still glides through lovely hands will in a few months be soaked with red blood!

A few ladies have left the gathering early. In the summer they completed an auxiliary training course and are now performing auxiliary service, partly in hospitals, partly in outpatient clinics for convalescent wounded; that requires being in good time and punctual. “How could all this be otherwise,” the lady of the house told me, “when our own sublime monarch leads the way untiringly with an inspiring example. You must go and see the Winter Palace; then you will understand what the Empress's love was capable of creating for our soldiers.”

A broad marble staircase led me the following day into a glittering foyer. A brief introduction to an officer of the Guards, who requests my card. Footmen in gold-embroidered liveries carry tea trays into the halls. Along the wall lie small and large parcels, sewn into coarse burlap, addressed to Mukden, Harbin, etc. I had kindly been exempted from the ceremonial prescribed for visiting the imperial apartments; I was introduced to Countess M., who conducted me into the halls.

First, a spacious apartment in which several hundred sewing machines are merrily turning. Among other things, handkerchiefs are being hemmed here, printed at the four corners with portraits of the imperial family, and in the centre with maps of the theatre of war showing Port Arthur. In the next hall, four stately ladies preside at large tables; I believe they cut the fabric and then distribute the work to young ladies who look quite charming in their white aprons and sleeve protectors. My complete ignorance of plain needlework unfortunately prevents me from following the factual explanations of my charming [[p26]] companion with proper understanding, yet wherever I look, I see diligent and exceedingly delicate hands busily at work. All linen goods are washed before being packed. We come to a spacious pressing room, where the linens are sorted and stamped. What a mundane domestic activity beneath lavishly gilded chandeliers and marble cornices! We come into a hall where bed linen for hospital trains is being made, then into a department for woollens and warm footwear. Now we enter the Empress's famous winter garden. In the vestibule is a large machine that automatically cuts bandages. Here I also meet several ladies from yesterday's gathering. Their hands are covered in paste; their task here is to pack and seal bandages.
