### At Redoubt No. 9.

At the gorge—the only exit from the redoubt facing away from the enemy—Nikolai Semyonovich, a captain in the 3rd Siberian Nerchinsk Regiment, was waiting for me and led me into his quarters, which, apart from offering a certain degree of protection against shrapnel balls, had few other virtues. A small door lined with torn sacking, reached via a narrow communication trench, led into a completely dark space; it had to be very small, for with every movement one bumped against something above or to the side. At last Nikolai Semyonovich found the matches. A board for a table, some sort of makeshift contraption that served for sitting and sleeping, a small tin stove with a tea kettle hanging above it. Here in this hole the officer had lived for four long winter months. Yet even this hiding place [[p209]] he had to share with someone, upon whose feet I had sat down in the darkness and who now started up from his sleep in fright. Second Lieutenant of the Reserve Pyotr Mikhailovich, his captain introduced him to me. He had kept watch through the night—thus sharing duty with the commander.

“When will there be peace?” was the lieutenant’s first question. “You see, I have a family, small children at home,” he added, as if by way of apology for his question, indicating with his hand the approximate height his offspring might have reached during his thirteen-month absence. I could well understand that his home over in Siberia had a greater appeal for him than Redoubt No. 9 with all its trenches and dugouts.

If I had fancied that it would be more pleasant here in the redoubt than outside by the battery, I had been sorely mistaken.

I was just trying to sip the rather hot tea when a peculiar slapping noise began above our heads, and immediately afterwards earth fell from the ceiling. Semyonovich cast a glance at the lieutenant—nothing more. “Can’t we step outside for a bit?” I finally ventured to break the expectant silence that had followed this sinister clapping. “No, no one may go out now, it’s about to start,” said the captain, “but later on you can take some pictures for me with that gadget there,” he pointed to my camera. We did not have to wait long for it to “start.” Here in the semidarkness of the dugout, it seemed as if outside a terrible hailstorm with stones the size of pigeon eggs had broken loose. Above us, beside us, everywhere it rattled, and from the ceiling, through the joints of the thick timbers, fell a veritable rain of earth. Mikhailovich offered me a second glass of tea. “It never lasts long,” he added, pointing upward with his finger. “Don't you care for tea?” “Certainly, but yours is still too hot for me—I’ll wait until it cools down.” “And what will happen,” I continued, “if the Japanese send over some Shimose shells instead of shrapnel?” “They’ve done that too, but they didn’t hit—God protects us from that, for He surely wants me to see my [[p210]] children again!” Under other circumstances such naive trust would have delighted me, but now, when the frail ceiling above our heads seemed barely able to withstand the shrapnel balls rattling down ever more fiercely, I cursed the hour in which I had resolved to enter this mousetrap. Here a single heavy shell splinter would suffice to smash through the none-too-thick timbers—and then, good night!

The reserve lieutenant—surely he had never heard of the penetrating power of projectiles—sat quietly through this calamity-threatening spectacle, drew a worn photograph from his pocket, and showed it to me by the dim light of the candle: it was his wife with two children in her arms.

Captain Nikolai Semyonovich had no children—but he had a toothache. He took advantage of the time when he could not go out anyway to brush his gums with ether, whose pungent smell only hastened the “empty feeling in the pit of the stomach” that was beginning to assert itself in me despite all resistance. “For four long months,” he explained to me, “he had asked every week for the regiment to send him a doctor; once one had indeed come, but he had brought no forceps with him. Since then, he had simply grown accustomed to the toothache as well.”

Thus we sat in silence side by side for perhaps another hour, until finally—as at the end of a thunderstorm—the slapping above us slowly ceased. “Now it’s over, let’s go outside!” said Semyonovich and opened the door, through which, like a deliverance, the bright, warm sunlight flooded in. “And how do you know that it’s over now?” “It’s the same story every day—the firing from across the way always begins at midday—that’s why I had you brought in to my quarters.” Our real duty only begins at night—then one of us must always keep watch—so they don’t take us by surprise. At times, however, both officers, along with their entire garrison, had to be on their feet day and night. None of them was permitted to leave the fort. When the sun shone bright and cheerful, they could sit on the gorge parapet and look down into the Shahe valley or, just [[p211]] like today, take pleasure in the flight of the mortar bombs that our mortars fired in high arcs across the redoubt. This time, however, it was better to keep just behind the sheltering crest line. From here there was a magnificent view into the valley, toward the Russian redoubt lying before us, whose abatis stood out sharp and thorny against the snow-glistening opposite slope of the “Two-Horned Mountain,” from which flashed the fire of Japanese batteries.
