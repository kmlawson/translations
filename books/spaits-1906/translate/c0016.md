## Harbin.

[Illustration: Doctors and Red Cross nurses.]

For four days we rolled from Manchuria to Harbin, and in third class at that. Yet it could have been worse, for nowadays one usually travels by *teplushka*, that is, in a heated freight car. The journey will always remain a fond travel memory for me. The car was spacious, well heated, and we soon made ourselves at home in it. Our commander, a reserve officer from Tomsk, refreshed us generously with canned provisions and *shimosa*—a military euphemism [[p61]] for vodka. From Hailar onward, a pretty, cheerful Polish woman took over the duties of housewife; everyone was happy and in good spirits, and so time passed more quickly than we would have liked. The journey across the Greater Khingan offered some scenic charm, and this stretch with its loops, viaducts, and tunnels is certainly quite interesting from an engineering standpoint; otherwise, the landscape seems monotonous and tedious. Bare brown hill ranges, now and then mountains with deep gorges and watercourses, from Tsitsihar onward flat plain, everything lifeless and desolate. Mighty are the Asian rivers, of which we crossed the Nonni and the Sungari here at the end. Before Harbin, for the first time in a long while, I saw well-tilled fields again.

En route one had the opportunity to visit hospitals and barracks. More frequently now one sees the Red Cross flag flying; almost every station accommodates wounded and sick. The military guarding of the railway line is already far stricter here. Next to each station stands the frontier guard barracks, a solid, airy timber structure, usually for fifty men and a few mounted soldiers. Beside the barracks is the alarm signal, a tall pole wrapped with pitch wreaths; on the nearest hill, an observation tower. The Hunhuz raid that I secretly wished for as a travel adventure unfortunately failed to materialize; in general, matters are said not to have been nearly so bad with these frequently recounted Hunhuz attacks on railway trains. For the most part, the fighting in the railcars played out between Hunhuzes and their mortal enemies, the Chinese; nowhere was I able to learn of any attacks by these bands on Russian troops. Nevertheless, the military precautions, especially the outfitting of all bridges for artillery defense, pointed to the possibility of such attacks.

When we crossed the high structure of the Sungari Bridge, Harbin lay before us in bright sunshine; first the harbor with numerous ships still trapped in the ice, behind it a vast sea of houses. We had to wait three hours before our train could enter the station, which was packed full with transport trains, locomotives, and railcars.

The station building surprises by its size, cleanliness, [[p62]] and above all by its modern Secessionist style. The station lies between the harbor and the New Town, to which a broad, straight avenue leads. The favorable impression upon arrival, unfortunately, soon passes. In the vicinity of the station is an ice rink decorated with colorful flags, then a very tasteful building that, intended as a hotel, currently serves as an officers' hospital. Farther along are the Russo-Chinese Bank, a row of wooden barracks, and finally a dainty, wooden Russian church in front of which stand two small muzzle-loading cannons of Chinese origin; those would be all the sights along the roughly two-kilometer route.

Of the many streets, only a few are built up; one of the latter bears the proud name “Sungari Prospekt.” Otherwise, this part of the city is nothing but an endless, hilly waste upon which residential houses, administrative offices, hospitals, and earthen huts are scattered far apart without any order; mostly unfinished, crude, tasteless brick buildings still awaiting their coat of mortar. The imposing, again Secessionist building of the Chinese Railway Administration makes no impression in these repulsive surroundings.

Down in the harbor quarter it is merrier, livelier, even if not exactly more beautiful. Of the 44,000 inhabitants that Harbin counts—not including the garrison—the greater number falls to this part of the city, which has a mixed European-Chinese character. Here one finds the commercial offices of German and Russian firms alongside the stalls of the natives, restaurants, cookshops, a Chinese theater, the Russian opera—today they are performing *La Traviata*—and even a circus. Old Harbin, the purely Chinese quarter, is filthy and of little interest. Before the war, the place maintained a significant trade with foreign countries. Pelts from Northern Manchuria (Hailar) went from here by boat to Germany; likewise, bean cakes, wheat, and pig bristles formed significant export commodities.

The people here are not entirely displeased with the handover of Port Arthur into Japanese hands; it is hoped that Dalny will in future become a commercial center similar to Shanghai and that the hinterland will benefit from this. Perhaps one will [[p63]] [Illustration: Harbin station.]
