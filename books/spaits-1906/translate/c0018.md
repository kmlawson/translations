My confidence in the Russian army had been renewed. Everything I had seen along the way of soldiers, officers, hospital trains, and hospitals had satisfied me to a high degree. That now and then an officer is drunk, or a soldier has begged from someone under cover of dusk, by no means permits an unfavorable conclusion about the whole. The men were excellently clothed and looked well-nourished. The lads around me, too, who still undauntedly sang “Körösi kis leány,” were splendid fellows, with dashing yet good-natured countenances—only they shouldn’t have sung. The dim candlelight above the carriage door had burned down, the hoarse voices died away in the steady hum and rumble of the rolling wheels, and then all became dark and quiet.

From the other corner of the carriage, a small red lamp glowed through the smoke-filled air. It burned beneath an icon of the Virgin Mary, which one of the Cossacks had nailed up there.

Over the final stretch before Mukden we had advanced quite slowly. At every stop we halted for several hours. Pushing past us back toward the north, however, came train after train, heavily laden with wounded. During the night, the groans and whimpers of those crammed together here in tight quarters could be heard. The battle on the western flank had begun yesterday; they did not seem to have been prepared for such heavy losses. And these here were only the less seriously wounded; those unfit for transport filled the hospitals of Mukden.

The outcome of the struggle was still undecided. I still [[p69]] harbored the hope of being able to accompany the brave Russian soldiers on their triumphant march toward Port Arthur. How astonished I was that few of the Russians shared my confidence. On January 24—we were then halted just outside Mukden—a rumor had spread that Sandepu had already been taken. I still remember a *colonel* who, beaming with joy, burst into our compartment with the words: “On February 1, Kuropatkin will begin the advance on Liaoyang with the whole army!”—“Why, how do you know that?”—“A wounded man just told me!”—Yet such childlike optimism could, only a few hours later, give way to a timidity as yet unjustified; people were already speaking of a rift that was said to have erupted between the commander-in-chief and the commander of the Second Army, General Grippenberg.

More and more wounded, and soon I heard from the southwest the dull rumble of the guns—slow, solemn, it echoed over from the flat Hun River plain, where the Grim Reaper had been reaping his harvest for two days.

Before I speak of the events at Sandepu, I consider it necessary to mention General Mishchenko’s raid. While it is merely an episode that had little influence on the course of the major events, it contains from a military-psychological standpoint such characteristic features that, after studying it, we can more easily explain many later events of this war.
