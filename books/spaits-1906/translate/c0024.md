There was no plan of the station and its installations on the basis of which the work to be carried out could have been distributed and organized. The two sapper officers had, as mentioned, fallen during the assault, and so the detachments ran about aimlessly in the dark between the buildings, setting fire to a storehouse here or a shed there; to attempt demolitions in this disorder would have posed the greatest danger to their own units.

On the Russian side, no one attempted to penetrate into the town of Yingkou itself—with the exception of the American correspondent Little! He had accompanied the raid and followed the troops to the station. Here, the journalist could not resist the proximity of the telegraph office in the town—during the night he succeeded in making his way into the town, dispatching his telegrams, and in the following days catching up with the Russian detachment on its retreat!

Is it not astonishing that the defenseless journalist penetrates into the town for the sake of his dispatches, while the twelve squadrons must content themselves with insignificant destruction at the railway station—must, for at 11 o’clock at night the order from General Mishchenko to break off the engagement and begin the retreat reached the attacking group. In this campaign things occurred that are so different from what we have learned, and especially from what we assumed of the Russian cavalry, that one had to have been on the spot to be able to believe such a thing!

In the group commanded personally by General Mishchenko, reports had arrived toward afternoon that a Japanese battalion was advancing from Dashiqiao. General Mishchenko sent a regiment of the Caucasian Cavalry Brigade forward against it, which naturally could not withstand the regular infantry attack of the Japanese and was forced to retreat around 10 o’clock in the evening.

One regiment fights, the other eleven stand idle and wait—wait until that one is beaten—then they all retire together, because they do not want to risk suffering still greater losses. This is the typical employment of forces by the Russian generals in small matters as in great, in tactics as in strategy, which alone is to blame for the fact that the Russians, despite numerical superiority and *excellent soldier material*, have never—not once in this campaign—recorded any noteworthy success. Twelve squadrons halt where a lone journalist presses forward, and at 10 o’clock at night General Mishchenko orders his cavalry corps to retreat in the face of a single advancing Japanese infantry battalion!—And how does the general justify this measure? “A more important task awaited him in the general attack on Sandepu, for which he had to arrive in good time and for the sake of which he had to spare his forces!”

Had General Mishchenko not later proved himself personally to be a brave soldier at Sandepu, had he not, braving enemy bullets, been wounded there, one [[p88]] would have to ascribe his measures before Yingkou to a lack of personal courage. But that is not so. Russian generals have rarely lacked personal courage; the casualty lists, headed invariably by Russian colonels and generals, prove this sufficiently. What was always lacking, however, was an understanding of the value of time and of seizing the given moment.

The raid on Yingkou had already as good as succeeded, for there were no significant enemy forces present to prevent the destruction of the town and the threat to the lines of communication of the Japanese field army. The Russian cavalry could have gathered its first laurels here and indirectly secured an outstanding share in the outcome of the Battle of Sandepu. As it was, however, nothing was accomplished at Yingkou because they still wanted to go to Sandepu, and nothing was accomplished at Sandepu because the troops were already exhausted by the arduous march to and from Yingkou.

The Russian losses before Yingkou amounted to 300 wounded and a few dead, the latter being carried along by the retreating troops and buried in the course of the night.

The detachment spent the night of the 12th–13th several versts north of the campsite of the previous day.
