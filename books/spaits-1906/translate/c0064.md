"The people here are like earthworms," my Russian companion explained to me; "they hardly feel the cutting off of a limb, and such tortures are necessary to arouse in them the fear of punishment; simple hanging or beheading excites neither the spectators nor even the condemned man particularly."

A few months later, I had the opportunity to convince myself of the correctness of the latter assertion.

It was some time after the Battle of Mukden, in Kuanchengzi, the second-largest city in Manchuria. Here, too, there was such a court of justice, and I was present there at the execution of 13 honghuzi. The executioner, recognisable by a gleaming curved sword whose blade he tapped affectionately with his crooked finger and whose clear ring seemed to delight him, had a fat, genial face. His assistants were smoking from small opium pipes; no preparations whatever indicated that a bloody spectacle would take place here in a few minutes.

At last, a pack of people came along from the opposite gate. They were the 13 condemned men, their hands tied behind their backs, dragging broad iron shackles with chains on their feet. No more than two Chinese, seemingly chatting intimately with the condemned, were their escorts. The whole scene was so peaceful that I could hardly believe in the impending beheadings. Since these were bandits whom the Russian border guard had handed over to the Chinese authorities for sentencing, they were still waiting for a Russian officer who was to attend the execution in an official capacity. Naturally, he kept everyone waiting. Meanwhile, one of the executioner's assistants stuffed wax into the ears of the honghuzi—"so that they would not hear the whir of the sword and flinch with their heads"! That the executioner had to strike precisely a certain spot on the vertebra with his blade was evident to me from the fact that another Chinese drew a line with red paint on the nape of each condemned man's neck, having first expertly [[p222]] palpated his spine with his index finger until he had found the right spot. The honghuzi seemed to recognise the expediency of this philanthropic precaution; today I laugh at it, but at the time an icy shudder ran through me whenever the condemned man indicated with an affirmative grin that the man with the red paint had found the right cartilage on the nape of the neck where the executioner's sword was to fall.

At last, the execution could begin. It seemed as though the condemned men had already attended such affairs. Without being asked, they knelt down in a row at wide intervals from one another; not a single expression, not a single gesture betrayed fear of impending death.

The executioner stepped up to the first; a barely perceptible movement of his wrist, and a thick, red jet of blood shot straight forward from the headless torso. It seemed to me as if it were still standing upright on its knees, and already the fat Chinese was at the second. Once more I saw the short sword flash upwards, saw again the red, horizontal jet stream over the head rolling on the ground, [[p223]] then disgust and horror, which I had hitherto steadfastly fought against, seized me. The steam of the warm blood took my breath away—I felt ashamed of my weakness, turned to leave, but after a few paces returned. Now I saw one of the assistants struggling to remove the iron shackles from the feet of the beheaded men. To be sure, he tried to open the irons on the first one with a key or some similar tool, but this did not seem to accomplish the task quickly enough. Thereupon he took a knife and whittled the foot off the carcass, much as one sharpens a pencil. Now, however, I had had enough. In vain I reasoned with my nerves that, as a bystander, I had no need to be more upset than those whose heads were being chopped off here. In vain I pointed out to them the cheerfully grinning faces of the other Chinese spectators—it was no use, they refused to function; I had to obey them and leave the ghastly place.

And so it will fare with us Europeans in war as well; we shall reach a certain limit where the strength of our will and of our physique will succumb to the weakness of our nerves—and indeed even more quickly than happened with the Russians, who are far better off regarding their nerves than the members of those armies in which nervousness is methodically nurtured.
