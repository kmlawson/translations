On the Russian side, it was only on this day that the Rifle Corps and a brigade of the 10th Army Corps were brought into action; Laobatai and Xiaotaizi were taken by them by storm, so that at this time almost all the places around Sandepu were in Russian hands. Instead of fixing the Japanese forces in Sandepu and energetically pressing the attack—already victoriously begun—further to the east with their own main force, the riflemen fell back to the north again in the evening!

The cardinal objective of Grippenberg's entire offensive—the surprise capture of the Japanese's most vital flank bastion—had been missed!

What had failed against a weak opponent in the first three days was now, after the latter had brought up strong reserves, all the less likely to succeed.

Since this book is concerned less with a meticulous record of all the engagements than with pointing out those psychological factors responsible for the perpetual Russian failures, a description of the further engagements that were fought on the western flank until [[p107]] 20 February is not essential here. In general, these combats resembled all those that had preceded them and all those that were to follow later: facing a uniformly directed Japanese attack stood isolated Russian corps, parcelled out by battalions and companies into village fighting, employed with neither temporal nor spatial unity.

The cold during these days was, to be sure, paralyzing to operations—yet this affected friend and foe alike, and did not prevent the Japanese from achieving fresh successes on this terrain that was already half lost.

The fighting surged back and forth until 2 February, promising victory now to one side, now to the other, and was finally broken off by the Russians on that day, allegedly because Adjutant General Kuropatkin refused to grant further reinforcements for the rifle brigades' repeated attacks on Lidarentun.

*To summarize*: Kuropatkin intends to attack with his three armies; he designates the right-wing army for the attack on Sandepu—the other two armies remain standing idle during this attack.

Grippenberg has three strong groups available for the attack on Sandepu. One attacks, after two days the second group attacks as well, while the third remains standing idle during this attack.—Finally, on the fourth day, the third group also intervenes vigorously and successfully, only to withdraw again the next day without exploiting its successes!

Under such circumstances, it was perhaps entirely justified on the part of the commander-in-chief to refuse the squandering of further forces, since their deployment would by then have come too late anyway.

Whether the blame for the defective attack dispositions of the Second Army lay with Grippenberg or with Kuropatkin, who was in the habit of meddling in the detailed orders of his subordinate commanders, opinions were divided.

Unanimously, however, Grippenberg's conduct in abandoning the [[p108]] army in such difficult circumstances was condemned by all those whom I had occasion to question on the matter.

On 4 February, the Russians stood roughly where they had stood before the battle began; around fifteen thousand men had fallen on their side—and frozen to death.
