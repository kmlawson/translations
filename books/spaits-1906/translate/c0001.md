## To Petersburg.

*November 1904.*

Would an able doctor be satisfied with his choice of profession if he were permitted all his life only to work at the dissecting table or to write prescriptions for "hypothetical" illnesses? An engineer who only draws plans, but never sees the product of his calculations rise from the ground? A composer who writes notes side by side according to all the rules of thoroughbass and counterpoint, without ever hearing the acoustic effect of his works? — No! — They, like all other creators, must see tangibly useful results emerge from the labour of their minds and hands; their diligence must translate into an immediately perceptible success, diminished by no criticism, so that they may believe in themselves and their fellow men may believe in them.

With an officer in peacetime, it is another matter. He must be the greatest idealist—and at the same time the greatest optimist—if he is not to lose faith in his work.

He works for a greater whole, in which the individual parts he has honed soon lose themselves again in the mass—he must blindly believe that the method and instruments by and with which he works are the best—for the only real touchstone, an actual conflict, he never gets to see. As a reward for his pains he has his *own inner conviction* and——*recognition*.

Kriegsspiel, tactical exercise rides, and Kriegsspiel once again. Here and there, however, the cannons have been roaring for years, now in the land of the Boers, now among the Chinese, a war here, an uprising in the colonies there, offering the officers of the country concerned a welcome opportunity to experience actual warfare and at the same time to cast a glance out into the wide [[p12]] world. — Yet we are permitted to read of all this only in the newspaper, and must quietly pull along at the old cart in the ever-same, rutted tracks, in which only now and then do the crushed stones laid in our path change their shape. What officer would not be struck by the thought of why he of all people should remain stuck in one place, why he of all people should not at least once see that for which he was trained—war? "Yes, my dear friend," people answered me, "if you were not an active-duty officer, the matter could probably be arranged more easily, but as it is, it will hardly do! — It sounds somewhat paradoxical, but it is true and quite natural. How do you actually imagine it? Do you want to march with the Russians as a Cossack, or enlist with the Japanese? Or do you believe that in a war one can simply go and watch, just as at a manoeuvre?"

I was still far from having settled these questions in my mind when, on a gloomy November day, I bought my ticket to Petersburg at the Vienna Nordbahnhof.

Now I was finally sitting in the carriage; passport, certificates, a stack of letters of introduction in my pocket—of the latter one can never have enough in Russia, so I was told. What work, what running about, what begging and pleading it had been! Having to endure the eternal head-shaking and shrugging of shoulders! Well, you will probably get as far as Petersburg, most had said to me by way of farewell—only a single, easily ignited journalist offered to administer my future laurels.
