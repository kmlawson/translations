A heavy iron gate, turning with a grinding creak on its rusted hinges, leads us through a low vaulted passage to the second courtyard; smaller than the first, this one lacks the dark green of trees; its paved floor, the high walls, and the three small temples facing us—all gleam in the grey silver shimmer of a kind of slate stone, which seems especially suited to the fine sculptural work with which the handsome edifices before us are covered from base to cornice.

The contours of the roofs here are much softer, more supple than those we usually find in Chinese architecture; the pillars supporting the cornice are more delicate, and the dragon heads yawning toward us from the plinth are rendered more artistically and with less of a grotesque grimace than usual.

Like jewel cases in a large chest, the three little temples stand here between the high, bare walls. Yet if we open the lid of such a casket, we find that the precious gems they were once built to protect are missing. Was it sacrilegious hands that had carried them off, [[p159]] or are those little heaps of yellow clay rubble covering the floor of the sanctuary their pitiful remains? The Chinese man cannot tell us; he merely points to a large, verdigris-coated cauldron, on the bottom of which lie thin brown votive candles—apparently the meagre offerings still made from time to time to the three bogeys sitting in the dark background with their hands and noses struck off. to the final courtyard, stands the "wall against evil spirits," such as we often find before the entrances to villages and houses. An ordinary brick wall, about five metres wide and two metres high, with Buddhist maxims. The gate to the third courtyard is barred with heavy padlocks. A Chinese man who has followed us from the entrance gate holds the key to one of them; he too demands his obol. His partner in the locking is a third temple guard, who will not draw the key [[p160]] to the second lock from his pocket until he too has received his baksheesh.

Does this extortion also belong to the primal culture of the Manchus, or did these good folk learn it only from us civilised Europeans?

The third courtyard is barely a few metres wide and unadorned; it resembles more a moat lying between the last wall and that high burial mound beneath which the great Manchu emperor rests—he with his three hundred wives, who had to follow him alive into the damp grave. At least so it is said to be written on the great stone tablet standing just opposite the courtyard gate, behind which the emperor's coffin stands, while the bones of the women, even in death, are supposedly to be found at a respectful distance from their ruler, beneath the centre of the tumulus.

A small piece of it can be seen in the left corner—and a pagoda, of which three are erected on each of the massive, broad courtyard walls, in the right corner—in that picture

Before I photographed the grave tablet with my Block-Notes, I had placed two Manchu boys, apparently sons of the temple watchmen, against the wall to embellish the picture. These rascals now clamoured, with shouts and gestures, for payment for their services as well, and would not be satisfied with the four-kopeck coins for which we had made them race. In the end their fathers arrived as well and joined in the howling of the brats. Oh, you dear Orientals, whether you are in Egypt, Turkey, Persia, or East Asia, as beggars and grubby rogues you are alike everywhere!... Before leaving, we mounted one of the courtyard walls. Convenient stairs lead up to the top, where a delightful scene presented itself to us. The sun was sinking toward the horizon, bathing with its final rays the reddish-yellow burial mound, which now seemed to glow in evening purple. The few bare little trees covering it stood out oversized against the sky, and between their wide-spreading branches, far behind across the plain, drifted small white puffs of smoke from puffing locomotives that ceaselessly brought fresh troops to the battlefield. In a wide arc, the path of the iron serpent curves around the consecrated ground so as not to disturb the sleep of the "great dragon" said to be buried deep beneath the temple here. The alignment of the railway near the imperial tombs had at the time to be determined in consultation with Chinese priests, who established with convincing certainty just how far the tail-tip of the monster, slumbering for thousands of years, extended, and how far away from here the modern steam horse, with its clatter and rumble, still had to veer so that the dragon, the emperor, and perhaps also his three hundred wives would not awaken any sooner than Buddha in his wisdom had foreordained.
