## The Engagements at Sandepu.

*Late January.*

“Our corps, the Siberians as well as the Eighth and the Rifles, fought bravely; but in the village of Sandepu itself there was a distillery with arm-thick walls that we could not master!”

These words, from the mouth of a Russian general staff colonel, sound somewhat incomprehensible—yet in fact it was so, and had good reason too.

Since its unsuccessful October offensive, the Russian Manchurian Army had stood north of and along the Shahe.

The right wing was formed by the Second Army under Grippenberg, the center by the Third Army under Kaulbars, and the left wing, which was pushed forward into the mountains in a southward hook-like curve, by the First Army under Linevich.

Whether Grippenberg’s relationship with Kuropatkin was already strained before Sandepu, I do not know. I will only state that in the autumn prior, Grippenberg had been designated to carry out an entirely independent task with the army group subordinated to him, and this force, already largely assembled in Jilin, was called the Jilin Army.

An independent operation by this army against the Japanese right flank seems to have been planned and might perhaps also have had prospects of success. Kuropatkin—as they say, in order to preserve the unity of supreme command, while others believe out of jealousy toward Grippenberg—drew him and his corps close to himself even before the October fighting.

In the unfortunate endeavor never to seek success in exploiting the opportune moment, but always [[p94]] again solely in relying on numerical strength, the commander-in-chief had allowed the entire winter to slip by in order to await the troop transports arriving from Europe.

“We must be twice as strong as the Japanese!” a Russian general staff lieutenant colonel, who had just returned from the headquarters of the army commander-in-chief, told us.—Does there not lie in these words, which were certainly an echo from on high, an admission of their own weakness?

To be sure, the Manchurian winter is little favorable for operations. Cold and lack of water significantly limit operational objectives. Here the operational objective lay quite close: the Japanese positions on the opposite bank of the Shahe, which at some points were no further than a thousand paces from our own outposts. It is no fable, but a fact, that Russian regimental bands played in the zemlyanka camps and the Japanese from across the way showed their approval with shouts of “khorosho.”

The climatic conditions at the beginning of the month of January (the fall of Port Arthur) had been no less favorable than at the end of it—yet if considerations of cold and lack of water were decisive for the choice of timing for the attack, then one ought not to have attacked even in February.

Although the time for resuming the offensive had already been postponed far enough, the eventual opening of action seems nevertheless to have corresponded more to Grippenberg’s urging than to the intention of the commander-in-chief. The entire design and execution of the attack points to this.

As in all previous Russian offensive attempts, here again we find the typical recipe for attack:

A wing group is assigned a task—the capture of an attack objective; the entire remainder of the army stands ready during the wing group’s fight—and waits. *Time, as has been said, has no value*, so the main body of the army can wait until the wing group has accomplished its task. Now the enemy, though numerically weak, is far more active than the Russians. He uses the time that [[p95]] the inactivity of his adversary affords him for rapid lateral shifts; he shifts his forces, *skillfully arrayed in depth*, to the threatened wing and suddenly confronts the partial attacks of the Russians here with relative superiority.

He can even dare to significantly weaken his center or the other wing, knowing full well that the Russians invariably fail to employ their numerical superiority—for the Russians were almost always numerically superior to the Japanese—*united and simultaneously*, to achieve a common objective. For this they lacked the proper sense for exploiting the given moment—and especially training in command technique, in which the most meticulous *calculation of time* is, in turn, the fundamental prerequisite.

Thus, here too, the attack on Sandepu appears to us as a purely partial undertaking by General Grippenberg, who was guided by the certainly commendable idea of putting an end to the long operational lull through a successful offensive by the Second Army, and thereby at the same time tying the hoped-for turn in Russian fortunes of war to his own name.

Such an attack, even if somewhat belated in terms of timing, suited the geographical conditions: attacking the Japanese frontally, that is to say a strategic breakthrough, had no prospect of success; the *main attack* had to be directed against one of the two Japanese flanks. The eastern wing stood in the mountains, where a decision was more difficult to bring about and where the Russians, coming from their homeland, were not accustomed to fighting. Thus, the favorable direction of attack for the *opening engagement* of the great general offensive remained that against Sandepu.
