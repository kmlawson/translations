Morale and endurance, indispensable though they are, do not suffice in the field when military training is lacking, and in the Russian army this stood at a low level. Above all, marksmanship training was completely inadequate. The Transbaikal Cossacks, the Don, Ural, and Orenburg Cossacks, and at times also the Caucasian foot Cossacks were excellent marksmen, since like the Boers they are peoples of hunters. The Russian infantry as a whole, however, shot poorly. The soldier is uneducated; he can scarcely read the numbers on the rear sight of his rifle, which [[p246]] in his clumsy hand is far too complicated a machine; I have met men who did not know what the rear sight notch and front sight on the barrel were for!¹) That the Russian is an uncomprehending rider and a poor horse groom, and that it is only thanks to the excellent [[p247]] and hardened horse stock that the good beasts held out to the end, has been discussed in greater detail elsewhere.

How difficult it must have been, given the generally low level of education, to obtain suitable personnel for the technical troops is clear enough. The artillery, with regard to both officers and enlisted men, is the elite force. I also encountered very skilled and resourceful men in the border guard. The Russian's slight sense of order reveals itself first of all in the fact that he cannot accustom himself to march discipline in our sense. Every detachment marches with stragglers, yet all reach their destination.

Just as with order, the Russian also has little sense of thorough cleanliness. His clothing, including that of the officers, was often greasy and stained; a burst seam or even just a missing button escapes his notice.

A bad trait of Russian soldiers is their penchant for begging. At all the railway stations, I was begged from by loitering soldiers. My travelling companion on [[p248]] the outward journey, Engineer Sch., told me that he had given alms to a beggar in Omsk. When a passing officer saw this, he called out to Sch. that he had been wounded at Liaoyang, pointed to his Cross of St. George, and likewise asked for alms! When I rode through Tieling during the retreat from Mukden, I was beset by whole swarms of begging soldiers. On the return journey, the railway trains on those stretches where bridges or other installations had to be crossed were manned by Landsturm men (*druzhina*). They stood with fixed bayonets in the corridors and at the door of the dining car, and had to ensure that none of the fellow passengers dropped a bomb onto the tracks. These men, too, begged almost without exception. At first they would usually ask for a newspaper. "What am I to do with the newspaper now, sir? After all, I can't read; give me ten kopecks instead!" was then the second request.
