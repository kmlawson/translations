Since in spring the army was tied to its supplies and to reinforcements from Europe—from which, moreover, one could not expect too much—its only further line of retreat remained the one leading west from Harbin along the railway, where it might perhaps find better chances for resistance at a *second Sungari position* than south of Harbin.

Despite some faint-hearted talk that could be heard especially from some Jewish physicians and other non-combatants, the *confidence of the Russian* [[p360]] *army on the whole was still not lost even after the Battle of Mukden!* “The war must go on for another two years, and then we will surely be victorious!” Thus spoke people who had a grueling, bloody seventeen-month campaign behind them! That is precisely the advantageous reverse side of the tedious phlegm of these people—that they *never despair!*

The losses of the Russian-Manchurian army in the period from 26 February to mid-March were in round numbers: 60,000 wounded, 20,000 dead, 45,000 captured. Far more significant than the Russian reports indicated must have been the losses in field guns, whose empty teams we saw by the hundreds in Harbin; the loss in ammunition and war materiel, including the installed field railway and bridge material, was approximately one billion rubles, though it was less the actual monetary value than the *temporary irreplaceability* of what was lost that was keenly felt. Besides various regimental dressing stations, the St. George Hospital remained behind in Mukden with approximately 3,000 wounded. The doctors, medical orderlies, and Red Cross sisters were sent back under escort by the Japanese to the Russian outpost lines.

It would be ungrateful to forget in this book a fellow campaigner who endured so much and so patiently during these hard days, who did so infinitely much good and alleviated so much pain—the Russian woman, who bravely and unpretentiously stood by her brothers in this war.

## Woman in War.

Whoever has never understood in woman that noble, self-sacrificing love which is her unique, innate domain wherein she surpasses us men, has surely never seen woman in the simple dress of the Samaritan, in the hospital, on the battlefield. Now by the stretcher, now at the operating table, but always with only pain and misery before her eyes, the “Sister of Mercy” performs her arduous service; no other reward awaits her than the consciousness of having alleviated the suffering of her charges.

Here in the war, it was perhaps once again the racial trait of the Russian woman which, when placed in the service of voluntary care for the wounded, proved worthy of *unreserved* admiration. Not that women of other nations would lack similar self-sacrificing courage; yet I doubt that elsewhere, *from all strata* of the female population—from the poor student up to the pampered princess—such a relatively *large number* would have submitted to this calling full of privation and endured it *over the long duration of the war with such unflinching, phlegmatic devotion* as we found in the operational and rear-echelon areas of the Russian army.

The whole war was certainly never popular, to be sure, and it was hard to demand that “patriotism” be inscribed on the banners of a military campaign whose vague objective was the preservation of state prestige in a country that to the great masses was initially unknown and later detested. But that patriotism—and indeed a great deal of patriotism—dwells in the Russian people was amply proven by the Russian woman in this war. Like the soldier, she too went without sleep, food, and [[p362]] shelter without complaint; yet she, the woman, suffered want and endured hardships *gladly and with self-sacrifice*, for she dedicated her labor to the best part of the fatherland: its sons! The soldiers, however, did not know why and for what they had to fight and die!

Of what the love of women back home in Russia created for the wounded and sick, I have spoken at the beginning. Later on the journey, I saw what hardships and trials the young girls who had dedicated themselves to hospital service had to endure. A hospital train brings two hundred or more sick from the Harbin hospitals back to Chita. Let us assume that this is not one of those comfortable trains fitted out specially for transporting the wounded, such as had been donated by the generosity of certain grand duchesses, charitable societies, or cities, but rather a hospital train improvised from ordinary freight cars. The journey from Harbin to Chita takes around ten days. This time the nurse spends virtually without interruption among the sick; so few places to rest are allocated to the sisters that they can only be used by turns.

Meals are irregular, depending on when one pulls into the feeding stations for the sick. At last Chita is reached; the hospitals there are already overcrowded. Together with the accompanying doctors, the sisters hand over their patients, and then the hospital train ceases to be one and returns as a freight or troop transport. “By which train will we return home to our hospital in Harbin?” doctors and sisters ask. “Go to the station and wait!” is the curt answer. Waiting! And waiting yet again!
