Toward evening, the fire slackened somewhat on both sides before Sandepu; the attacker took advantage of this lull in the firing to push forward and finally penetrate into the village, whose northern and northwestern parts were occupied by the Russians. Darkness as well as exhaustion put an end to the fighting that night. It was mostly troops of the Eighth Corps who had penetrated into the village.

[[p103]]

The rolling field kitchens had even caught up with some companies, so that warm meals could be cooked here, almost chest to chest with the enemy. Where this was not possible, his old friend the *chaynik* once again provided the Russian with a cup of hot tea, which helped him endure the dread cold until the following morning.

That evening, news of victory in the fall of Sandepu spread through the army and, with the swiftness of long-awaited glad tidings, made its way back to Russia that very night. Unfortunately, the joy was premature.

In the early morning hours of the 27th, the struggle in the narrow lanes of Sandepu began anew. The Russians attempted to advance toward the center of the village from the north and west, but in doing so encountered resistance that could not be overcome either by rifle fire or bayonet charges—a row of buildings that, connected with one another by wire and other obstacles, were heavily manned by the Japanese and prepared for defense. The redoubt in this defensive line was formed by the *chin distillery*, whose broad fluted stone walls and flanking towers, looming over the surrounding houses, enabled the Japanese to bring fire upon the Russian reserves—if one could speak of such at all in the confusion of battle—while their leading ranks dashed themselves to pieces at the foot of the wall.

And where was the Russian artillery? No reproach can be made against it here. Almost the entire Eighth Army Corps, battalion after battalion, had entered the village; Russians and Japanese were fighting in all the alleyways; every shot would have struck friend and foe alike.

Later, during the Battle of Mukden, when I had the opportunity while with the Second Siberian Corps to observe the precise fire of the 2nd East Siberian Mortar Battery, I was reminded once again of this struggle. A few well-aimed shots from a *mortar* or *howitzer battery* would even then have still sufficed to clear the aforementioned obstacles out of the way, since they had already failed to prepare the [[p104]] [Illustration: Sketch map of the actions at Sandepu.]

Thus once again all the strength and all the courage of the Russians were in vain. The successes of the preceding two days had instilled confidence in the bayonet attack among both men and officers. General Kondratovich led his troops forward to the assault at the head of the 1st East Siberian Rifle Division (First Siberian Corps). The general and almost all the officers of the lead battalions fell; more than fifteen hundred men covered the lanes of Sandepu as dead and wounded.

The Japanese received reinforcements in the course of the 27th—the Russians had, after all, allowed them two days' time to bring them up.

The *Rifle Corps* near Santaizi and the *Tenth Army Corps, held back as army reserve, did not enter the engagement on this day*, nor did the elements of the Central Group advancing along the right bank of the Hun River, which had been deployed in an excellent direction of attack threatening the Japanese rear (Sumapu and Niuju), but were probably for their part *waiting* for the fall of Sandepu instead of actively cooperating in taking it.

Only when General Mishchenko’s turning column made itself felt around noon on the 27th from the south against the Japanese left flank, threatening Sandepu itself from the south, did Sumapu fall into Russian hands.

This *indirect assistance, however, came too late*; by that time, Sandepu had already been evacuated by the Russians.

*Adjutant General Kuropatkin*, following the fighting of the Second Army, had likely come to the conviction, in view of the terrible losses that the two days of fighting for the village of Sandepu alone had cost, that further bloodshed, further throwing-in of individual battalions piecemeal, as had now become the fashion, would be futile. [[p106]] On the evening of the 27th, the commander-in-chief—contrary, so it was said, to the representations and entreaties of the commander of the Second Army—issued the order to evacuate Sandepu, which took place during the night of January 27–28.

By that day, however, the Japanese had brought up and closed up their reserves, and now went over to the offensive themselves.

Their most dangerous opponent was General Mishchenko’s group threatening their rear, which was thrown back near Landonggou with considerable losses.

By around noon on the 28th, those Russian forces that had already gained ground southwest of Sandepu were also driven back toward and across the Hun River, where elements of the First and Fifth Siberian Corps held Geijiao and Heigoutai.
