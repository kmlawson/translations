From the dressing station to the redoubt, one could still cover a good distance in complete safety. The howling high above was now drowned out by the nearby thunder of the cannons, mingled with the sharp crackle of the skirmish line. The infantry still lay far from the batteries, but the icy air stood above the earth like a mighty pillar of crystal, vibrating every sound with metallic clarity into the distance. Here it was far colder than down in Pajiazi; had the artillery sergeant known what a thermometer was, he would have estimated the cold at twenty-five degrees. It would not have made him any warmer, and his thoughts remained single-mindedly fixed on “endure and wait—God will help!” He now began to mutter these words incessantly to himself—he needed them. Tch—tch—striking now to the right, now to the left of him, then again the whirring hum of a ricochet, and the bursting of the shrapnel above the batteries could now also be heard. Vasily

[Illustration: Bayonet thrust.] [[p244]] Stepanovich crawled on all fours. He could barely make headway; the heavy clothes hindered his movement. To take off his heavy fur coat, or at least unbutton it, in order to get out of the range of the incoming bullets more quickly and reach the cover of the redoubt—such a thought never entered his head. Cold hurts, and only a warm fur coat protects against it—so he had always heard and seen. Walking fast or running, for that matter, was not his style—he preferred to hold out slowly over this last stretch. What if he were hit by a bullet here? Left lying helpless in the dark night to freeze to death! He began to pray aloud and crept slowly forward on all fours. “And to think I hoped,” ran through his head, “that down there at the general’s they’d give you a small glass of vodka—and the little brothers probably think I’m bringing back a whole flask; but there was nothing, absolutely nothing to be had down there anymore—and there isn't even snow in this cursed land to rub your nose with when it begins to freeze.” — Then he began to sing a hymn aloud.

The colonel was sitting in the gorge shelter of the redoubt. His face was bandaged, only his left eye looking out between the white linens. Shot after shot struck the fortification, but it had so far been spared from the heavy shimose shells. The Japanese attacking batteries had changed their positions during the night, so that the Russians could determine the approximate direction from the flashes of fire, but could not judge the effect of their shots in the darkness. Zeroing in on the enemy's possible positions while it was still daylight was something no one had thought of. Thus, fire on the Russian redoubt had been ceased—being futile; officers and men sat in the covered dugouts and let the bullets patter above their heads into the protective frozen layer of earth. Most of the men were asleep. Vasily Stepanovich stepped into the dugout. His bashlyk was frozen to his beard; mouth and nose were covered with ice. He was gasping for breath, for the march on all fours had not agreed with him. He took the shaggy papakha from his head and walked toward the corner of the dugout, where a small icon of the Virgin Mary was wedged between massive wooden posts [[p245]]—before it burned a little red lamp. It was the only light in the dugout. Firewood had long since ceased to exist here, only dried dung for boiling tea.

The artillery sergeant bowed to the ground before the icon of the Virgin Mary, crossed himself three times, then went to his colonel and reported what General Schwerin had ordered him to report. “Very well, we shall wait. Now go, Vasily, to the forward works as well and tell our people there. Hold out until tomorrow, then God will provide further help.” This time Vasily took a man with him who guided him to the batteries on the sopkas located farther forward—whether he reached them, I do not know. In the following days, these positions too were taken by the Japanese, and the Third Corps retreated toward Shihuicheng.

Yet these brave people did hold out and wait until they were ordered to retreat. Why God did not help them is another question entirely!

We fast-paced Europeans find it hard to enter into such ways of thinking. We lack the vast, flat, monotonous milieu in which these children of the steppe grow up, the endless snowfields in which space loses its value and whose barrenness would scarcely make it worthwhile to utilize the long summer day, let alone the few daylight hours in winter, for any intensive labor. These are the geographic factors acting upon the spirit of the man; alongside them, we lack the naive outlook of the Russian peasant, cultivated through the deliberate stultification of the people, who imagines the source of all that exists and all that is to come to lie in the omnipotence of God and the Tsar.
