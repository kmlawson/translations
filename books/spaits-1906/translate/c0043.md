Now that we have become acquainted with all the gentlemen who at that time represented their respective military powers in the theatre of war, let us take a look at their daily routine. It should be briefly mentioned that the two Spanish attachés had already departed by then, as had two of the American attachés. For of the latter—much to the annoyance of Germany, which had sent only three representatives—four had appeared at the beginning of the campaign. A Danish cavalry captain was also still expected—to the horror of Baron Tettau, who saw in this an intimation that the campaign would drag on even longer.

The foreign officers had already become Russianised to such an extent that rising was generally begun very late. Since the waiters in the dining car also did not begin laying the tables until around 8 a.m., any premature diligence would have been entirely out of place. Breakfast consisted of tea or coffee with butter and eggs, all exquisite and good, only the Caucasian waiters were usually still unwashed at this hour. The first glance of every morning guest upon entering was directed at the thermometer, which was maliciously graduated in Fahrenheit, so that the day began at once with a difficult conversion to Réaumur. My skill at mental arithmetic aroused great admiration—until it was discovered that in the second compartment of the dining car, behind a window curtain, hung a Réaumur thermometer that spared me all the division and addition.

During breakfast, the *Mandzhurskiy vestnik* [*Manchurian Herald*] was read, a kind of bulletin on the military events of the preceding day. It reported entirely insignificant matters, and even these were rarely true. The morning was devoted to drafting reports, riding out to the nearest corps, and the like; at half past twelve everyone gathered again for lunch. Particularly plentiful and appetizing [[p152]] were the *zakuskis* served on the first two tables on either side of the entrance door. Even the most pampered palate found satisfaction here. The cuisine was excellent and offered ample variety. How pleasant it was to chat here at the well-appointed table over a bottle of red Crimean wine, which was served in abundance, while outside the windows the most colourful military life surged to and fro across the large station square. While we comfortably sipped our coffee over a green Manila cigar, long columns of wounded moved past outside toward the waiting hospital train, ammunition columns rattled past the dining car, then again a detachment of newly arrived reservists stacked their arms in pyramids right in front of our train and began cooking their rations. Regularly toward the end of luncheon, Chinese jugglers appeared before the carriage. A monkey had to ride on a goat, which seemed to provide the greatest amusement to the simple minds of the soldiers standing around.

At times, the cheerful flow of talk would fall silent, and everyone looked somberly at the sorrowful procession passing slowly by the windows—a funeral, simple and plain, as befits a soldier in wartime.

It would be difficult to say which language deserved pride of place in this Tower of Babel; the French, English, and German groups were numerically roughly equally represented. French was familiar to all the foreign officers, German to almost all, while there were many who did not understand English. Apart from Camperio, only the two South Americans spoke Italian. Flawless Russian was spoken only by the representatives of Austria-Hungary, Sweden and Norway, and Captain Boucé, yet even among the rest there were only few who did not at least understand the language sufficiently.

The art of photography counted many adherents among the attachés. Baron Tettau, Macomb, Rostagno, Follenfant, Camperio, and above all Captain Boucé, were inseparable from their Kodaks—rewarding subjects presented themselves at every turn, though amidst such abundance many a thing worthy of a photograph was passed over in order to save one's plates for something even better. And when that something better did come—during the Battle of Mukden—it happened to many of them as it did to me: plates or films developed at night and still wet had to be abandoned during a sudden early morning departure.

On that evening, when I returned from Huangshan, all the naval attachés from Vladivostok had unexpectedly arrived in Mukden. What did the naval officers want here in the Hunhe plain? At first, the purpose of their arrival was shrouded in deep secrecy; they were to be received by Kuropatkin the following day. Soon we learned the reason for their presence. One among them, who wore a handsome blond pointed beard, had courted the favour of a lady in Vladivostok—and, so it was said, with success. But this lady was the mistress of the fortress commandant or—I do not recall exactly—of his chief of staff. Now jealousy is a wicked passion, to cure which the fortress commandant found it most expedient to send all the naval attachés home! By order of Kuropatkin, they returned to Vladivostok after a few days.
