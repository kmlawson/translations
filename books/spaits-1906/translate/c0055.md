The Japanese were indeed driven back by reinforcements that had meanwhile arrived from Gongzhuling, but on 14 February the pursuing Russians encountered significant Japanese forces some 20 versts *northeast* of Gongzhuling—that is, *directly in the rear of the Russian operational army*—and were beaten back with severe losses, the Japanese also carrying off a gun.”

“How strong might the Japanese have been?” “*On that point*,” said Colonel Dragomirov, “we are *completely* in the dark, for the reports received on the matter contradict one another. I believe that the Japanese were not nearly so strong, and that only the commander of the Harbin district, whose ambition strives for a larger sphere of activity, is painting the matter so black so that more troops will be placed under his command. To be sure, the bridge was already repaired the following day, but, as you see, this Japanese cavalry feat has bad indirect consequences for us. Not only have we been standing here idle for months, but now, just when things are finally supposed to move forward, we must pull troops from the front and send them north; how, then, is confidence in victory ever to awaken in our men!”

The good fellows, however, seemed not to occupy themselves with such grave thoughts at all. On an open flatcar before us, between loaded carts and crates, a platoon of a sotnia had just made itself as comfortable as possible. The night promised to be cold, and for this airy journey the soldiers had nothing more than their warm fur coats. Yet already the kettle was going round, and one of them had brought out his accordion and was playing a little Cossack song, to which the others beat time with their heavy boots dangling down the sides of the flatcar. The train started up, a hearty hurrah roared through the cold air—scarcely did they know in which direction of the compass they were traveling. A blessing for you, Russian generals, that you have such good soldiers! Thus good core troops moved to the rear from the front, [[p196]] perhaps merely to indulge the vanity of a general, and to gloss over the matter, figures and events are inflated or minimized at will. But that is possible only where the ground is paved for *falsehood* by a system of unaccountability, where the *servants of a corrupt bureaucratic regime* crush the people’s striving to know the truth.

Thus it is that through this dense web of lies even the commander-in-chief cannot see what is happening behind his back, and allows himself to be led into measures that actual conditions would never have warranted.

Yet in *military history* one often hears little of such things; that is then “constructed” after the fact at the conference table—or much like here in the railway carriage, while the campaign is still under way!
