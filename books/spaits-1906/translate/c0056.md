### The Russian Plan to Resume the Offensive.

The news that General Rennenkampf had assumed command of the cavalry corps on the Russian right wing had an invigorating effect. That Rennenkampf would do his job better than Mishchenko was certain.

As could be gathered from the overall situation, *General Rennenkampf had the task of using his cavalry to harass the Japanese rear in the direction of Liaoyang, while Adjutant General Kuropatkin, similarly to late January, concentrated strong forces on the western wing in order to open the general offensive once again with an attack on Sandepu.* Depending on the progressing successes of the Second Army, the command of which had been taken over by General of Cavalry Baron Kaulbars following the departure of General Grippenberg, the Third Army—General Bilderling—and the First Army—General Linevich—were then to take the offensive.

Guiding idea for the attack: *Rolling up the Japanese front from the western wing.*

In the past October, Kuropatkin had announced by means of an army order—which was reproduced in all the newspapers of the world—that he felt strong enough to attack the enemy—and throw him back. Such an announcement of one's own operational intentions—one might almost say in the manner of an advertisement—which, besides its inexpediency, cannot be spared the accusation of boastfulness, was certainly something entirely new in the realm of command procedure and also had its ill consequences in every direction.

This time all plans were kept strictly secret, and only the sharp reconnaissances already begun by Rennenkampf against the Japanese left flank, as well as heavy troop movements toward our own right wing, pointed to the eventual resumption of the Russian offensive.

On the Russian side, as we learned from a reliable source, it would have been preferred had the Japanese attacked, and this waiting also seemed to correspond to such an expectation. "If only one could put it into the Japanese' heads to attack us now," Lieutenant Colonel Bazarov had also said once on returning home from army headquarters.

Despite all the secrecy, however, it was in the air that we stood at the threshold of great events. A great battle makes itself felt in the atmosphere like an approaching thunderstorm, only we did not know from which quarter the first lightning bolt would strike. Where should I go now in order to be an eyewitness to the expected great events? An individual observer must not imagine that in a great battle he will gain any kind of overview of the whole—for this, even the vast apparatus of the army commander, equipped with all the resources of the reporting service and modern technology, is not always sufficient.

The threads connecting the commander with his troops and keeping him informed of their combat situation converge farther behind the battle line the wider that line is. Back there, the battlefield spectator would hear nothing but the roar of the guns, see nothing but the coming and going of ammunition columns and transport of the wounded, the deployment of army reserves, the establishment of dressing stations [[p200]] and the like. The psychology of war and the sight of combat remain unknown to him back there.

Yet even he who ventures farther forward should avoid the plain! Here the eerie whistling of bullets and the crash of bursting shells will force him to turn back before he has even caught sight of the reserves of the engaged troops. Whoever has the opportunity should try to take to the hills. The observer is hardly bulletproof there either, but the opportunity is offered to follow the course of an action under cover behind a knoll, in a gully, or elsewhere.

On the plain, moreover, a thaw was in sight, during which one could sink into the soft loam of the fields. Thus I preferred to return first to Huangshan to await the further development of events there.

In Huangshan things had become lively toward the end of February; in place of the customary noon shots, the guns boomed over into our fanza from morning until evening. During the night, rifle fire could occasionally be heard from the nearest positions. Three Japanese columns, it was said, were marching against the left wing and had attacked the Eastern Group of General Alekseyev. The news issued every morning by the General Staff section on a lithographed sheet was quite meager; mostly it ran: village A or sopka B was fought over throughout the night and the following day, changing hands repeatedly. The last hand into which it passed was usually that of the Japanese. Several times I saw General *Linevich*.

A good, worthy old gentleman in whom the soldiers had confidence. "The Japanese, they are wild Asiatics," he had once said after dinner; "we must take them by the scruff of the neck and show them civilization!" "Well, hardly civilization; they already have that!" one of his generals remarked modestly. "Fine; power, then, is what we must show them—our power!"
