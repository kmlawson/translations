And now, if one imagines this slowness, unpunctuality, and lack of energy, inoculated into the people by all the instruments of state power, *transferred to the military sphere*, one finds these hereditary evils of the Russian army clearly illustrated once again in every phase of the recent war, in every single operation from a battle right down to the undertaking of a raiding detachment. While fully acknowledging the difficulties rooted in the vastness of the distance and the inadequacy of communications, Russia cannot, [[p373]] this time as in all its earlier wars, be spared the reproach of having prepared its strategic deployment insufficiently and carried it out slowly.

Despite the numerical superiority finally achieved after several months, the *initiative of attack*—setting aside the ill-fated attempts on the Sha Ho and at Sandepu—was left exclusively to the Japanese. The *value of time* was never appreciated on the Russian side; that is to say, the moment for a promising offensive was consistently missed. Thus, after the fall of Port Arthur, Kuropatkin's army stood idle on the Sha Ho and tolerated the Japanese forces released in the south joining the field army. Further inactivity: roughly three hundred and eighty Russian battalions stood motionless opposite an adversary now reinforced to some two hundred and sixty-odd battalions, and had the Japanese not attacked at Mukden, the Russians, after their initial failure, would once again have halted the attack and quietly awaited the onset of the thaw, which would have prevented operations for months to come. In the execution of the operations themselves, the stereotypical Russian *unpunctuality* reveals itself.

On the 4th and 5th of March, dispositions were issued in the Second Manchurian Army west of Mukden, none of which was carried out. The orders arrived too late or not at all, were only partially obeyed or—having arrived too late—no longer obeyed at all; under the pressure of the enemy, the general unpunctuality had soon led to chaos in which keeping track of the troops was impossible. Formations were broken up and units taken wherever they happened to be at hand; thus it happened that on March 4th General Tserpitsky, commander of the 10th Army Corps, found himself entirely without troops, as now army headquarters, now the high command had assigned them in the most diverse directions. And during these long fourteen days, Linevich stood in the mountains with eighty-four battalions and quietly waited until the centre and the right wing in the plain were beaten! To concentrate this considerable force and commit it with an energetic thrust to extricate the Second and Third Armies [[p374]]—for this, he, like most of the others, lacked the energy and a proper understanding of the value of time.

Yet the gigantic dimensions of the Russian Empire and the resulting lesser sensitivity to the loss of space also give Russia's wars a peculiar character. In the Russo-Japanese War, there is the further circumstance, virtually unprecedented in military history, that throughout its entire duration the campaign took place in the territory of a third party, which placidly tolerated the devastation of its lands and the destruction of its villages—a fact that only further tempted the Russians to portray their forced retreats as voluntary shifts of position to the north.

The moral impression that the fall of Port Arthur made on the Russian army at Mukden was extremely slight; for a day or two it was talked about, then the loss of the fortress was gotten over just as easily as the entire territory conquered by the Japanese, in which the Russians had already made themselves completely at home for years and where millions of rubles had been invested in railway construction, barracks, hospitals, fortifications, and the like. *The consciousness that it was still not Russian soil from which they were being driven produced a feeling of indifference and insensitivity to the value of the terrain* on which they were fighting, which unfavourably influenced the will to offer energetic resistance. Thus, during the days from the 26th to the 28th of February—that is, just after the first serious Japanese attacks against the positions south of Mukden—the Russians immediately sent back to Harbin those three hundred fortress guns which had been placed with great difficulty in the centre of the Sha Ho position and had formed a formidable defensive front there. “The Japanese can have the Sha Ho, but they won't get our cannons!”

Thus it was also possible that, in the face of all the misery presented by the defeated army surging back northward from Mukden, a Russian General Staff colonel explained to us in the most convinced tone that “Mukden had been evacuated voluntarily and they were now returning to the north because southern Manchuria, sucked dry by the war, had no value left anyway!” And the good [[p375]] man believed it himself; he said it with the same self-assurance with which the Tsar, for instance, in his telegram of thanks to General Linevich, spoke of a “stubborn repulse of all the enemy's attacks”: *Tenete ridere*—but that is how it is; people truly believe in such things.
