Just behind the dining car lived Major Baron Tettau of the Great General Staff. His adjoining compartment was mostly empty; it belonged to Major von Runkel, who lived out at the front line. The German staff surgeon Dr. Schäfer also came in only rarely from Gongzhuling, where as head of a Red Cross hospital he was certainly best able to perform his duties while also doing useful work. Major Tettau had chosen the Tenth Army Corps, [[p146]] as mentioned in the previous chapter, as his corps of assignment, whose headquarters lay fairly close to Mukden. The next large compartment belonged to the French staff surgeon Follenfant, who later also had to take in his American colleague, Senior Staff Surgeon Howard—incidentally also a Frenchman. Follenfant had already spent a part of his life in the colonies and in the war against the Chinese in Tonkin, just as the other attachés had already travelled far and wide across the world before this war and had taken part in many of those great events that become known to us humble petty bourgeois in Austria and Hungary only by hearsay.

All those states committed a grave sin of omission that, out of petty stinginess or failing to recognise the equal importance of the military medical service, sent no medical officers to the theatre of war. Among all military representatives in this war, the foreign military surgeons certainly found the broadest field open for positive observations. The physician, lending a hand himself, was able to enrich his experience; in doing so, he stood closer to his profession than the tactical observer and, owing to the directly beneficial use of his skills, was welcomed everywhere. Even Bulgaria, indeed even China, in proper recognition of this necessity, had each sent a doctor to the theatre of war, who were assigned to hospitals in the lines-of-communication area.

The Americans, Artillery Major Montgomery Meigs Macomb and the engineer Captain Judson, had also been assigned a large compartment, but lived permanently in the house of a Chinese general (?) in Mukden, quite far from the carriage, which they had rented together with the two American correspondents and where they also ran their own joint kitchen. The next half-compartment was occupied by a very jovial gentleman whose perpetually cheerful mood and lively jokes had an invigorating effect on the entire company: Camperio, naval lieutenant and “Head of the Royal Italian Mission by Sea and by Land”, as he had playfully written on his visiting card. His headquarters was that of the Third Siberian Corps, where the ever-critical, yet ever-cheerful man was known to and liked by [[p147]] everyone. At the beginning of the campaign he had been in Vladivostok, from where, after the blockade of that fortress, he had joined the field army at Liaoyang. He had quickly settled into the role of a general staff officer, and his remarks revealed a keen power of military observation. He enjoyed the privilege of being allowed to say the bluntest things to the Russians’ faces. “Vous êtes un marin, vous ne comprenez rien!” the Spanish military attaché had once told him, and ever since he loved to quote this saying in excuse of his often quite sarcastic assertions. With his mighty black beard he was an imposing figure and, like all naval men, a passionate rider, always keeping five to six horses in camp as well. An equally characteristic figure was his faithful Chinese servant Balilla, who cooked us many a genuine Italian risotto. A solemn pair occupied the last compartment: Norway and Sweden, peacefully united, while the countries in question in Europe were already at each other’s throats. Captain Edlund knew better than anyone how to handle the Russians. Calm, composed, accustomed from his homeland to strong alcohol, speaking Russian fluently like his mother-

[Illustration: In front of the Mukden railway station.] [[p148]] tongue, he possessed the absolute trust of the Russian officers. Edlund always knew everything several days earlier than the other attachés, with whom, moreover, he always most willingly shared what he had learned.

The carriages resembled, especially at times preceding the opening of major operations, an intelligence bureau in which confidential news was exchanged from compartment to compartment. Edlund’s roommate was the Norwegian Captain Nyquist, the source for the reports on Mishchenko’s raid, which he had taken part in.

While the mood in this carriage was always a cheerful, exuberant one—the mere fact that Camperio lived here would have been sufficient for that—Carriage No. 203 maintained the seriousness prescribed for it by the dignity of its residents—all heads of missions.
