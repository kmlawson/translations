It is a pity that I never engaged in comparative studies of religious customs, for here there was much that recalled our own Christian worship. Above all, there also seems to be a divine trinity in the religion of the lamas; the frequent occurrence of three gods, [[p190]] which always stand together in similar form in the temples, suggests as much. Processions with banners, just as with us; similarly to our bishops, the chief priest here also wears a golden tiara, and when, at the conclusion of his prayers, he turns toward the crowd, it almost seems as though he wished to bestow a blessing upon them with the sign of the cross.

Yet the entire ceremony is neither devout nor uplifting; everyone shouts, laughs, jostles, and when, during the prayer of the priests, four dancers disguised in animal masks accompany their wild leaps before the altar with the deafening din of metal cymbals, the merriment of the spectators reaches its climax. Carried along in the dense stream of people, we then proceed to another, smaller temple, from whose gate a line of young girls comes tripping out, all in blue silk, with yellow flowers in their hair and on their garments.

How gladly I would have stayed here longer to delight in this vivid scene, had I not wanted to head back out to the positions in the afternoon, where the guns had been more audible since yesterday than before.

Colonel Pestić had told me to report to the censorship office that afternoon, where the papers were to be handed over to me that were to serve as my safe-conduct to the Second Chita Cossack Regiment. That I was to see Mukden for the last time that day, I certainly would not have thought. Thus, at least, a cheerful, warm picture concluded my memories of that city, which for so long had been the destination of my desire to travel.

## Preliminaries to the Battle.

### The Successful Japanese Raid on Gongzhuling.

One morning, shortly before I rode away from Mukden for the last time, the entire large square in front of my railcar window down to the post office building was occupied by troops. They were battalions that had returned to Mukden from the positions. What was this supposed to mean? No one knew. At noon, as we sat at lunch, the Don Cossack Brigade crossed the square, likewise heading north. Among the Cossack regiments, those from the Don are perhaps the best. They have noble, medium-sized horses and, like the Orenburg Cossacks, carry long lances. Again, people asked why so many troops were moving north—shrugs and silence were the answer; even Captain Edlund, who usually knew everything, returned from his corps toward evening without news. It was quite remarkable how little one learned in this war of what had occurred in one's immediate vicinity. Those who possessed official reports wrapped themselves in profound silence, and the other sources, especially the reports of the *Mandzhursky vestnik* [*Manchurian Herald*] or the Harbin newspapers, were so full of lies that one no longer believed them at all.

So one lived in the midst of war and could know only what one saw with one's own eyes.

The next day, the infantry battalions and the Cossacks were dispatched to the north, and a sotnia of border guards stood in front of the station ready to entrain. Only now did one learn, slowly and vaguely, that something unpleasant had happened to the Russians in the rear near Gongzhuling and that [[p194]] measures also had to be taken for the better security of Vladivostok.

On this occasion I was able for the first time to observe that unfortunate mania of the Russian army high command, with which it endeavored during the later events as well to create conglomerates of new formations. This time it was a *composite division*—pieced together battalion by battalion from various corps—which was sent back to Nikolsk to reinforce the Vladivostok district, and a composite brigade put together company by company—the Russians called it the *Harlequin Brigade*—which went back to Gongzhuling. “This move to the north means peace,” said Lieutenant Colonel Csicserics, who had come down from Huangshan in the course of the day and likewise knew only in general terms of a Japanese success at Fanjiatun.
