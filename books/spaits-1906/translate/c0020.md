### Forces.

The *forces subordinated* to Adjutant General Mishchenko consisted of:

Transbaikal-Ural Cossack Division under the personal command of the Adjutant General; deputy, General Abramov: 4 regiments.

Tomsk Cossack Division under General Teleshov: 3 regiments.

Dragoon Brigade, assembled from the 10th and 17th Army Corps ¹), under General Samsonov: 3 regiments. ²)

Caucasian Cossack Brigade under Colonel Prince Obeliani: 2 regiments.

A mounted sapper detachment (2 officers, 30 troopers).

::: notes
¹) *Army Corps* designates the European corps, as distinguished from the Siberian corps.

²) These were the only three dragoon regiments in the theatre of war; all other cavalry consisted of 1st and 2nd line Cossacks.
:::

[[p76]]

Three horse artillery batteries (7·6 *cm*) of six guns each: 3 batteries.

A half-battery (8·4 *cm*) of four guns (old pattern): 1/2 battery.

Four scout detachments (okhotniki) of approximately 150 men each from the 1st Siberian Corps: 4 companies.

A Red Cross medical detachment with 10 Finnish ambulance wagons; in addition, four pack horses per regiment for transporting the wounded and Chinese arbas ¹) acquired along the way for this purpose.

A supply column, consisting of approximately 1,500 mules.

Total: 12 cavalry regiments ²), 3 1/2 batteries, 4 scout detachments, one medical column, and—1,500 *mules for hauling supplies*.

The ammunition column did not march with the troop column, but had remained to the rear with General Kosakovsky's detachment, which consisted of cavalry, infantry, and frontier guard troops, and was intended to serve as a sort of support for General Mishchenko's force.

If we examine the composition of the force designated for the raid, its considerable cavalry strength is particularly striking. Twelve cavalry regiments correspond to three of our cavalry divisions. While such a substantial number of cavalry regiments ought, organisationally, to be provided with a correspondingly strong artillery component—two horse batteries per division can be considered a minimum—here we find only three in total. The addition of a half-battery of heavy 8 1/2 *cm* guns was intended to facilitate the destruction of various objects—it was thus meant to find less a tactical than a technical application.

The allocation of infantry appears sufficient, considering that the Russian dragoon regiments in particular [[p77]] can be employed advantageously as dismounted infantry.

::: notes
¹) Heavy two-wheeled cart with a sturdy wooden axle.

²) Each consisted of six squadrons; only the Verkhneudinsk [Regiment] of five, its sixth having remained in Port Arthur at the time.
:::

*What seems entirely incredible, however, is the taking along of fifteen hundred pack animals to haul provisions, while the ammunition column remained two days' march behind!*

These organisational disparities could hardly have been accidental, for even during the war there would still have been time to provide the cavalry division with adequate artillery, infantry, and administrative units. On this as on many other occasions, however, one will not go astray in assuming that the well-organised Cossack divisions existing already in peacetime, *of which we heard so much before the war*, were in fact mere fables mechanically repeated in the reports arriving annually from Petersburg.

Apart from the existing defective organisation of the Russian cavalry formations, even this ad hoc assembly of the raiding force was an unfavourable omen for carrying out this cavalry expedition.

Whoever burdens himself with fifteen hundred supply mules seems to have little desire to cover daily distances of a hundred kilometres, as Stuart or Hadik demonstrated.

Whoever leaves his ammunition column two days behind hardly intends to engage in a stubborn fight with the enemy.

Whoever wants to destroy a railway in such a way that an army's supply is disrupted by it takes along more than two sapper officers and thirty sappers!

Whatever obstacles might later stand in the way of carrying out this raid and frustrate its success—already *its very conception shows that from the outset it lacked that energy and will for swift action* which, above all, a cavalry commander must possess.

The *primary operational objective* was Yingkou; the *destruction* of the magazines and railway facilities there.

[[p78]]

The secondary operational objective was the railway bridges of Haicheng and Gaizhou (south of the former).
