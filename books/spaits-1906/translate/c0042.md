In the first double compartment was Lieutenant Colonel of the General Staff Aleksandr Bazarov, whom a fortunate choice had placed in the difficult post of “Attaché to the Attachés,” an office held before him, with less success, by Cavalry Captain Count Ignatiev. Bazarov personified everything in the way of charm, tact, and culture that can be united in a man; his honest, straightforward manner and his German, tinged with a Swabian accent, betrayed the former Stuttgart university student.

This officer’s task was to serve as the liaison between the military attachés on the one hand and the Army High Command in Sayatun as well as Colonel Pestich’s censorship office on the other.

In addition, he was the military superior of all the enlisted personnel assigned to the attachés. Alongside these time-consuming duties, he was entrusted by the Army High Command from time to time with drafting operational studies. The Army High Command was in the habit of issuing identical operational problems to senior General Staff officers to solve, which, similar to our proctored exams, were to be worked out without consulting one another. Comparing the submitted papers at the Army High Command was apparently intended to serve as an aid in planning its own operational measures.

Next to Bazarov lived the Chilean Major Schönmayer, likewise of German descent. Rarely have I seen anyone master the most diverse languages with such fluency as he did. Artillery Major Cheminon—a Frenchman—was rarely seen in the compartment; he was constantly with the 1st Siberian Corps. By contrast, an all the more faithful resident of the carriage was General Silvestre, head of the French mission, who, together with his adjutant, Captain Boucé, occupied half a compartment each. General Silvestre had been military attaché in Berlin for many years; Captain Boucé had been sent by his government to Graz for an extended period some years earlier to learn the German language. Both, general and adjutant alike, seem to have concerned themselves little with Germanic studies during their stay in German lands—or at least did not wish to give it away. It was remarkable that the French in particular voiced their disapproval of the operational blunders of their great ally quite uninhibitedly. After having been attached for a considerable time to the 17th Army Corps, where there was relatively little to see, General Silvestre had already requested his recall to France the previous year, but it had not been granted. From that time on, he harbored a grievance and declared the Russian position in Manchuria to be untenable.

The Argentine Lieutenant Colonel Rostagno felt somewhat uncomfortable. His appearance, his temperament, and his longing for the warm south betrayed the southern Spaniard, who could not feel at home in this cold, bleak monotony. Argentina had sold ships to Japan during the war, and he was made to feel this at the 6th Army Corps, where he stayed for some time—at least so he thought. Since he did not know Russian—which could hardly be expected of an Argentine anyway—his situation out with the troops was difficult. He was certainly an excellent man, gifted with the keen perception of a well-traveled observer, and had already achieved significant accomplishments in the military field, such as in the organization of the army in Bolivia.

Lieutenant Colonel von Lauenstein, head of the German mission, was at that time at the headquarters of the First Army in [[p150]] Huangshan, as were Lieutenant Colonel von Csicserics and the doyen of the military attachés, the British General Gerard. How painful it was for me to learn later that General Gerard, who despite his age had endured all the privations of war with true English phlegm, succumbed on the journey home in Irkutsk to the illness that had long been gnawing at him. I would call him the “Englishman without fear and without reproach,” so completely did he seem to unite all the good qualities of a soldier grown gray in colonial service with those proud and yet engaging manners that are peculiar only to the noblest sons of Albion.

No less agreeable was the British Major Mockler, who had come from India to Mukden during the campaign.

The Romanian military attaché and the Austro-Hungarian Captain Count Szeptycki had preferred from the beginning to attach themselves entirely to a headquarters and forgo accommodation in the already overcrowded carriages. The former was with the 3rd East Siberian Division; the latter was for some time with General Bilderling, but otherwise the loyal companion of General Rennenkampf, at whose headquarters he remained until the conclusion of peace.

The last compartment in carriage No. 203 was occupied by Staff Captain Kachalov. To him the physical well-being of the attachés [[p151]] was entrusted, and he discharged this duty in the most punctual manner. Kachalov, too, possessed those engaging manners of address that distinguish the educated Russian officer so favorably. Later, two more officers were assigned to the attachés—a measure of care that was quite superfluous.
