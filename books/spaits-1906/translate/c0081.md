Our two sotnias would not have been enough against these, and the support of our infantry, which stood on the eastern heights separated from us by a deep ravine, was not to be counted on; thus the most sensible course was to withdraw to Tesinda, where we met our wounded. We had no doctor with us, so that the condition of these poor people was truly pitiable. Some were still able to proceed on horseback, [[p276]] while for those who had bullet wounds in their legs or abdomen, two arbas were already waiting; their owners, however, were still holding out for a gratuity, which Prince Dolgorukov gladly gave them just to get his men to safety. At a brisk trot we returned along the way we had come. The two arbas with the wounded drove ahead of us; there had been no time left to prepare a bed for them on the hard wagons. What agony the poor men must have suffered during that fast ride over the rough, stony track! To be sure, the feldsher had hastily bandaged their wounds, but after a short time we, riding right behind the arbas, saw the white linen turn red with seeping blood and heard the groaning—and praying—of the unfortunates.

After half an hour's trot we slowed to a walk; the path branched off to the northeast and northwest, toward Mamangou and Jaugoa. Prince Dolgorukov also thought it would be better

### With Lieutenant Colonel Slatin's Reconnaissance Detachment.

*5 March.*

The sun had long since disappeared behind the mountains when, on 5 March, I entered the low fanzah where Colonel Leontin Antonovich Zakrschewski was quartered with his adjutant. He knew of my arrival, and when I delivered General Rennenkampf's greetings to the Second Chita Cossack Regiment, he gave a military bow as though the gallant cavalry general were standing right before him.

“How and in what way can I best be of service to you?” he asked, warmly shaking my hand.

“Colonel, I should like to witness further combat action by your regiment.”

“You will have an immediate opportunity for that, Rittmeister. In about an hour, Lieutenant Colonel Slatin will be here; he is setting out tonight with the Fourth and Fifth Sotnias against the Japanese right wing. Do you have reliable weapons, Rittmeister?” I showed the colonel my small pocket revolver, which I had bought in Moscow. “That is no good at all.” — “Have the gentleman given a shashka and a good revolver!” he commanded, turning to his adjutant. “Or would you prefer a carbine instead of the revolver?” I asked for a revolver. Matters are taking a warlike turn, I thought to myself, and went to see that my grey got some oats. The animal had now been under the saddle every day from morning to evening for nearly a week, had stood outdoors every night in twenty degrees of frost, had received dry straw or at best a little gaoliang for feed, was not even shoed—and yet, even after the hard rides [[p279]] of today, was completely sound. When he saw me, he opened his mouth wide—which he always did when he was hungry. He was not handsome: small, with a short neck and large head, and a coat like a bear—but it kept him warm! How miserably our neatly groomed, smooth-coated horses would freeze in a winter campaign!

When I returned to the fanzah, Colonel Zakrschewski had finished official business with his adjutant, a long-bearded staff captain, and was just about to dress for dinner. He was a handsome, stately man, displaying that impeccable cleanliness and neatness in dress and person which those brought up to it from childhood never neglect, even under the most difficult circumstances. And it truly does not take much time to put oneself into a condition tolerable both to oneself and to one's fellows. Some officers, however, consider it especially martial to sit down to dinner greasy and dusty straight off their horse after a manoeuvre, perhaps even tossing their sweaty cap onto the table—that way, at least not a single minute of the cherished post-dinner nap is lost!

The colonel, who spoke German and French fluently, told me that he had had the opportunity in Vienna to become acquainted with the Austrian and Hungarian cavalry. “Yes, what one sees *there*, that is what I call *cavalry*!” he said, turning to his adjutant and a lieutenant colonel who, having just woken up, came over to join us from a corner of the room.
