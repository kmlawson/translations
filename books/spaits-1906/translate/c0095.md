General *Kuropatkin* proceeds toward Yuhuantun; shells strike near his staff, which now moves into open formation.

Yuhuantun—the Japanese appeared to have had only a little infantry and one battery here—is taken in the afternoon, whereupon the *5th and 10th Rifle Regiments march off to the north again as a reserve*.

Throughout the day (7 March), *five batteries stood idle in reserve* near Lanjiatun and Luguantun, namely two from the 25th and three from the 51st Artillery Brigade.

In the evening, six battalions from the 6th Siberian Corps arrived at Mukden. Caohetun and Bajiazi are attacked by the enemy and fall during the night. At the latter place, General Staff Colonel Zapolsky is mortally wounded.

General *Bürger* had fallen back from the north as far as the Imperial Tombs.

The *railway and telegraph communication* to the north was cut, though only temporarily. No destruction of the railway took place. (Lack of Japanese cavalry!)

### The Evacuation of the Shahe Front on 7 and 8 March.

Adjutant General Kuropatkin had already intended on 6 March to evacuate the Shahe front, but refrained from doing so at the request of Generals Bilderling and Kaulbars.

Under the influence of the events of the 7th, however, the commander-in-chief saw himself forced to evacuate the Shahe front on the night of 7–8 March and withdraw the southern front into the fortified position of Mukden–Fushun.

Dating from September 1904, the fortified sectors here consisted of: the Mukden bridgehead on both sides of the railway and on the Mandarin Road; furthermore, a flanking work for this bridgehead above the Mandarin Road bridge; furthermore, the positions at Fuling (steep bank), Jinshan, and Fushun. The southern front began its withdrawal on the evening of the 7th, with the general direction of march toward the northeast.
