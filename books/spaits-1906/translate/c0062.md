## Contempt for Death.

It was before the Battle of Mukden, on the eastern wing, deep in the mountains. The place where I spent the night back then was called Fanshen, if I am not mistaken. South of it lay the Russian positions on the steep heights flanking the Shahe; across the river, which was frozen solid at the time, stood the Japanese.

One gets used to shooting in war just as one does to the clatter of a mill. When an onlooker at that [[p214]] time found a warm place to sleep, he was grateful and glad of it. If he was awakened in the morning by brisk rapid fire rattling up in the mountains, he rolled over onto his other side to sleep on, glad of his safe little spot. After all, it had been the same thing for weeks! By day, from our side and theirs, the dull, rumbling roar of the guns rolled over the mountains at long intervals; at nightfall—as if to call it a day—quiet settled over both sides until morning, when now one side, now the other attempted raids on the opposing villages. This time the crackle of rifles had been closer to our *fanza* than usual, but as the artillery remained silent, it could hardly be any major Japanese attack worth getting up so early for. My sleeping companions, two orderly officers from Rennenkampf's corps, must have calculated along the same lines under their warm blankets, for they showed no sign of getting up anytime soon when I left the *fanza* a few hours later. Right beside our house, on the hillside, stood a dilapidated temple with a spacious courtyard and a well-preserved priest's dwelling that had been converted into a divisional field hospital. The first wounded from the nighttime engagement were just being brought in, among them a senior lieutenant whom I had met in Mukden a few weeks earlier. He had taken a bullet through his right upper arm, but spoke enthusiastically of the fight he had just come from. “Yes, my dear sir,” he said, turning to me, “you mustn't think that we Russians always come off second best—this time we really gave the Japanese what for! Only yesterday evening our Chinese told us that the yellows intended to raid the village tonight, the one lying right in front of the lower redoubt, where the only wells we can draw water from are still located. Two scout detachments of our own were supposed to cross to the other bank of the Shahe tonight. They are held back to wait for the Japanese. Our Chinese hadn't lied—for once—around 3 a.m. the Japs were there; our men lurking in the trenches had by then almost frozen to the ground, so it became all the hotter [[p215]] for them later. In the end, we too had to go down from our position, which is where I caught this lead pill—but not one of them gave us the slip this time! The men from the 10th East Siberian Corps cut off their retreat from behind, and then one after the other was put to the blade ¹). But they fought like devils. When dawn broke toward morning, we saw that there weren't so many of them after all—two or three dozen.” The victory-flushed senior lieutenant would gladly have gone on talking to the listeners gathered around him, had the doctor not called on him to come inside the hospital. “Wait a moment,” he shouted to us as he went, “they're bringing a few Japanese over too, three or four wounded men we took prisoner.” He said this with an affectedly indifferent air, as if taking Japanese prisoner were the most ordinary thing in the world!

Meanwhile, several curious onlookers and doctors had gathered and were waiting for the escort of the wounded, which was now coming down the path from the hillside. Black papakhas, bayonets glinting in the red morning sun, and two white stretchers—that was all we could make out in the procession, which disappeared from our view at the next bend. “People always say that the Japanese never surrender—and now you see for yourself that we take prisoners,” remarked an officer. “Surely those being brought here are severely wounded, and,” I added politely, “as I have heard, Japanese prisoners are treated so well among you that it is hard to see why they should prefer a useless death to captivity. I am glad that I finally have the opportunity to see Japanese up close.” My wish was soon fulfilled. From the gate opposite, the procession of wounded entered the courtyard. The escort formed a wide double file, in the middle of which a small Japanese limped along, leaning heavily on a cane. Behind him followed two more stretchers. The Japanese may have been about thirty paces away from us. He held his head proudly erect, his eyes fixed firmly upon us; his gaze was mesmerizing. No one spoke; only the [[p216]] dragging footsteps of the escort could be heard. The Japanese began to smile. His eyes seemed to grow larger. His mouth opened as though he were about to speak to us; his whole face was so joyful, and yet so sublime. Slowly he came closer, his left hand resting on his cane—that his right hand seemed to be searching for something in the folds of his loose robe we hardly noticed, so captivated were we by his gaze. Now his eyes grow even larger—he laughs out loud—or perhaps he shouted something—a sharp crack, a violent blast of air that nearly throws us to the ground, white gunsmoke swirling before our eyes. It had happened so fast that no one knew what had actually occurred. The front ranks of the escort and the front stretcher with the wounded man and his bearers lay on the ground. The bright-eyed, laughing Japanese? A cloud of smoke still hovered over him—then one saw feet in a pool of blood, something pulpy from which entrails hung, and a torso from which part of the skin and clothing had been pulled back over the head.
