If a military attaché had not understood how to make friends somewhere, his situation was often quite difficult from the moment he left his railcar; not only was [[p131]] every opportunity for insight made difficult for him, but even in procuring the most essential necessities of life, such as quarters and the like, he encountered such obstacles that he could clearly see how little anyone was pleased by his presence. Sometimes political reasons also played a part, as for instance in the case of the Argentine attaché, for whom it was certainly quite awkward personally that his government had sold warships to Japan during the war. Sometimes “making friends” probably also required more than tactful accommodation—a bit of affability, a handshake in the right place, one or even several little glasses of vodka at the right time—useful secondary traits that seemed superfluous to the straightforward nature of the German Major Runkel, which explained his bad mood in Masatun.

In Huanshan, besides the Austro-Hungarian attaché, there lived the English General Gerard, doyen of the military attachés, and the Prussian Lieutenant Colonel von Lauenstein. Assigned to these three gentlemen was the Russian Staff Captain Kachalov.

The quarters assigned to the attachés in the *chin* distillery consisted of a small anteroom, in which Cossacks and servants lodged, and two rooms, separated from the former by a handsomely painted paper wall. Nothing was lacking for the comfort of these rooms except a cozy warmth, which, with the thin walls and torn parchment windows, could not be attained despite all heating. The gentlemen had settled in between the bare walls as best they could—each had set up his camp bed, a camp chair, and a little three-legged table, on the latter of which they wrote their diaries—crafted military history. The Englishman was accommodated most practically; being the eldest, he occupied one half of the room, while Csicserics and Lauenstein shared the other half. The second room—if one could call it that—housed Kachalov and also served as a dining room—two chairs with a plank laid across them took the place of a table.

When we stepped into the fanza, General Gerard happened to be sitting in his “home,” writing in his diary by the light of two English candles. He was a handsome old gentleman with friendly blue eyes, gaunt and wrinkled, as nearly forty years of service in India and Africa could not fail to make him. He had formerly been military attaché in Petersburg and a personal friend of the Tsar, with whom he had hunted tigers in India. This circumstance, together with his distinguished and engaging manner, made him far more popular with the Russians than the representatives of several other states that then maintained much friendlier relations with Russia than England did. General Gerard was ill. Only a month before had he left the hospital in Gongzhuling, and he was now seeking recovery here in the fresh mountain air. “What news, General?”—“Rats, lots of rats at night!” was his laconic reply. “Ils se proménent toute la nuit travers ma figure!” Lieutenant Colonel von Lauenstein had already learned of my arrival from his wife in Harbin. He was newly married and had brought his wife with him at the beginning of the war, knowing she was in good hands, initially with the family of General Uexküll and later, when all women had to leave the theatre of operations, with the [[p133]] [Illustration: General Sir Gerard, English military attaché, in front of the military attachés’ carriage in Mukden.]

friendly German merchants Tillmann in Harbin. At dinner we sat together quite merrily; only the Englishman was somewhat taciturn. The food, from General Linevich’s kitchen, was excellent. Serving at table was Sanyi, Lieutenant Colonel von Csicserics’s orderly—here in Manchuria he did full credit to his native town of Makó on the Tisza. Deft, nimble, and always clean, he stood out advantageously from the *dull*, ever-greasy Russian servants and was soon the favourite of all the attachés, which earned him not only kind words but also clear-ringing coin.

Thus life here would have been quite pleasant had two unwelcome guests not come to us during the night through the gaps and cracks in the walls: the cold and the rats! For me it was all the harder since, lacking a camp bed, I lay on the cold floor, and the disgusting quadrupeds could stroll back and forth across me all the more easily.

[[p134]]

Yet in time a person gets used to anything. With what stoic calm General Gerard would search every morning for his soap, which the rats had carried off to some corner of the fanza during the night. “Coberas are worse.” The cobras in India are even worse—was his curt self-consolation; he wiped off the soap and washed with it.
