On his hands he wore large lined mittens. All the photographs accompanying this book clearly show, I think, that the army was *superbly equipped against the cold*, and that all the talk and writing about half-naked, frozen Russian soldiers was either malicious fabrication or stemmed from letters that deceitful reservists sent home to arouse pity and wheedle out money.

For military purposes, this clothing had several disadvantages. Above all, it was too heavy and hindered the man in moving freely, in aiming properly, and in pulling the trigger. As advantageous as the absence of any shiny metal on uniform and weapons was, the colour and shape of the misshapen, large white or black fur caps were equally unfavourable. These offered an excellent target to the enemy and had to be wrapped around with the grey bashlyk during combat, which only made the entire headgear even more clumsy.

I had frequent opportunities to see captured Japanese. In their yellow caps, little yellow overcoats, and yellow gaiters—though admittedly with an excellent snow hood pulled over their heads—they seemed, beside the fur-swathed Russians, to be marching along as if in summer overcoats.

Just as suitably as against the cold, the Russian dresses in the hot summer, when white linen caps and linen blouses replace the heavy winter furs. In place of the valenki he wears high boots, which may well be a Russian national tradition, but is less practical from a military standpoint. The high leather boot, even for the cavalryman, is not a battle-worthy type of footwear. If the boot gets wet, the man is often unable to pull it off his foot for weeks. It is strange that army administrations so rarely take as an example the experiences of tourists regarding suitable footwear and clothing, but still [[p252]] cling to traditional ways! Today the whole world already walks, travels, rides, and engages in sports in natural leather shoes and natural leather gaiters, and yet there are still poor soldiers who freeze in winter and languish in summer in brilliantly polished boots! There is surely no sportsman who would wear the same outfit for ice sports and rowing, yet there are dress regulations that know only one cap, one coat, one pair of trousers, etc., for winter and summer, for south and north!

The reality of war soon does away with all unnecessary and impractical frippery, forcing the soldier's clothing to adapt to the geographical conditions of the country in question. Kuropatkin had not only permitted soldiers and officers to dress in whatever way best protected them from the cold, but even had local footwear and padded Chinese garments with long sleeves purchased from Chinese contractors and distributed to his soldiers.

All of this shows that the Russian high command [[p253]] did everything possible to clothe the army in the field irreproachably and beyond complaint.

An interesting comparison was afforded by the various uniforms of the military attachés. Here it was clearly evident that the representatives of young armies, such as the United States, Chile, Argentina, Sweden, Norway, and others, were uniformed far more practically and in a manner better suited to military purposes than those of the so-called old military powers, who cannot part with the paintbox, yet when it comes to the most important thing:

forget the “field serviceability of the uniform in winter as in summer”!

*The provisioning of the Russian army was excellent.* One needed only to look at the well-fed faces of the men to be convinced that all the calamitous reports of starving and destitute soldiers were sheer fabrication. A further proof of the good and regular nourishment was the state of health of the army. That there are certain times in war when regular provisioning [[p254]] becomes impossible is self-evident. The conditions for provisioning the army were not unfavourable despite the considerable distance from the mother country. Manchuria is a highly cultivated, fertile land; barley, gaoliang, and bean cake are its export articles, the rivers abound with fish, and in the north of the country as well as in neighbouring Mongolia there are numerous herds of cattle. German contractors supplied cattle for slaughter from China. From Russia, mainly cabbage and sugar had to be brought forward. Supplying the cabbage caused the greatest difficulty, as Quartermaster General Guber told me, since the soldier is accustomed to this vegetable, but it was not to be had in Manchuria.
