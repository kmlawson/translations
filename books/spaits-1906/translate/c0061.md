Toward evening it grew quiet; a peaceful stillness rested over the wide valley, from which the silver ribbon of ice of the Shahe stretched far into the plain, visible from afar. The flames in Nanshanpu seemed to have been extinguished; only thick black smoke betrayed the successful day's work of the Russian mortar battery.

What had become of the scout detachment down there, we did not know.

As I was leaving, Nikolai Semyonovich asked me to send him toothache drops from Mukden when I had the chance. Does he still need them? His redoubt was taken by the Japanese two nights later.

[[p212]]

As I rode down the mountain slope in the evening stillness toward my ammunition park, I thought to myself: What is *courage* and what is *cowardice*? Could I call it courage that the night before, out of curiosity, I decided to attend the combat of a battery? And should I now consider myself a coward because there in the battery, where, chattering with cold, I had to press myself flat against the earthen slope, the thought came to me that it was really quite pointless to have come up here just to have Shimose shells and shrapnel flying over my head? Was it my fault that during this time I could not help thinking of the stereoscopic photographs the lieutenant had shown me early that morning? Did I have less courage than those Russian officers who, in the zeal of their duty, seemed to pay no heed to the looming danger? Certainly not! Courage is the *disregard of danger*; this can come from *ignorance* of it, in which case it only *seems* to be courage. The man storms courageously against the enemy rampart—if he knew, as his officer does, that the enemy has laid mines around the rampart that could destroy him at any moment, he might turn back! Furthermore, a man can be courageous who *does not notice* the danger because he is too absorbed in his own duties—in his manual tasks; that is what the mechanical training, the *drill*, of the soldier must strive for, which, precisely for reasons of fire discipline, is not as reprehensible as is often asserted. In the impact of enemy shells, the artilleryman sees the approach of deadly lead, but the mechanically drilled operation of his gun—and, for the officer, the strenuous observation of the results of his own shots—leaves no time for other thoughts. I saw men in the battery make the sign of the cross on their foreheads with their right hand, while with their left they mechanically reached into the wheel spokes of the gun recoiling backward!

From what has just been said, one might conclude that through a minimum of education and a maximum of drill, that—I would say *mechanical*—courage can be instilled which the Russian army as a whole possessed, and which would indeed have sufficed for a skillfully conducted defense.

[[p213]]

Who, then, is courageous in the most unrestricted sense of the word? He who sees the danger, recognizes it, and is nevertheless ready to sacrifice his life for a higher goal. With the terrible effect of modern weapons, danger reveals itself with shattering force to anyone who leaves his sheltering cover to advance; to carry one's body into the rain of bullets, mechanical courage no longer suffices for this; today, the courage of enthusiasm must lead the attacker against the enemy—against a real, hated enemy. Short-sightedness and drilled routines, which are certainly an excellent means up to a certain level of moral demand, are never sufficient in an attack against a modern-armed and morally capable opponent—just as they did not suffice for the Russians, despite all their numerical superiority. For this we need more: enthusiasm, enthusiasm for a great idea shared by the entire people. And in our intellectually advanced age, such an idea can today be awakened only by the concept of nation or religion, as we have seen with the Boers and the Japanese, and as everyone learns to comprehend who has seen an earth-smoking, death-sown assault field of the recent battles.
