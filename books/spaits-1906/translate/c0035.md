The fact is that, as a result of this ceaselessly waged petty warfare, there were newly wounded men every day. —

For the correspondent here in the positions—when he was granted access to them—there was an abundant field of observation. If he knew how to talk with the soldiers—the men are trusting and talkative—he could sit with them in the evening in their dug-out and listen to their conversations. “How well off the Japanese over there must be,” they said, “they always have schnapps, for every time they come at us, they are drunk.”

“And how well our prisoners over there are said to be doing!”

“Chefoo”—so they called a Chinese interpreter—“showed us letters from Russia, and it was in the newspaper too: the Japanese have had a large ship of cabbage brought in so that they can give our men borsch. Vasily Alexandrovich showed us the letter—the same man who was left behind near Linjiapu last week along with four others from the scouting detachment. But he wasn’t killed; he surrendered along with the others—Chefoo told us—and the Japanese brought them to their general, who presented each of them with a ruble; Chefoo found out on that very same day.” “We’ll see Chefoo hanging from a tree yet,” another said. “Fyodor Maksimovich”—that was their captain—“beat him the day before yesterday when we came back, and threatened that he would strike him dead if he ever showed his face among us again.”

More difficult than in the positions was monitoring the men’s intercourse with the Chinese [[p126]] at the railway stations. Here, where individual troop trains often stood at a station for days on end, Japanese emissaries had the best opportunity to mingle among the loitering men.

[Illustration]

Yet socialist propaganda, too, was conducted most successfully during the long train journeys, and many a man unwittingly brought a pamphlet along into the dug-out that a newspaper vendor at some station had slipped into his coat sleeve. Why, at the station in Harbin no less than *sixteen poods* of such pamphlets were discovered one day!

Many assert that *socialist propaganda* was to blame for the misfortune of the Russian army; I *do not believe this*, for the Russian soldier seemed to me intellectually too undeveloped for such ideas to have taken root with him.

When we rode away from Gouluozi, we kept to the military road that ran parallel to the foremost lines opposing the First Army—towards Huangshan. From the Japanese side a white cloud of smoke rose here and there, and soon the dull thud of their siege artillery came rolling over. The Russians replied, but rarely did the cannonade last long. Serious damage has not thus far been caused by the heavy Japanese guns; they seemed to be too far away to observe the effect of their fire. If the field guns chimed in from time to time, one could more easily guess their positions from the simultaneously exploding shrapnel of the battery salvos. A beautiful—one might almost say—manoeuvre scene.

Along the way I met a poruchik—a reserve lieutenant whom I knew from the journey. His one-week stay in the section trench seemed to have agreed [[p127]] with him rather badly. He could not tolerate the fare out there, he said. Instead of cooking forward in the battle line itself, the field kitchens drove up as far as the nearest village, from where the fatty cabbage soup with meat was then carried into the defensive line by individual men. Naturally, the food arrived there half-frozen. “Thank God that one only had to choke down this grub once a day—I would rather have done without it entirely,” remarked the poor fellow, who showed distinct signs of the dysentery that the men, in their vexation with the whole country, called “Manchurska,” “but after all, one cannot live on chocolate and frozen bread alone!” Now, as far as bread is concerned, a loaf was rarely to be found in the troop area that had not frozen on its way from the field bakery. It soon thawed out again, to be sure, but retained a strange, unpleasant taste. —

As we rode on, we were able to study the technical installations of the positions. These were laid out with circumspection and with ample means. Over the Hun River five bridges were built—partly on fixed, partly on floating supports; an equal number of military roads led radially from Mukden to the front.

Extensive use was made of the light field railway, which on several stretches even had small passenger carriages running at regular intervals. From the main line of the standard railway, a branch line ran to the left wing towards Fushun, and another to the right wing towards Dawangjiapu and Xiupu.

Along these standard railways, or connected to them by light field railways, lay the first-line divisional field hospitals. The standard railway towards Xiupu had rendered excellent service in evacuating the wounded during the fighting around Sandepu.
