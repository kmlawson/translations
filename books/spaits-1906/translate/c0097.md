### Kuropatkin's Counter-Offensive on 9 March.

Kuropatkin's plan was to extricate himself from the threatening encirclement from the north-west and, to this end, to advance toward Ci'ertun on 9 March with approximately *eighty battalions*. Of the forces positioned at and west of Mukden, the commander could place scarcely more than thirty battalions under his direct command for this task. The remainder required was to concentrate under General *Zarubaev* at Dawa, that is north-east of Mukden, in the course of 8 and 9 March.

[[p322]]

For this purpose, fifteen to twenty battalions each were detached from the corps that were just retreating northward from the Shahe to the Hunhe, and these were now to continue their march toward Dawa, that is, toward the north-west. Yet this concentration order, issued on 7 March, had failed entirely to reckon with the *imperative conditions of time and space*! The corps that were to detach battalions had already begun their retreat from the Shahe on 8 March; the new order did not reach them until the evening of 8 March or even early on the morning of 9 March, on and north of the Hunhe; carrying out this order meant, however, that the battalions concerned, ordered to Dawa, had to make detours—with the exception of those of the 1st Army Corps—which entailed a considerable loss of time. Those of the 4th Army and 2nd Siberian Corps arrived late, while the twenty battalions expected from the 3rd Siberian Corps did not reach Dawa at all.

General-Adjutant Kuropatkin had frequently demonstrated his skill in concentrating and deploying forces during the campaign. This time, his effort at the eleventh hour to form a powerful strike force for a decisive attack from elements of the 3rd and 1st Armies—which by then were already in retreat—was to prove his undoing. The detachment of the aforementioned troops to Dawa had the direct consequence of *weakening the centre*, which enabled the *enemy to achieve a strategic breakthrough*!

It is difficult to deduce what motives may have guided army headquarters in concentrating numerically such a powerful force at the eleventh hour, which could scarcely be done without impairing the cohesion of the affected corps, already loosened by the various group combinations. The strength of such a reserve would indeed suggest an offensive attempt, as it were a final exertion of all forces to turn what was still not an entirely hopeless fate in favour of their own arms. Praiseworthy as such a resolve would have been several days earlier, in the actual event it came [[p323]] decidedly too late; it was impracticable and was carried out only partially and belatedly.

Just as little as the timing were the conditions of space in which this offensive was planned favourably chosen, for two reasons: Dawa, where the group was to assemble, was too far removed from those parts of the 3rd and 1st Armies from which the various battalions were drawn for it to be reached in time, with the exception of the battalions of the 1st Army Corps. The direction in which the attack was launched, that is, generally from Ci'ertun toward the south-west, was neither sensitive for the Japanese—at best it could have brought the enemy's turning attempt to a halt, for which, however, fewer forces than those assigned would have sufficed—nor would an attack with eighty battalions have encountered a numerically worthy adversary here; it was bound, therefore, to be a blow in the air!
