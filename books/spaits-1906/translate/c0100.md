With his bayonet, a soldier quickly dug out the nearest bullet—it had the reddish-brown jacket of a Japanese infantry projectile. But before the debate on this could continue, a violent explosion in the village drove us to a hasty retreat. Whether it was a Japanese shell that had struck there, or whether the Russians had left ammunition behind in the village that was now exploding in the fire—we did not know; after a violent detonation, we saw a massive column of smoke, mixed with

[Illustration: Yingpan.]

earth and rubble, rising before us, similar to those that Japanese Shimose used to throw up.

When I looked back toward the south half an hour later, the heavens, as far as the eye could reach, were fiery red. High flames blazed up from Yingpan; above the blaze, sparks danced and showered in the black smoke. Down the Hun River, the villages were burning as well.

This was roughly the time when the Russians, after a bloody two-week struggle in which they had left twenty thousand dead and sixty thousand wounded, were forced to abandon Mukden. The decision had fallen far away [[p332]] in the west, but disaster had overtaken us too in wild haste. From Mukden to Yingpan, the flames flickering toward the sky, now blazing together in the darkness into a vast sea of fire, showed that Russia's rule here had come to an end.

The regimental staff was situated a few versts north of Yingpan. Late in the evening, the commander had been summoned to the brigade staff. The officers of the third and sixth sotnias, who had marched in during the day, had just laid down to sleep when the regimental adjutant called into the room: "We are falling back!" Up again, back to the horses, standing for an hour in the courtyard, waiting mounted on the road for an hour—then the regiment marched off. The night was dark; along the road, infantry bivouacked by campfires. Behind us, villages were burning again—no wonder! No one put out the campfires upon marching off, and the dry gaoliang easily catches fire in the wind. But no one cared about that now; after all, there was no going back, and the land in which the war was being waged was not their own! The path could not be seen in the darkness; only the tread of the horses indicated whether one was still marching along the wheel ruts cut deep into the clay by the arbas, across a field, or over a frozen body of water. The troops had no maps for the region north of the Hun River—who would have thought that one would have to head north instead of south!

Often it was difficult to get through the campfires with the horses; when one had finally wound one's way between the blazing pyres and pyramids of rifles, with the horses often stepping into the still-glowing coals of a burnt-out fire, one's eyes were so dazzled that in the darkness the man next to him could not be seen.

A broad body of water crossed the line of march. Barely in the middle, the ice gave way. We pulled our feet up, but the horses are small, and soon the saddle too is underwater. From behind they press forward, and the riders in front cannot get out of the water; the horses strike their chests against the sharp-edged sheet of ice, which they cannot climb onto. Several Cossacks attempt to smash the ice with their rifle butts [[p333]]—in vain! We must turn around and return to the bank to attempt the crossing elsewhere. The sotnias split up and now each seek their way individually in the dark night. One Cossack after another; in this way, the ice holds.

Over on the other bank, the regiment assembled; but soon there is another delay. Up ahead, somewhere in the sunken road, an overloaded wagon has broken down; the entire column halts and cannot move on. Dismount—wait. If only there had been a campfire nearby to dry our soaked clothes and boots! In the night frost, the wet garments had quickly become coated with a layer of ice, and our legs could scarcely bear the pain of the cold any longer; men hopped from one foot to the other to keep from freezing.

At last the lantern hanging from the last wagon of the baggage train began to move; the way was clear—Mount!—Walk!—March! Those who had stood in the water, however, soon dismounted again and ran alongside their horses.

It may have been one o'clock in the morning when the regiment reached Xiaohadao, where General Danilov's troops were bivouacked.

My feet were numb with cold, as was my left hand, which held the reins. To make matters worse, during the sudden departure from Yaopu I had been unable to find my left glove in the dark fangzi. That too had now become war booty for the Japanese.

In a small, grimy fangzi, Colonel Zakrzhevsky was awaiting the general. I pushed the hard leather case holding my camera under my head and fell asleep instantly—after all, I had ridden more than ninety versts that day!
