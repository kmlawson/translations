To pin the enemy along his entire front through vigorous engagements, possibly through diversions against his flanks and rear, while at the same time attacking and taking the pivot of his left wing—Sandepu—with a relatively superior force—as Gripenberg's army indeed was at the start of this operation—and then: to [[p96]] *roll up the Japanese position line from the left wing toward the right!*

That was the idea—or at least it ought to have been the idea for the resumption of the offensive on the part of the Russians at the end of January.

In actual execution, however, we see here first of all a diversion timed and positioned ineffectively—carried out by General Mishchenko with a cavalry corps against Yingkou. After this caricature of a raid, which has already been discussed in detail, had ended without result, the Second Army's attack against Sandepu began, having been, as it were, announced in advance to the Japanese by the aforementioned cavalry excursion.

[Illustration: Chinese liquor distillery.]

The Second Army fights, bleeds—bleeds to death. The Third and First Armies stand by—waiting—and looking on!

Where is the commander-in-chief here, who should have led the whole unified force to the attack—and to victory? Where is the general staff technique of his army chief of staff, General Sakharov, already known to the world from his exquisite telegrams?

Poor, brave, gallant corps, who stood under fire there at Sandepu again [[p97]] after a murderous one-year campaign, and those of you who, newly arrived from Europe, had to receive your baptism of fire there! For you it was all the same whether it was dissension—or the incompetence of the commanders. Your commander, Adjutant General Gripenberg, could indeed—whether out of wounded pride or because he realized the futility of his efforts under the given circumstances—turn his back on you and go home; whether he acted rightly as a soldier in doing so remains another matter; but you had to pay for this botched game with your blood—uselessly and ingloriously, which is the bitterest thing of all for a soldier!...

And now to a concise account of the events and how it came to pass that the army could not master the liquor distillery.

To carry out the attack against the Japanese left wing, *General Gripenberg* had at his disposal a substantial force, more than sufficient for the assigned task: four corps and a cavalry corps, the latter reinforced by approximately an infantry division.

These forces had been grouped further to the west toward the Hunhe about a week before the start of the attack, so that the western wing groups, advancing straight ahead and pushing through victoriously, should have struck the enemy's line of communications. These troop movements, as well as all other preparations for the attack, appear not to have been noticed by the enemy, for as events showed, he had taken no countermeasures against them. The Chinese had been expelled; those caught spying—or sometimes merely suspected of it—were beheaded, and their wrinkled, yellow severed heads, impaled on poles, adorned many an entrance to a village. Even the military attachés were not allowed to see Gripenberg's cards, and journalists were not permitted to leave Mukden at all at that time.

The attack was planned for January 24. On the evening of the 23rd, the thermometer dropped to −22° R, and during the night it grew even colder.

Among some units the rumor spread that the attack had been postponed on account of this abnormal cold, [[p98]] and several commanders are said to have made representations in this regard as well.

To the cold was added an icily sharp wind from the Hunhe, against which no winter fur coat and no papakha offered protection. The troops of the VIII Army Corps were nonetheless full of enthusiasm. They had arrived fresh from Europe and were to receive their baptism of fire on the morrow.

[Illustration: Chinese border near Dalinor.]

Early on January 24, the *Second Army* was deployed from the right toward the left wing as follows:

*West Group:* General Mishchenko, with roughly the troops that had been placed under his command during the raid on Yingkou, reinforced by the 54th and parts of the 61st Reserve Infantry Division, between the Hunhe and the Chinese border northwest of Panpuosa.

*Center Group:* I Siberian and VIII European Corps north of Heigoutai and at Changtanhenan—that is, echeloned to the left rear of Mishchenko.

*East Group:* The Rifle Corps formed from the 1st, 2nd, and 5th European Rifle Brigades northeast of Sandepu; echeloned to the left rear of this, the X Army Corps.

[[p99]]

If we now believe that these forces, so promising in both numbers and quality—they were mostly European corps, superbly disciplined and well equipped—would launch a unified attack on the morning of the 24th, we are mistaken! On this day nothing more occurred than an advance by General Mishchenko; with his Cossacks on the right bank of the Hunhe in the vicinity of Heigoutai, he encountered weak Japanese cavalry, which fell back—and then he halted! In other words, General Mishchenko's advance, scarcely unleashed before it ground to a halt, had a result that day similar to the general's raid on Yingkou already discussed; it told the Japanese, who were somewhat unprepared here: “Look out, gentlemen, tomorrow we attack you; take your precautions, shift your reserves from the center toward the left wing—you may do so without worry, for you must already know from experience that the Russians with their three armies *never attack at the same time*!”
