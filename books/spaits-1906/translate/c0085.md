When one arrives in a village in the pitch-dark night, it is almost impossible to reconnoitre it thoroughly. So it was in Zabei; our only concern had to be to get some rest at last. The Chinese, whom we had awakened from their sleep, proved friendly and helpful. Soon the fire was burning under the kang, and my moustache slowly detached itself from the upturned long-haired fur coat, to which it had been frozen fast since evening. Our host had also thrown some gaoliang straw onto the kang, on which, stretched out comfortably, we now sipped the hot tea with which the Chinese served us.

Some men who had been in Qingjiapuzi that day were brought before us and stated that this village was occupied only by a weak force of Japanese infantry. Thereupon we lay down to sleep. Had outposts been posted outside? After all, we were close to the enemy—Japanese had been reported barely a few hours away from us—yet I had not noticed any of the officers attending to outposts. I went outside and saw that the Cossacks had seen to this themselves. Two men at a time, their carbines ready to fire, stood before the entrances to the village.

[[p289]]

*7 March.*

Around eight o'clock in the morning we had set out from Zabei, and at eleven o'clock—marching south—we arrived in Nanjiahe. Here we found a sotnia of Amur Cossacks belonging to General Maslov's group; their commander knew no more about the enemy than we did, and was sullen and taciturn. Living for thirteen months in Manchuria without a permanent abode,

[Illustration: Russian border guard officer with Chinese spies.]

enduring the tropical heat in summer, nearly sinking in the mud during the rainy season, and wintering in fanzas with paper walls—that is a life which may perhaps still seem bearable to the Asiatic Cossack, but inevitably dulls the European officer. The Amur Cossacks had sent out spies and were waiting here for their return. Thus Lieutenant Colonel Slatin also decided to rest here, all the more so [[p290]] as the fanza in which the Amur Cossack officers had already settled was very inviting. It seemed to belong to a wealthy Manchu, for the kitchen and servants' quarters lay opposite the main building, on either side of the large, handsomely painted entrance gate. The Cossacks whom we took into the courtyard of the fanza with us quickly found their way to the kitchen, just as they generally displayed a special aptitude for camp life. However well the Chinese might conceal his pigs and chickens, the Cossack soon found them, and shortly thereafter they were already stewing over the campfire, for which he ruthlessly used rafters, doorposts, wagon shafts, and the like. For the rest, such damage was always handsomely compensated to the inhabitants, and since the officers maintained discipline among their men, the Chinese were always well disposed toward us—or so at least it seemed.

That the Russian soldiers took little fancy to the diminutive Chinese wives could only be pleasing to the latter; this was probably also the main reason why they preferred to see Russian troops enter their villages rather than their racial kin, the Japanese, who, upon arriving in a settlement, were accustomed to requisitioning not only the usual foodstuffs, but also—women. Hence the great haste with which the Manchus sent off their "Madame" whenever they caught wind of Japanese.

This time, however, one of the Cossacks did set out in search of adventure; we had barely entered the fanza when a party of Chinese arrived, gesturing vehemently, before the commander of the Amur sotnia. The aggrieved Chinese held in his hand the papakha which he had pulled off the intruder's head in order to identify him. Naturally, it was the clerk's cap! A brief interrogation, resounding slaps left and right across the culprit's cheeks, and the surrounding Chinese bowed down to the ground in satisfaction, praising the master's justice; the avenged husband acknowledged the vindication of Babushka's honour by falling to his knees into the bargain.

Toward midday the spies returned and reported that the perevals south and southwest of the village of Chebaipo (about [[p291]] ten versts south of us) were occupied by the enemy. At three o'clock in the afternoon an officer with his platoon was dispatched toward each of the aforementioned pass heights. Both platoons found the passes occupied by Japanese infantry, came under fire, suffered wounded, and returned toward evening to Nanjiahe, where Lieutenant Colonel Slatin was just dispatching spies, for which service a whole pack of Chinese offered themselves. In such cases the craftiest faces had to be chosen at random; usually, once they had received their assignment, the men also demanded an advance of money—whether they would return, or whether they went off at all and did not find it more convenient simply to make something up out of their own imagination the next day, of that one had no certainty whatsoever. They were paid thirty to fifty roubles per man.
