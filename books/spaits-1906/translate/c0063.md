I can find no words to describe what I felt at that moment. The appalling spectacle shook us all; in silence we gazed at the horror our eyes had seen happen with such bewildering speed, yet it was not the blood before us, but the torn corpse and the thought that we ourselves had escaped death only by sheer chance, that made our nerves tremble. Every one of us felt that in this moment he had seen a revelation in which he had previously not believed: *that even today there is still a people that knows how to die laughing, that prefers death even to an honorable captivity!*

This was the first Japanese I had seen at close quarters during the war.

And now one might ask: why were there no Russians capable of such an act?

To such a question it is surely enough merely to mention the names of all those Russian generals and colonels who fell in the ranks of their soldiers, flinching not from the hail of bullets—making, to be sure, a useless sacrifice. seek, however, a counterpart to the fanaticism and contempt for death of that Japanese, then let us ask not only among the Russians, but also among the rest of us, the white civilized nations, and we shall see that our nerves and our ideals are already far more worn out than those of the East Asians—I say East Asians—the Chinese and Manchus included, as far as nerves are concerned.

What is the psychological obstacle to bringing troops forward under enemy fire? The fear of death and of painful injury! Now, the East Asian possesses the precious quality of bearing pain more easily than we do, of being more indifferent than we are to the pain of his fellow man and the sight of blood, and of having no fear whatsoever of a swift, painless death. In modern combat, where the mass effect of modern weapons produces a shattering impression even on the distant observer, these are decisive advantages that already secure the East Asian a certain superiority over us for years to come—until, that is, by absorbing our culture, they stand on the same level with us physically and morally.

To this indifference to the physical pain of death, however, there is added in the Japanese an ideal element. *Death for the Fatherland* is regarded by him as the highest honor; for his fatherland he goes to his death not merely willingly, but joyfully; his religion and his ancestor worship have instilled these feelings into his breast from childhood on.

We know that every Japanese soldier carried with him a postcard for his relatives, informing them that he had had the good fortune to die for the fatherland. If he was actually killed, this postcard was sent by his comrades to Japan, and the Russians, too, never denied the fallen enemy this act of piety.

In order, however, to better characterize that physical indifference to a painless death, which is common to all East Asians, and the “consecration of oneself to death,” which is an ideal trait of the Japanese, I wish to mention the following examples.

[[p220]]

Even before my journey to East Asia, I had read a great deal about the ingeniously cruel death penalties that Chinese justice makes its victims suffer. Consequently, I imagined the Chinese to be a crude, hard-hearted people, and was not a little astonished to find in them good-natured, affable folk. Notwithstanding this, however, I had the opportunity to convince myself that the Chinese's inventive genius for tormenting his fellow man surpasses all the tortures of our Middle Ages.

In Mukden there was a large courtyard enclosed by high walls, in the middle of which stood a fantastical wooden dragon. This was the execution ground of the Chinese court. Floggings, the tearing out of fingernails, the hacking off of limbs, and the like could be seen here once a week, though I had no desire to witness them.

Accompanied by a Russian officer, I visited this place of torment one day. Among the various instruments of torture, an iron pot caught my eye, resembling a flowerpot, with a small hole in its bottom. The Chinese man accompanying us—he was probably some executioner's assistant—explained to us the purpose of this vessel. The condemned criminal is stripped, tied face down to a table, and the iron urn is strapped opening-down over his buttocks, so tightly that a starved rat, which had previously been placed inside the urn, can find no way out. The condemned man is then left for a day or two to the rat gnawing at his living flesh. What follows, however, would have surpassed the imagination of the bloodthirstiest Grand Inquisitor: the rat, satiated with the flesh of its victim, begins to grow sluggish. Now the executioner comes with a red-hot iron rod, inserts it through the small opening in the bottom of the pot, and tortures the rat with it until, to escape the glowing iron, it creeps into the rectum of its victim. Yet the red iron rod pursues it even in there, burning and prodding it until it has bitten its way forward, not into the belly, but into the intestines of the man, who by now has been tortured into laughing madness! A skilled executioner, through careful selection of the [[p221]] rat, is said to be able to keep the condemned man alive for three to four days despite all the agony!
