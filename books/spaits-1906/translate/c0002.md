Despite all these dim prospects of reaching the theatre of war in any acceptable capacity, an outfit and other equipment nevertheless had to be procured for the purpose. What headaches—and money—that had cost! Not too much and not too little—not too expensive and not too cheap—and not a single soul who could have given any halfway practical advice. Will you drag your trunks with you everywhere? Will there be a somewhere or a something over there in Manchuria where you can securely deposit part of your luggage? Or might you also be able to procure what you lack over there?

[[p13]]

A sound principle in such undertakings is: better too much than too little. Sending things back or, in the worst case, leaving them behind is always easier than having missing items sent on. In cases where transporting all one's luggage is uncertain, it is advantageous to divide it among several small trunks. Whoever cannot remember what each individual one contains should draw up an inventory of them.

It was advisable to procure everything at home, since in Russia even furs and galoshes are far more expensive than with us.

Photographic supplies, too, should be taken along from a firm known to be reputable; people are all too fond of foisting old plates and spoiled chemicals onto a stranger, whereby then—as chance usually has it—precisely the best shots are ruined.

My quite deficient knowledge of the Russian language still had to be supplemented, several books and papers on East Asia and the present war read, and several most humble letters written to high-ranking personages in Petersburg. An orderly person also makes arrangements in the event that he should never return.

All that, thank God, now lay behind me; I had even succeeded in stowing the trunks and bags—seven in number—in the compartment—and now, with God's help, we could be on our way!

I know no more magnificent feeling than setting out on a great journey. When the propeller begins to turn in the mighty colossus of a ship as it steers majestically away from the mole toward the open sea, or the comfortable Pullman carriage glides slowly along the platform, my heart fills with so much joy, so much longing for what is distant, for the unknown...

The train rumbles over the points, hurrying out of the suburbs of Vienna; the Giant Ferris Wheel still waves from the Prater, and then the train races in wild haste across the Marchfeld—and with it, far, far ahead into the indefinite, the uncertain, races my imagination.

Thoughts weave and entwine with one another until they turn into dreams; smoothly the springs sway and [[p14]] the wheels hum their lullaby. Thus it goes on for a few hours—then a jolt, the screech of the brakes, wakes us—the train comes to a halt. Outside, deep night; at the station of Granica, brilliant white, deep snow.

“Pozhaluysta, pasport!” “Passport, please!” The first word on Russian soil. Here, a passport is as much a part of a person as his soul. The police officer and customs officials are extremely polite; even the anxiety that they might charge duty on my two cameras once again is happily past. A large, elegant dining room, dignified calm, everything proceeds slowly, deliberately—confidently, so to speak. For the first time I feast on the famous Russian “zakuski,” delicacies laid out in lavish variety at the buffet.

They contribute to the favorable impression that I feel upon first stepping onto sacred Russian soil—just as I had expected, for at heart I had always been a sincere Russophile. Surely these were merely the slanders of a malicious press that sought to undermine Russia's prestige. To me, its military failures to date—though admittedly lasting rather long by now—were explainable by the exposed location of the theatre of war and other strategic reasons; I did not doubt that the tide must turn before long—only not too soon, so that I might still arrive in time!

My table neighbor—in his case the inspection of passport and baggage had taken somewhat longer, for he was a Jew—asked me about the purpose of my journey. “Why didn't you go to the Japanese instead?” he remarked. “Things won't go well for you among our people.” “Better than things apparently went for you in Kishinev,” I replied angrily, disturbed in my illusions, and went to the ticket office to purchase my seat reservation to Warsaw. Beside the counter stood a large tin with a red cross—a collection box for the surviving dependents of those fallen in the war. When I had dropped into the box the change from the ticket that the cashier slid across the counter to me, I felt that I too had now contributed my mite to the costs of the war—and was worthy to continue the journey.
