Here in Irkutsk, however, one truly feels that one is drawing nearer to the war. The recruits have only just been called up. In the barracks, in every larger courtyard, even on the street [[p45]] [Illustration: Red Cross feeding station for the sick.]

drills are held. “Na pravo, na levo,” one hears from all sides. Many detachments are still in civilian clothes, wearing only the large, shaggy papakha on their heads. In this principal staging station, many commissariat officers and military officials have their permanent quarters; military doctors for 7,000 sick, officers arriving and departing daily, among them some in the picturesque costume of the Caucasians—all this offers an ever-changing, colorful scene. Even apart from this, Irkutsk, as an entrepôt for Chinese and Siberian trade to the West, enjoys quite lively commerce. At midday and in the evening there is bustling life; in the brightly lit display windows of the department stores, some of which are laid out on a grand scale, are to be found all the goods of European industry, everything fabulously expensive, of course. The only thing here that is excellent and for which one pays nothing is the crystal-clear water of the Angara, which makes up for the lack of good wells.

[[p46]]

Once the stores are locked up at eight o'clock in the evening, however, deep darkness reigns and shady rabble roams through the town. To be sure, night watchmen patrol the streets, striking their sticks against the ground—a reminder of Constantinople—yet cases of late passers-by being stripped of fur coat and purse even on the main streets are by no means rare.

And now a look at one of those places where an attempt is made to heal the many wounds inflicted by the war. It is the hospital built from an endowment by General Kaufmann and bearing the name of the Dowager Empress. The chief physician, a German, receives me and most kindly explains everything to me.

[Illustration: East of Tankhoy.]

The permanent medical establishments begin in Mukden. Formerly there were also splendid hospitals in Liaoyang, which are now in the hands of the Japanese. The largest casualty distribution station is Harbin, with beds for 30,000 sick; from there begins the evacuation to the west and to the east, to Nikolsk, Vladivostok, and Khabarovsk. Before Irkutsk as well, along the entire railway line, [[p47]] there are hospitals, the largest in Chita. Actual hospital trains run as far as Irkutsk; from here, only the evacuation of convalescents or medical patients takes place.

The hospital trains outfitted through the generosity of Their Majesties and of individual princes or corporations are said to be splendidly equipped, whereas those of the Red Cross as well as the military hospital trains consist merely of improvisations from freight and passenger cars respectively.

Things are said to have been dreadful after the days of Liaoyang and Mukden. Often there was barely any straw for bedding; the wounded simply had to be crammed into the cars as quickly as possible, and one was glad to find enough time at the stops to remove those who had died along the way. Now, thank God, it has become quieter, and we can attend to the patients in greater detail. The doctor was wearing a white work coat splattered with fresh blood. “What, do major operations still take place now?”—“They are mostly internal suppurations caused by remaining projectile fragments or bone splinters, which necessitate reopening.” From his purse he took a shrapnel ball that he had just removed from a man’s shoulder blade.

“My colleagues in the field hospitals do not have enough time,” he explained to me, “and here we often face cases where the wounds have scarred over beautifully, but the man complains of terrible internal pain. I extracted this bullet jacket from a patient, for which almost the entire pelvis had to be opened.” The bullet jacket he showed me resembled a coiled spiral, with a piece of the bullet base still jammed into its upper end. What terrible pain the poor man must have suffered with this thing in his bones for two months! We came to the patient, who lay pale and lifeless in bed after enduring the operation. A Red Cross sister was sitting with him. These young ladies—they are exclusively girls from good families—do not belong to any religious order, but have completed a two-year nursing course in Petersburg, Moscow, or other larger Russian cities, after which they are assigned to auxiliary service in the hospitals.

[[p48]]

Usually they receive free board and thirty rubles a month—certainly not much in a country where the ruble has the purchasing power of a crown! In this hospital of the Empress, the young ladies nursing have renounced all remuneration for their services. I could not conceal from the doctor my admiration for his undertaking, far from home, such a task that consumes both physical and moral strength!

In the hospital lay nearly two hundred patients. Most wore the Cross of St. George or another reward for their bravery pinned to their shirts; in exchange, however, they had had to give up their arms or legs. Many amputations had been performed—almost everywhere bullets had struck at long ranges and the impact had been not straight on, but sideways.
