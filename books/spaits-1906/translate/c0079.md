Just north of Tesindsa, the Cossack Dzhenik came toward us and reported that south of the village of Tesindsa there was a “pereval”—pass—held by the Japanese. At the same time, two other Cossacks arrived and reported that two Japanese companies were advancing toward the heights southwest of the said locality. “And this village here, Tesinda?” “It is still clear of the Japanese—but we had better not go in,” the Cossacks said.

The pereval, clearing the Japanese from which was our immediate objective, lay a little more than a thousand paces south ahead of us; the path there led up a broad, gently rising slope. To our left were steep heights covered with scrub; to our right, the advance of two companies had been reported. We had to finish off the Japanese at the pereval before they struck our right flank.

Any other Cossack commander would certainly have tried his luck again with the famous *lava*—an attack by cavalry detachments dispersed in swarms—only to realize, after several unsuccessful charges, that cavalry, surprise attacks aside, is powerless against intact infantry detachments, and especially so when they have dug themselves into a defensive position.

Dolgorukov had drawn lessons from several such botched cavalry charges and decided that the infantry should bypass the pereval. With the cavalry, which could not have made any headway on the steep heights anyway, he intended to dismount for a firefight and keep firing at the enemy up on the pereval until shots from our own infantry could be heard, whereupon he intended to have them mount and charge.

[[p271]]

It was truly gratifying to find someone at last who devised a battle plan; after all, one had grown accustomed to seeing only aimless advances that bounced off the first—naturally always “unforeseen”—obstacle and came to nothing.

It was a wonderful morning, the sun shone in southern splendor, for the first time in ages a few green trees could be seen on the mountain slopes, and the wind had also finally ceased blowing. The guns to the right in the mountains and from the Japanese over there did boom more sharply than the blank rounds of peacetime exercises, yet the day was so beautiful, so peaceful, the men were so calm and indifferent, that I kept thinking I had a maneuver before me in which Esaul Dolgorukov had to demonstrate his fitness to become a staff officer. From Tesinda, too, the Chinese were now coming out, curious to see what would happen.

In a tone more intimate than commanding, Dolgorukov communicated his plan to the commander of the Argun Cossacks and to the infantry captain, who also seemed to agree with it—that Dolgorukov was the cleverest among them they had to feel for themselves; soon the infantry had vanished into the scrub of the heights to our left.

Now, however, from the commander to his men, the Cossacks! I myself have disparaged them much in this book already and will do so again later—and yet I must acknowledge that these small, daring horsemen have in them the makings of the finest soldiers! Once again I saw how the men burned with desire to come to grips with the enemy at last—an enemy whose position they knew; not like yesterday, when the carelessness of their commander had exposed them to the bullets of an unseen foe.

No one had paid any attention to the sotnias standing a few hundred paces behind us, no one had given them an order, and already they scented the enemy on the heights and were trying to get a clear picture of him. Right and left, individual horsemen galloped forward, skillfully taking cover along the steep valley wall or in depressions in the ground, but in front of the village, too, there was suddenly shouting. Four Cossacks [[p272]] had crept around the Chinese and now held in their midst a stout man with a long queue, who was shouting and gesticulating violently.

Why had I sent away both of my cameras yesterday! This morning would have compensated me for all the dreary days during which my cameras had had to lie idle. The fat Chinese whom the four Cossacks were now dragging back amid the screaming and howling of the whole village rabble would have made an excellent picture! Our Cossacks know their way around the Chinese here; the fat fellow must serve them

as a hostage to ensure that no Hunhutzes fall upon our flank from the village.

“Two Japanese squadrons, Esaul, are stationed up on the mountain,” the Cossacks reported to their commander, which was what they had learned from the Chinese and what the horsemen who had ridden ahead confirmed soon afterward.

In a broad line we now advanced at a sharp trot, off the road across stubble fields. I gazed intently at the pereval, but could see no enemy.

Suddenly a thin white cloud rises along the crest, followed immediately by the sharp crack of a rifle volley. [[p273]] -- Halt! -- No further command was given. The horses were left behind in groups. Those holding them struck them on the shins with the handles of their knouts, and the animals lay down. I tried the same with my white horse, which, however, instead of lying down, began striking out at me with its forelegs. Stupid beast! If you want to get shot, stay standing and show your white coat to the Japanese, I will not keep you company! and, holding the white horse by the lengthened reins, I lay down between the furrows of the field.
