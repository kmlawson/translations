# The Lull in Operations until the Battle of Mukden.

[[p110]]

## Life in the Russian Positions.

After Sandepu, all had become quiet once again. Every train arriving from the north brought fresh troops—it was already openly admitted that they had to be *twice as strong* as the Japanese to be able to engage them with any prospect of success. All Mukden was one vast hospital, from which the wounded, as they gradually became fit for transport, were evacuated to the rear. General Mishchenko also lay wounded here.

My first visits in Mukden were to the chief of censorship, Colonel Pestich of the General Staff, and to the now historic railway train in which the military attachés lived. It had been shunted onto a siding next to the station and consisted of two elegant first-class Pullman cars and a dining car, in which the attachés were the guests of General Kuropatkin three times a day.

The eye soon grew accustomed to the colorful hustle and bustle of Mukden, with its constantly shifting scenes of European and Asian local color. Tempting as it would have been to linger here longer in order to penetrate that foreign world unfolding in all its originality, to visit the lama temples, the imperial tombs, and all the other sights of ancient Manchu culture, such studies were, after all, not the main purpose of my being here. No less tempting was the pleasant life in the railway carriage, where, through the kindness of the Russian General Staff officers assigned to the attachés, a half-compartment had been placed at my disposal for my temporary stay. Most of the foreign officers were in Mukden at that time; a few, the so-called "diligent ones"—among them the two Austro-Hungarian [[p112]] attachés—had, however, remained out in the positions, so that I felt quite lonely amid this great international bustle.

Probably never in my life, neither before nor since, have I felt the sentiment of gratitude, joy, and belonging so deeply as on that morning when Lieutenant Colonel Csicserics opened my compartment door and greeted me—a total stranger to him until then—here in a foreign land with that warmth and genuinely fraternal manner that has—not yet died out in our army. How comforting and familiar the comrades' informal "Du" sounded back then, whose true, inner worth we at home are often not even conscious of.

[Illustration: Main street of Mukden.]

With delight I accepted his invitation to accompany him out to the positions the following day, on which occasion we also intended to visit our comrade Captain Count Szeptycki, who lived out in Kouloutsi at the headquarters of General Bilderling (17th Army Corps).

Early in the morning in biting cold—it may have been around 22° R—we rode away from Mukden. Dense clouds of yellow dust whirled across the plain from the direction of the Hunhe. Above us was the magnificent, deep blue sky of the south, and whenever the icy north wind ran out of breath for a brief moment, one could even feel the warm rays of the sun.

[[p113]]

We pass the Mukden bridgehead with its entrenchments and batteries. "Hide your camera," Csicserics warns, "the Russians do not take kindly to amateur photographers." Poor art of light! Too much honor is paid to you when you are credited with vital services in betraying military secrets! I consider the value of photography in espionage to be a fable believed purely out of tradition. Just tell me, after all, what more precise data could an admittedly questionable photograph provide during the reconnaissance of a railway or a fortification than what I inconspicuously enter in my notebook—or perhaps commit to memory for the time being—on the basis of observations made with an expert eye, whether naked or aided? To note how many tracks a station has, how long a loading ramp is, how high an elevator, how many embrasures and the like, it is hardly necessary to photograph the things in question!

Further south we pass the headquarters of the commander-in-chief. With a certain awe I approach the place from which Russia's destiny for times to come will be decided. How little has a commander learned compared to other scholars! What a simple, one might almost say elementary, science are tactics and that bit of strategy compared to medicine, astronomy, and so forth. And yet there has never been anyone in the world—with the exception of the teachers of new religions—be they inventor, statesman, or scholar, who has intervened so forcefully in the machinery of world history, who has so thoroughly redrawn the maps of continents, as the victorious commander with his bit of strategy!

Not far from Mukden is a railway station, Sujiatun. A siding branches off from the main line, and on it stands a short train. Two locomotives, one facing forward, the other backward, and a few elegant Pullman cars. A carpeted flight of steps leads up to the last carriage; two lampposts are driven into the ground before it. All around the train, a large perimeter is likewise enclosed by lampposts, with sentries stationed at its entrances. From here the commander-in-chief directs his [[p114]] armies by electrical wires. The commander speaks a word into the telephone, and hundreds upon hundreds of thousands rise from the trenches, crawl out of the dugouts, leave the hospitals half-healed, to throw themselves upon the enemy. But at that time, the commander did not speak this word into the telephone—nor did he later.
