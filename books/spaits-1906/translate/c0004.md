It had been a hard day for us; we had been on our feet since early morning and had eaten nothing yet. I felt hungry and went into the officers' mess, where the Easter table was still set. As I entered, I saw the Admiral standing quietly and pensively in the room. Around the table stood seven or eight officers, drinking tea and discussing [[p18]] the events of the day. Tired as I was, I sat down on a sofa; next to me sat an engineer officer.

We were two miles from the port; on deck, preparations were already being made to drop anchor. Suddenly, I felt as if the sofa I was sitting on were falling through the floor, and immediately afterwards the colossal ship shuddered and groaned in every joint.

“A mine!” flashed through my mind at once, and I ran up onto the deck. The deck swayed and buckled beneath my feet. On the way to the tween deck, a second terrible shock—our own mine magazine had exploded. Hot billows of smoke poured into the upper officers' quarters; red flames leaped toward me from the battery deck. Groping, I found the second companionway, where a crowd, already unrecognizable to me through the smoke and fire fumes, was crammed before a half-open battle hatch. I pushed my way through and gave the order to open the hatch from the outside.

Now I could breathe and see again. The sight was terrible. The entire ship was listing to port, the propeller thrashing in the air. A mighty column of fire rose from the spar deck and swept, hissing and crackling, across the upper deck, which was already rearing skyward. Masts, funnels, everything crashed down in confusion. I remember seeing Grand Duke Cyril on the conning bridge. I looked around for a lifebelt, but saw none; next to me was the accommodation ladder, and I lowered myself down to the water. Our priest was already battling the waves; a few times he spun around in a circle, then a vortex dragged him down into the depths; immediately afterwards, a wave swept me far away. Foaming water washed over me; the instinct to survive gave me strength, but rapid blows from hard objects in quick succession to my head knocked me senseless for a moment. Suddenly it grew lighter; I felt myself carried to the surface and drifted upon the foaming waters. The waves grew calmer; I looked around, but in vain: the proud 'Petropavlovsk' had vanished. Beams, pieces of wreckage, an overturned boat to which several men were clinging—that was all there was to be seen.

I slipped several of the floating pieces of wood under [[p19]] my arm and tried to pull off my shoes, but soon I felt violent pain and my strength waned. I remember seeing the Grand Duke beside me again at that moment; he was wearing an overcoat and cap and clinging to a shattered steam launch. My body began to grow numb; every second seemed an eternity to me; for fifteen minutes I had to keep myself afloat like that, then the cutter 'Gaidamak' took me to the torpedo boat 'Bezshumny', where first aid was administered to me. Debris had severely injured my head, particularly my ear. Taken ashore, I had breakfast in the railway carriage of the Grand Duke, who had suffered severe burns to his hands and feet. The next day I was still able to attend the memorial service, but then I collapsed. After being bedridden for two months, I was brought unconscious to Harbin, to the Moscow branch of the Red Cross, where cranial surgery was performed on me.”

To my question as to how, despite the Russian precautionary measures described at the outset, the Japanese had managed to lay that fateful mine in the immediate vicinity of the harbour, Herr von Schlippe remarked to me that this could only have happened during the engagement of the torpedo boats that had likewise already been discussed.

Our conversation naturally turned also to the Baltic Fleet. Its potential future pivot point, Vladivostok, is by no means impracticable in winter for the entrance and exit of large ships, as is generally assumed. The sea itself is ice-free, and the ice in the harbour is never so thick that the fleet's icebreakers could not establish communication with the open sea.

At that time, the articles by “Captain Klado” were creating a great sensation both in Russia and in the foreign press. The Second Squadron, which, having finally put to sea after months of trials, had immediately made a tragicomic laughingstock of itself at Hull, was at that moment still pinned down in Vigo and Tangier, and already people were speaking of a third squadron.

This squadron will—and indeed must—depart, so it was said, if [[p20]] Russia does not want to have its entire naval power destroyed piecemeal in the Far East. People here had already arrived at the realization that Rozhestvensky must confront Togo not as soon as possible, but *as strongly as possible*. Recent days had proven that he could count only on the forces he brought with him; but according to a table drawn up in September of this year by a high authority, this balance of forces stood none too favorably for the Russian admiral. Specifically, according to the compilation—excluding torpedo boats—15 Russian ships faced 33 Japanese, 95,200 tons against 216,000, 703 guns against 941. While this ratio was probably no longer entirely accurate at the time, since several more ships had been assigned to the Baltic Squadron after the month of September, and on the other hand Japan had also lost ships during this period, this circumstance cannot alter the fact that the forces placed under Admiral Rozhestvensky up to that point were bound to be too weak to fight for and secure lasting command of the sea in Japanese waters.
