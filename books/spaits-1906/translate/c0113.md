Through his endless “keeping people waiting”, the official educates the people in wasting time. Through the system of corruption in all offices and courts, faith in earthly justice cannot but vanish. Religion and trust in God were certainly a mighty support for the Russian army in this war, but since the priest, too, does not teach fulfillment of duty and love of truth, but instead ensures that his flock does not awake from its thoughtless cult of icons, and does not consider evil to be evil so long as it goes unpunished, the lie cannot be eradicated. Yes, the lie! It is nurtured in the Russian Empire, among high and low, poor and rich! How could it fail to have taken root in the army as well? All too often it struts through orders, dispatches, and reports! At one moment timidly modest in the patrol report of an officer who reports as “seen with his own eyes” what a Chinese told him for a few rubles; at another joyfully jubilant in the hurrahs of detrained soldiers in Harbin, who were informed in the order of the day that Rozhestvensky had blasted the Japanese to the bottom of the sea; at another haughtily proud when General Linevich telegraphs of offensive intentions—and here too it is neither malice nor the intention to speak untruth, but merely *the lack of any feeling for the value of truth.*

Yet it must also be admitted that in these harmful psychological factors discussed so far there lies, on the other hand, such a sustaining power for the Russian army—to which it is thanks that, despite nineteen months of failures, it was not yet demoralized (for this, contrary to many accounts on the subject, should likewise be stated here)—which made it possible that, despite the critical situation in which the Manchurian army already found itself, the *conclusion of peace for Russia*, viewed from a *purely military standpoint*, was still [[p376]] by no means a *compelling necessity*. Much was said of the great panics following the Battle of Mukden—panic never lasted long among the Russians, for they are too apathetic for that. On the night of March 9–10, when Mukden was evacuated under the impact of Japanese shrapnel and enemy cavalry broke into the baggage trains in the pitch-dark night, there was indeed a scene of the most dreadful confusion and all formations dissolved. After a few days, however, tempers had already calmed and order was perhaps not restored only because little store had ever been set by it in the first place.

The greatest disappointment in this war was surely prepared for us by the cavalry—the famous Russian cavalry. To be sure, of its regular army cavalry Russia had no more than three dragoon regiments at the theatre of war; all the rest were Cossacks—though these, after all, also belong in part to the regular cavalry; but even with the irregular second-line regiments more could have been achieved than actually was.

[Illustration: Circum-Baikal Railway.] [[p377]] paign, voices were often raised that sought to deny this arm any value at all. Without going into this question more closely here, it suffices for the present to point out that it was precisely the failure of the cavalry reconnaissance and reporting service among the Russians that led to the most mistaken dispositions in both the strategic and tactical spheres, and that the lack of adequate battle cavalry among the Japanese made it impossible for them to exploit their success in all their victories.

What would have become of the Russian Manchurian army if one or even several well-organized divisions of modern cavalry had been at the disposal of the Japanese after the Battle of Mukden? Yet cavalry divisions would not even have been necessary here—intact cavalry regiments on March 10 and 11 would have sufficed to drive entire Russian corps in any direction they pleased; the war would have been over.

It is not the lack of necessity, but on the contrary the eminent importance and indispensability of cavalry that this war has proved—while also showing, however, that even masses of cavalry are useless if their commanders are not inspired by drive and energy, and their officers not tactically trained.

Besides military training, however, the Russian cavalry above all lacked the firm will *to sacrifice itself*—and that was perhaps its greatest failing!

Discipline had been maintained in the ranks of the men right to the end. Yet the discipline of the Russian soldier is less the result of his military education—his officer cares little for him—than of the innate religious feeling of devotion inherent to the man. The only sustaining element common to the entire army, preserving it even in the darkest days from moral disintegration, was religion! And if one were today to take away the faith of the Russian people before providing them with a substitute in education and enlightenment, little indeed would remain of the moral worth of the Russian army! In this war [[p378]] it was faith in a better future that unshakeably animated officer and man.
