"The war must last two more years, then in the end we will prevail after all," the officers said after Mukden; "what is the little bit of land from Port Arthur to Gongzhuling that we have evacuated compared to our Russia, which stretches from the Baltic Sea to the Pacific Ocean! And are we beaten, destroyed? No, after all, we are still here! And when we are no more, other hundreds of thousands will come!" To be sure, such a way of thinking does not conform to modern concepts of warfare. Yet it imparts to the whole people that passive tenacity to which the Russians ultimately owed a great success: that of the conclusion of peace.

Yet there will be no peace in the land of the Russians until the people have shaken off the fetters that hinder their intellectual and economic development.—And these fetters will fall! For no system and no law is strong enough to sever the vital artery of a healthy people. When the autocratic regime, together with the falsehood, corruption, and patronage in the Tsarist Empire, has outlived itself, then a modern citizen army will arise there as well—for the protection of its ruler, its country, and its laws, strong and formidable, as befits a mighty people.
