All the higher commands were connected with one another laterally and to the rear by telegraph, and in some cases also by telephone; strong posts guarded the lines of communication, wells, and other important installations. The entire area behind the foremost positions revealed the foresight of a commander who had established here a base for later operations—only this "later" must not be postponed too far. At the end of March the thaw usually sets in here, [[p128]] transforming the entire plain into a bottomless quagmire in which light railways and other technical installations must sink without a trace.

For the time being, however, nothing seemed to indicate that anyone intended to shake themselves out of the operational inactivity into which they had fallen after the Battle of Sandepu anytime soon.

Above Putilov Hill—known from the newspapers as the "Hill with the Three Trees"—the last little white puffs of Japanese shrapnel had vanished into the grey haze that hung leaden over the plain in the evening. From the mountains came the lingering echo of isolated shots, then all around grew quiet. In the fields, individual soldiers from the nearby dugouts were pottering about, weeding out gaoliang roots or gathering dried dung—fuel for their cooking fires. We rode on eastward along the hard-frozen military road toward Huangshan, the headquarters of General-Adjutant Linevich, then commander of the First Russian Manchurian Army.

Huangshan lies at the foot of the heights that push forward from here to the south and east with moderate elevations, yet sharply defined contours. Just beyond the village, a narrow mountain ridge rises steeply, with a temple standing on its summit. Still further south, near Erdagou, visible from afar, a tall stone tower was built by the Chinese—to ward off evil spirits. From up there, the land is visible as far as the Liaohe and even further west; to the southeast lies the valley of the Shahe, here already hemmed in by steep mountains.

[Illustration: Main guardhouse in Huangshan.]

Here, in this space that was by no means overly vast for mass armies, the two adversaries had been facing each other since mid-October. Only a practiced eye with good binoculars could spot from here the Russian positions, which, cleverly concealed in the terrain, extended along the mountains, along the Shahe, across the Mandarin Road [[p129]] and the railway, as far as the Liaohe. Little was to be seen of the Japanese. Anyone looking at the landscape lying before them, calm and peaceful, the fields still well-tended from the autumn sowing, could scarcely believe that there was a war going on here.

Riding through the village of Mazatun, headquarters of the 1st European Army Corps, we halted outside the quarters of the German attaché, Major Runkel. He occupied one half of a *fangzi*; in the other room an office of the corps general staff section was housed. Major Runkel was visibly pleased by Lieutenant-Colonel von Csicserics's visit, pulled out his notebook, and shared in a whisper what he had recently learned about Russian troop movements. His notes indicated a *shifting of forces toward the western wing*. Apart from that, he complained about the more than reserved attitude of the Russian officers toward him, offered us tea, and promised to come to Huangshan soon.

Dusk was already falling when we rode into Huangshan. Outside General Linevich's quarters lay strange equipment. There were various assault implements, such as portable assault bridges made of timber and gaoliang mats, as well as chevaux-de-frise of various sizes and several large, thick rollers made of wooden planks, whose purpose was not clear to us. Everything was lined up neatly side by side, apparently for inspection by the general.

"It does seem as though things will finally get underway," remarked Lieutenant-Colonel von Csicserics; "perhaps they will try it from the left wing this time; it is high time!" Soon we reached the liquor distillery in which those military attachés who had attached themselves to the headquarters of the First Army were quartered.

In principle, foreign military attachés were free to choose a command with which they wished to follow the operations. To be sure, this choice was not always accepted; instead, the army high command would assign the attaché in question a corps whose headquarters he might join. Such cases were rare, however; for the most part, the foreign officers could go wherever they wished. They usually preferred a command whose [[p130]] commander or chief of staff they were on somewhat closer terms with as a result of prior acquaintance.
