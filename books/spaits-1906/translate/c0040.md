## On the Military Attachés' Train.

Being able to observe the colorful pageant of wartime life from the window of a Pullman car is certainly an achievement of the age of the iron horse!

Never before had the railway played so vital a role for an army in the field as it did here for the Russian Manchurian Army, for which the trans-Asian line of rails was not only the lifeline funneling troops and materiel to the army, but to whose individual field armies branch lines also led, at whose railheads—much as we saw with the Army High Command—an elegant train of several carriages was likewise placed at the disposal of each respective army command.

In Mukden and further to the rear in the lines of communication area, railway carriages were used as permanent quarters to the extent that they could be spared for that purpose. Chanceries, senior officers, the editorial office of the *Mandzhursky Vestnik* [Manchurian Herald],¹ the retail stores of the "economic societies" that brought supplies for officers and men from Russia to Manchuria, were all housed in carriages, which ensured their constant readiness to move—unfortunately, almost always only for retreat. It was therefore only natural that the home of the military attachés was likewise on a train, which stood at a discreet distance behind the zone of operations and, in the event of reverses, quickly steamed off northwards to bring the foreign guests, entrusted to the protection of the Russian flag, to safety.

Russian railway tracks are broader than those of all other states, the carriages accordingly larger and more comfortable. [[p141]] If Russia is generally superior to its western neighbors in the practical and elegant appointments of its carriages, this is especially true of the Chinese Eastern Railway,¹ whose sleeping and dining cars are certain to satisfy even the most discerning traveler. Two such carriages, a dining car, and two cars for luggage and servants formed that international island where one could live in comfort amid the hardships and privations of war, where physical well-being was sheltered from Japanese bullets and property from Chinese and other sticky fingers.

A practical man soon learns to make himself at home in the most confined space, and so the compartments and half-compartments assigned to the attachés were certainly the safest and most comfortable quarters for this restless wartime existence. The elegant train in which the foreign agents followed operations in Manchuria may perhaps set a precedent for the future. In a future campaign, the belligerents will surely place such a train at the disposal of their official spectators once again; for the latter, this will be a great convenience, and no less so for the army high commands concerned. Concern for the safety of foreign officers, and no less the precautions against their curiosity, will in future cause the commander-in-chief little headache, provided that in a coming war the two adversaries admit foreign officers—whether official or not—at all! Should the proximity of the enemy become dangerous, or conditions arise in one's own army that make the presence of foreign witnesses undesirable, a nod to the engine driver will suffice to send the distinguished guests traveling off in the opposite direction from operations. Any military thirst for knowledge flaring up on such occasions among the attachés rolling about in the rear lines of communication during major battles must then be suppressed by especially fine cuisine and exquisite drinks in the dining car [[p142]] . The odd ambitious striver who resists such philanthropic arrangements—who is not content to construct military history from a railway car, but, disregarding his own physical well-being, wishes to gain a firsthand view of the firing line—will simply be sent packing home. In a future war, however, one would certainly prefer to exchange even such summary procedures against attachés for a polite and total refusal to receive them, were it not that their presence must be tolerated if only out of considerations of reciprocity, and did cases not frequently arise during a war—such as violations of neutrality, the Geneva Convention, and the like—whose impartial investigation requires the participation of disinterested neutral observers.
