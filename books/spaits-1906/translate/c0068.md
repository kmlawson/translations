One battalion after another came along the valley road that I had ridden yesterday, entering Padsjaza, stacked arms here in pyramids, and waited until they should be sent into the mountains. In this manner there arrived one after another two battalions of the 72nd Division and one battalion each of the 71st Division and the 10th East Siberian Regiment, as well as detachments of the 286th Kirsanov, 288th Kulikov, 7th Krasnoyarsk, and 287th Tarusa Regiments.

The field kitchens came driving up, and the men, quietly [[p235]] and in the finest order, formed up around them in radiating ranks. When they were all gathered, they sang a brief grace, then the man standing atop the field kitchen lifted the lid from the copper and, with a large ladle, filled each outstretched mess tin. Standing, the soldiers slurped down the hot cabbage soup, for which they then thanked the good Lord with three crossings of themselves.

[Illustration: In the Gaotuling Pass]

There can hardly be a soldier in the world more undemanding and enduring than the Russian. What a pity about this splendid material!

Past the battalions moved the long column of stretchers and wounded being taken to the field hospital. On the way, the dressings were beginning to stain red, and on the canvas of the stretchers were large patches of blood.

Soldiers were now standing around one stretcher. The man lying on it was dead, his hands hanging limp. The men seemed to have known him and were searching through his [[p236]] pockets for letters. He was from a reserve division. The bullet had struck him square in the forehead. I wanted to photograph the group, but the men noticed me and dutifully snapped to attention. “What are you looking for in his pockets?” “His wife’s name—I want to write to her that she shouldn’t wait for her husband any longer; she’ll never hear it from the ‘orderly room’ anyway!”

Among the 72nd Division, the men began winding the woolen scarf around the black papakha. Why this monstrosity was put on the poor fellows’ heads? A fur cap with ear flaps, such as the Buryats and Chinese wear, is far more practical than this tall grenadier cap. But the Japanese are as little impressed by this absurdity as by the daggers, sewn-on cartridge pouches, and other frippery belonging to the theatrical costume of the Caucasians—who, for the rest, are better brigands than good soldiers. When moving against the enemy, the black cap had to be wrapped with the grey bashlyk so as not to stand out too conspicuously against the white snow.

In the mountains it crackled and rattled again with renewed violence; overhead in the sky shrapnel shells wailed and hissed, their little white clouds hanging over the Russian batteries.

Toward noon, a battalion of the 72nd Division, with blue shoulder straps, went forward as a reinforcement to the position south of Padsjaza.

The battalion stood ready to march. The commander had the companies form a square and addressed the men simply and briefly: they should think of God and the Tsar, then they would have nothing to fear from shrapnel and shimose—everyone had to die sometime, after all.

In conclusion, he took off his papakha and began to recite the Lord’s Prayer. The men prayed with him. Then arms were shouldered and they marched off. Every single man, before taking the first step, took off his cap once more and crossed himself.

I followed as well. How dispiriting it is for troops marching into battle when the dead and dying are carried [[p237]] past them to the rear! How anxiously the men’s eyes followed the groaning wounded or mutilated corpses carried right past their ranks. Where was the ringing, metallic singing innate to the throats of these men now? Their throats were constricted—just like their hearts—the men felt that they were going to their deaths.

From the observation post of a battery, whose officer I had met through the artillery brigadier, I was able to follow the battalion’s further fate. It had to occupy a long trench—a rallying position—into which Russian troops from farther ahead were just then surging back.

The Japanese seemed to have succeeded in emplacing guns here that enfiladed the forward Russian fortified lines, for clouds of white smoke and black earth billowing up along them showed the terrible effect of the Japanese batteries, which rendered any further resistance by the infantry impossible. But the ground up to the next rallying position, and a good distance behind it, was like a hot stone slab onto which cold water is poured, so thickly did the earth billow and swirl up from the densely falling shells!

And are there really men, troops, who will walk into this fire, storming forward in this hail of bullets? “Yes! The Japanese—they can endure it!” the artillery officer told me—“but not by day either, only by night. By day one moves, just as our battalion did just now, through a prepared communication trench into the fortified line and pours fire into one another from here until evening; *to attempt to push closed formations forward across this ground swept by enemy fire would be madness—even the Japanese bring up their reserves only in loose swarms.*”
