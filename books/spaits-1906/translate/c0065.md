## Fighting with the Third Siberian Corps.

The tranquil life at the 2nd East Siberian Mortar Ammunition Park in Xiaoyanxintun had been disrupted by a Shimose shell. It had struck early in the morning; the men came running together to look at the enormous brown cloud of earth that, whirled up tower-high, stood over the ground. Slowly the dust settled, and the soldiers went to scatter gaoliang before their horses. Ivan Ivanovich, the ordnance sergeant, was just having the loaded ammunition carts brought down from the hill; now they stood right next to our fanza. Yet life in it was far too cozy to abandon it at the very first shot—besides, it had not been aimed at the park at all, but at the battery up on the mountain. At midday, word went round that the situation before the Third Corps, which stood to the east of the Second, had improved; the Japanese had taken possession of three heights there during the night, but the third had been recaptured by the Russians. That the good people never want to admit their failures straight out! An improvement in the situation, when instead of three sopkas only two have been lost!

With the Second Siberian, everything still remained fairly quiet on 26 February. It was believed that the great offensive would begin in a few days. General Linevich had promised to cut off the three Japanese divisions advancing under Kuroki against the Russian left flank from the center of the Japanese army by a bold thrust. As we learned, on this day he had driven with his inner staff from Huangshan to Shihuicheng.

That such a thrust always begins with a step backward! I thought to myself, since Shihuicheng lies further north than Huangshan.

[[p225]]

The First Siberian Corps as well, which only a few days before had stood on the western flank, was said to have arrived in Shihuicheng on this day—elements of the Sixth Corps, too, had marched eastward from the center the day before; so it was now determined that the Russian offensive this time would begin not with a strong right flank, as had initially been thought, but with a strong left.

To be sure of the matter, I went at noon to the headquarters of the Second Siberian Corps, where General Pappengut, the corps chief of staff, received me very cordially. He introduced me to Lieutenant General Zasulich, a somewhat corpulent gentleman who at the time on the Yalu had opened the campaign none too auspiciously. Here I learned that General Rennenkampf, too, had returned from the extreme right flank back to the extreme left in order to take back command from his successor, General Alekseyev. One of the gentlemen here had the day before been an earwitness to a telephone conversation that Lieutenant General Linevich had held with Alekseyev. Linevich was said to have been furious over the headlong abandonment of the positions that Alekseyev had yielded to the Japanese at their first assault, and not to have minced his words on the telephone. In conclusion, he had advised Alekseyev to look for a position with the Red Cross and had stripped him of the command of the Western Detachment.

“Would you not like me to send a few Cossacks with you on your way?” General Pappengut asked me courteously. “Thank you, General, I hope to reach Shandalingshan by tomorrow evening, and there I have acquaintances who will help me further.”

In a wretched snowstorm, I left the hospitable house of the brave bombardiers the following day. Since then I have never seen them again. In the turmoil of the great retreat, some two weeks later, I saw mortars driving among other wagons, shrouded in dust. Perhaps they were the East Siberian ones. In the tangle of men and wagons that surged past me then, they were soon lost to sight.

Along the positions that stretched eastward from Kangdalingshan from sopka [[p226]] to sopka, it was almost completely quiet. I rode down in the valley and encountered here only one battery, which was in position for indirect fire. Its commander sent an officer after me who invited me to linger with them for a while, and then provided me with two Cossacks who escorted me to the 2nd Siberian Infantry Brigade.

Again we rode alongside the telegraph line, whose wires whirred and sang in the wind. Had I understood the language of the wire, I would also have been able to explain to myself why supply train wagons of the First Siberian Corps were coming toward me, marching westward.

As we already know, the sorely tried First Siberian Corps had on 24 February again stood ready to attack Sandepu. On the same day, however, the Japanese had attacked in the mountains themselves—and the Russian commander-in-chief yielded the initiative to them; the attack against Sandepu was called off, the First Siberian sent to Shihuicheng on the *extreme left flank*, only to be *reassigned back to Mukden* a few days later. But of all this I knew nothing at the time! In firm confidence in old Linevich’s “display of might,” I rode on eastward and after half an hour arrived at the 2nd Siberian Infantry Brigade. This time it was a fine, spacious dugout in which the staff officers were housed. The regimental adjutant, who here simultaneously performed the duties of brigade general staff officer, led me to the bedside of his severely wounded father, commander of one of the two regiments whose dugouts stood in the valley.
