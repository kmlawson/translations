### The Breakthrough.

Toward evening, however, Japanese columns appeared in the north, in Daxintun, and what was far worse—*in the southeast of Mukden: the Japanese had broken through the center, which was falling back to the Hunhe and had been weakened still further by the transfer of troops to Dawa*. Mukden was thereby lost. At the beginning of the battle, precautions had been taken against a Japanese turning movement around their own left flank; then seeing their right flank threatened, the center of gravity was shifted to the latter, in the course of which the center was so severely weakened that the enemy finally succeeded in achieving a *breakthrough*.

At 8 o'clock in the evening, army headquarters was forced to order the *immediate evacuation* of Mukden.

A complete debacle of the army was prevented partly by the bridgehead fortifications south of Mukden and partly by the fact that the *evacuation of Mukden* had already begun quietly on March 2.

The Third Army was still able to hold out in the bridgehead south of Mukden; the First Army naturally now had to abandon the Hunhe [[p326]] and withdraw to the Fanhe line in order to secure *Tieling*, the main staging and railway station in the army's rear.

General *Rennenkampf*, whose energy in these battles had helped the left wing through many a difficult situation, had fallen back to Sainpanj. With him was the division of General *Eck*, then to the east the Danilov and Maslov groups, and on the extreme left wing at Wangaro General Lyubavin with the 2nd Chita and 2nd Argun Cossack Regiments and five sotnias of the Amur and Nerchinsk Cossack Regiments.

## Yingpan and the Hunhe Are Abandoned.

With four Cossacks whose horses still had strength left, I rode on to the regimental commander to obtain orders for the detachment. As early as the morning of the 9th, that dreadful Manchurian wind had started up again in the mountains, and when we emerged into the Hunhe plain, it was raging here as a horrific dust storm. The air was saturated with brown sand, which the hurricane, gathering itself from time to time for a tremendous gust, swept across the plain in thick clouds. Tree branches, thatched roofs, and whatever else it found along the way were swept along in its raging vortex, and anyone who did not want to be dragged along held on to a tree, the corner of a house, or lay flat in a sheltering hollow. The horses would scarcely move forward. At every fresh gust of wind, they turned their croups to the howling elements and hid their heads between their forelegs.

On the way to the Hunhe, we encountered a retreating scouting detachment; in a village stood a rifle battalion, which knew nothing of the enemy. From the direction of Fushun rolled the thunder of cannon. Late in the afternoon, snow began to fall and the roar of the storm subsided, but all the louder now was the booming of the guns and the crackle of rifles carried over by the west wind from the broad Hunhe line.

A dense fog lay over the river, so that Yingpan, on the other bank, was difficult to find. The Cossacks tried several times to ride onto the ice, but the crust broke through each time, and the water was already deeper than the stirrups right near the bank. Riding upstream, we finally found a track that led to the eastern end of Yingpan. The place seemed large. Inside the lanes, a strange sight presented itself: a crowd of [[p330]] soldiers and wagons, all heavily laden with clothing, bread, canned food, and sacks of oats. One rider and his small nag were literally covered by a pile of padded drill smocks. Felt boots hung down on both sides of the horse's neck, and coats were tied behind the saddle, hanging down to the ground. This one was followed by many others who were similarly laden.

“Where is General Maslov’s staff headquarters?” “Here in the large fangzi, but the general already rode away in the afternoon.” In the courtyard of the abandoned house, the same superabundance reigned as in the street; a crowd of horses stood ankle-deep in oats, gorging themselves to bursting, tin cans lay strewn about the ground, and a pack of dogs was gnawing on a wheel of cheese. The regimental staff was a few versts north of Yingpan in Yaopu, so I was told by Colonel Radetsky, who was marching through Yingpan with the 7th Krasnoyarsk Independent Siberian Reserve Battalion.

After half an hour's rest, during which our horses had thoroughly gorged themselves, we rode on toward the north. From the southern edge of the town tall flames rose up, in which the thatched roofs of the houses crackled. The fangzis in the next lane were just being set on fire as we rode through.

Toward the northern exit of the town, the last arbas were driving out, heavily laden with sacks of oats and flour. In the village, however, many thousands of poods of supplies that could no longer be moved away had to be set on fire so that they would not fall into the hands of the enemy.

Just north of the village, on a rise, the Krasnoyarsk Battalion was encamped. Colonel Radetsky invited me to drink a cup of tea with his officers. Cheerful, we sat around an upturned crate and followed with our binoculars the engagement of a scouting detachment on the other bank of the Hunhe, east of us, which seemed to be firing at Japanese infantry on the opposite mountain slope. From the other bank of the Hunhe, from a southerly direction, close rifle fire could also be heard. “Those are our men!” said some. “No, those are Japanese!” said others. And while they were arguing whether it was friend or foe, bullets suddenly struck right in front of our tea crate.
