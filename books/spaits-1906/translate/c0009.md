## To Mukden.

*January 1905.*

There is something splendid about traveling. To speed through one and a half continents in flight, to see different regions and different people every day, and with it the refined comfort of the Trans-Siberian Express! I had left Petersburg after long waiting and hoping. It was on the evening of January 3, at a time when newspapers abroad were already carrying editorials on the fall of Port Arthur. Here people still believed that the foreign dispatches had exaggerated this time as well, for only on the following day was the event officially confirmed. In Moscow I was surprised by the fierce tone of the papers. The foreign newspapers, too, had been spared that black stamp which usually covers most of their columns. Was this supposed to signify a nascent freedom of the press, or were the Christmas holidays to blame, during which even the strict censorship grew weary of its duties?

Since Sunday evening I have been rolling toward the Far East, and the time when I shall see European newspapers again lies far ahead of me. From now on, the people themselves will be an open book to me, in which, if one observes attentively, a great deal can be read. The Russian is approachable and talkative. Recent events seem to have loosened his tongue, and he is particularly fond of speaking about them to strangers. The military situation, especially the fall of the fortress, is heatedly discussed, though rarely from a military standpoint, mostly more from a social one. A colorful company always gathers in the dining car. In Colonel K. of the artillery I shall have an excellent traveling companion; he is traveling with me as far as Mukden, where he will join the staff of the [[p36]] Third Army, under General Kaulbars, as chief of transport. An engineer from Vladivostok, who paid his respects to Admiral Rozhestvensky in Vigo, will accompany me as far as Harbin. He describes the admiral as a very calm man, though it seemed to him as if a certain nervousness lay concealed behind his reticence. “We were not mistaken!” he is said to have remark regarding the Hull incident.

Among the gentlemen, a gigantic Caucasian in national dress also catches my eye, for whom even the high doors of the Siberian railway are too low. We have few ladies on board. A merry little grandmother who, back during the Russo-Turkish War, had established a large hospital in Moscow out of her own means. This time the matron has set up a feeding station in Chelyabinsk and a hospital for fifty-six soldiers in Omsk. But entirely independently—she wants nothing to do with the Red Cross! Not only she, but many others as well, are seeking ways to direct charity straight to the right place, bypassing the collection points of the Red Cross, which has lost the trust of poor and rich alike. Much is told about this, as well as about the meager generosity of the wealthy monasteries toward the wounded, likewise about the famous blanket affair that took place between the well-known industrial magnate Savva Morozov and Grand Duke Sergei.

Other great lords who were involved in the timber speculations on the Yalu appear in an equally unfavorable light. People point to their greed and predatory policy as the real cause of Russia’s entanglement in this war. The war does not seem to be popular. Among the fellow passengers there is only one—a long-bearded timber merchant from Irkutsk, a genuine Russian type—who finds good in everything God and the Tsar have done. He crosses himself at every spoonful of soup and before every icon of Mary; he represents, so to speak, the war party.

Yet the day is short, and it is far more worthwhile to look out the window during the few daylight hours in order to imprint the ever-changing scenes upon one’s memory, to which [[p37]] my amateur cameras have so far rarely been able to come to the aid because of snowstorms and fog.

Early Tuesday morning we crossed the Volga bridge near Syzran. A monumental structure resting on twenty-eight piers. We crossed it slowly, taking about ten minutes. At the beginning of the war, Japanese officers wanted to blow it up; the daredevils were caught and hanged. Since then, platforms on which sentries stand have been erected on every pier. At Samara station I was shown a large barracks camp. The city built it for seventy thousand rubles; barely finished, it proved entirely unusable. It is said to have stood empty ever since.

[Illustration: Station in the Urals.]

On Wednesday we crossed the Urals; splendid winter scenery, accompanied by a snowstorm that whipped thin ice through the carriage doors. But we are quite splendidly provided for against such weather. The enclosed vestibule forms a kind of anteroom, from which two well-fitting doors lead into the heated corridor. Thanks to the holidays we have only five first-class passengers, so that each of us occupies a compartment to himself. When the electric table lamp is lit in the evening on the small table upon which the conductor has placed a glass of steaming tea, it sounds quite cozy behind the double-locked window as the storm outside sweeps across the vast snowfields. Then one's thoughts wander into the past and into the misty future. Time for that we have, indeed. With time one can no longer be so particular here anyway. The timetable is drawn up according to Petersburg time, yet our delay varies between five and seven hours. Local time also changes from day to day, so that the only reliable timepiece left is one’s own stomach. My other traveling companions seem to have plenty of time as well. The question [[p38]] of whether we arrive in Irkutsk on Sunday, Monday, or even Tuesday seems to affect them little.
