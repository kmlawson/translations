In the village, people and wagons were jammed together on the muddy road; before the bridge stood a line of stretchers with wounded that could not get across. But no one was loud or impatient.

At last, one managed to get out of the village. The sun had set, and only indistinctly could two opposing steep slopes be made out, between which the road continued toward the Gaotuling Pass. From there came the noise, in which the crackle of rapid rifle fire could now clearly be distinguished alongside the crash of artillery. It sounded very near; high in the air there was a howling and rushing, as though bullets were already whizzing overhead. In the twilight, flashes now came from the batteries stationed to the rear on the mountains, and rifle volleys also lit up now and then from the opposite mountainside. Pajiazi was full of officers.

[[p230]]

The commander of the Third Corps, Lieutenant General Ivanov himself, had come here with his immediate staff to follow the course of the engagement. Several battalions were stationed beside the village; one of them was just moving into the mountains. The newly arrived column occupied the abandoned campsite.

### The Fighting in the Gaotuling Pass.

South of Panjiazi the valley widens, only to close into a narrow pass between the two aforementioned steep slopes. Behind this, fighting was underway. It had grown almost dark; the firing lines of the infantry occupying the positions resembled trembling, glowing ribbons that fringed the crests of the five heights, which stood out black against the grayish-white firmament. Right by the sunken road, a Russian battery seemed to be stationed and delivering rapid-fire salvos.

The wounded were being brought back from there. In the darkness, not much could be distinguished: two-wheeled carts on which figures crouched huddled together, or stretchers carried by four men. “Look at this one,” Naval Lieutenant Camperio told me, whose tall figure I had soon found, holding his hand lantern over a stretcher that the bearers, stopping to rest, had set down on the ground beside us. On it was a man doubled up; he had been shot in the abdomen. Beside him, however, lay something completely misshapen: a corpse lacking the head and the entire back—“Shimose,” Camperio added by way of explanation. “Wouldn’t it be simpler to send the priest and the gravediggers further forward, rather than transport the corpses all the long way back? Whether he is buried here or there is all the same to the poor devil after all.”

For five days now the struggle for the Gaotuling Pass had been going on. Of the eight heights that lay to the south, the Japanese had already taken five, and indeed the highest ones. On the other three, however, the Russians held on with tenacity.

here at closest quarters the nerve-shattering surge of a battle in the mountains. As if from the chasms of hell, the rolling echo of the volleys thundered from the dark valleys, only to die away immediately afterward in the rapid-fire bursts of the nearby batteries. It seemed to me as though, through the crackle of the rifles, I could hear the voices of the combatants from the black mountain ridges. The air trembled in one great, wildly vibrating tone. And now the moon, smiling amiably as ever, rose behind the mountains. The fire of the skirmish lines paled in its wan light; all the more distinct against the greenish-yellow sky were the little clouds of exploding shrapnel and the swirling columns of Shimose smoke.

“We shall see this quite often yet,” Camperio told me, and led me to a nearby *zemlyanka*.

General Schwerin, who had most graciously invited us into his dugout, where he was staying with his officers during the battle, had just returned from the

[Illustration: Killed by a Shimose shell.] [[p234]] batteries to his dugout. It was an officers' *zemlyanka*, meaning that the entrance led through a small anteroom intended for the servant; this time, however, officers were glad if they could find room even here. In the *zemlyanka* it was cold; the wind whistled through the repeatedly opening door and the hole in the wall opposite, which, formerly covered with parchment paper, had served as a window. Straw had been scattered on the ground, but too little to prevent the chill of the frozen earth from being felt. On a crate stood tin mugs of tea, from which thin wisps curled up into the cold air. The soldiers had brought a bowl of cabbage soup, and General Schwerin invited us to join in. “*À la guerre, comme à la guerre*,” he remarked, giving us his silver cutlery while he himself tucked in heartily with a wooden soldier’s spoon. Throughout the night, the rattling of rifles continued without interruption. In the *zemlyanka*, too, sleep was hardly to be thought of. Reports arrived, orders had to be written, and then it was necessary again to drink a glass of hot tea to drive the chill from one's bones. How the soldiers must have frozen in their positions that night! I could not help thinking of the poor fellows who had splashed into the water that evening and were now drying their wet feet by a campfire somewhere in the open. In the morning, fresh troops arrived in Pajiazi. It was the 72nd Division of the Sixth Corps, which the day before had bivouacked north of Fanshen. But the Vyborg Regiment had also joined the Third Corps, and a regiment from the 1st Siberian had remained here, so that once again, happily, troops from four different corps were jumbled together in one place!
