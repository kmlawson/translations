## After the Retreat.

In our boxcar, upon the frozen sacks of flour—uninviting as they were—Beiningen and I did not remain alone for long. Already by the following morning, four other officers had discovered that there was still room here to get forward, and by the fourth day there were already twenty of us sitting beside and on top of one another. At the last stop before Gongzhuling—the engine driver did not know whether we would proceed today or perhaps not until tomorrow—I packed my saddlebags and horse blankets onto my back in order to cover the few versts on foot. The others, among whom were some who claimed to be billeting officers of the Third Army Headquarters, preferred to wait perhaps another twenty hours in the greasy boxcar rather than walk the short distance.

So I moved on northwards alongside the railway line. The field on either side was still strewn with solitary travellers, wagons, and troops of all arms, yet here some semblance of order did seem, after all, to be slowly returning to the detachments.

The sun was burning hot again, my fur coat was warm, and the horse blanket, the rucksack, the saddlebag, and on top of that the two cameras amounted, after only the first quarter of an hour, to a most uncomfortable burden. And what on earth would I do if the attachés' train had already left Gongzhuling as well?

Suddenly, to my astonishment, I hear my name being called from behind! A soldier leaps from a wagon and hurries after me. It is the driver of Lieutenant-Colonel von Csicserics! "Where is my master?" he asks. "In Gongzhuling, hopefully, just ahead of us here!" Thus, in an unexpected [[p353]] manner, I had found a conveyance, upon which, in good spirits, I now trotted towards future events.

On the way, the soldier told me how near Dawan they had all first been shelled with shrapnel by the Japanese, and later he claimed to have seen Japanese cavalry as well; since then he had been driving straight northwards, having had just enough money to support himself and the horse until now, but this morning his last kopeck had run out—and then, to his great delight, among the many wandering "homeward," he had spotted me. He thought that it must now lead straight to Russia, which surely could no longer be so very far!

At Gongzhuling station I was told that the military attachés' train had already departed for Kuanchengzi, which is 60 versts further north. I had already climbed into the dvukolka and was about to move on when, after all, I got down once more, went to the stationmaster, and asked him again most urgently whether the attachés were really in Kuanchengzi. "Please wait a moment," said the station commandant, a German, and after an hour he returned with the answer that the attachés' train was still here in Gongzhuling, on track eight down by the bridge. Now I drive over to track eight by the bridge, and on the way I happen to run into Major Macomb! "Where to?" "Looking for the attachés on track eight!" "They aren't here," said Macomb, "I've been looking for them myself for three days." Now we go to the station together, and there we were finally informed in the most definite terms (?) that the attachés' train was already in Harbin.

Macomb is staying at the staging point. A large, spacious dugout, stoutly and well built, with broad plank beds. About *two hundred* officers are lying here! What they are waiting for here and where they belong, they perhaps do not even know themselves. Here Macomb proves himself once again a practical American; in one corner of the dugout—on precisely as many square feet as fall to his share—he has pitched his tent, set up his table and camp bed, and sat here for three days without speaking to anyone. For me there was at last something to eat as well! For four days I had lived almost exclusively on chocolate and hardtack.

[[p354]]

Scarcely had I been there an hour when, one after another, the drivers of the other attachés presented themselves, brought together here by sheer chance. Only the baggage of the British and French generals and of the German Lieutenant-Colonel von Lauenstein had been lost; that of General Silvestre was found after some time in Harbin.

In the afternoon, Kuropatkin's train arrived in Gongzhuling. Behind the cordon formed by the guard, the crowd stood waiting for the general—no longer commander-in-chief—who stepped down from the carriage. No cheer rang out, everything remained silent—it was truly sorrowful.

I stood off to the side with two German-speaking field telegraphists, to whom I was recounting the story of the lost and recovered baggage of the attachés, for which I was now looking for an empty boxcar to convey it, as well as Major Macomb and myself, to Harbin.

A Cossack colonel, whom I saw alighting from Kuropatkin's carriage, had listened to our conversation and asked me—likewise in German—what had been lost. In reply to my request whether he might perhaps be able to help me secure an empty boxcar for our baggage—which, after all, simply had to be brought to Harbin eventually—he remarked: "I am afraid I cannot help you, but I will give you a good piece of advice: ask no one! For either people will lie to you, or they will tell you: 'Ya ne mogu znat'—'I cannot know'—for in this . . .," he added, "no human being can make head or tail of anything anymore!"
