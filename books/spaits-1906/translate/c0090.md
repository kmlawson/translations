### The Threat to the Russian Right Wing.

*25–28 February.*

Unlike the Russian recipe for attack, the Japanese did not seem to wait for the outcome of their eastern attacks first, but attacked vigorously along the entire front. At the same time that over in the mountains the Dalin Pass, as well as five redoubts south of the Gaotuling Pass and three south of Fanshen, fell into Japanese hands, fighting was raging in the centre around *Putilov Hill*, and reports of the advance of strong columns along the Liaohe had already reached headquarters on the 25th.

How thoroughly the army high command was dominated by the spectre of being cut off, how little confidence it had in its own power of resistance, and furthermore how lacking was the will to commit its entire forces to an energetic resistance, is shown by the fact that *as early as 25 February, work began on dismantling and sending back the 300 heavy siege guns installed in the centre*, [[p305]] which had been transported here and emplaced a few months earlier with so much trouble and expense.

To ascertain the Japanese advance from the south-west, the Dagestan Dragoon regiments were sent southwards along the Liaohe. The reports sent by this cavalry up to 28 February were bound to convince the army high command that it had acted *hastily* in deploying such significant forces against its own left wing!

On 28 February, strong Japanese turning columns were reported from the Liaohe!

To counter this movement threatening the Russian Second Army, recourse had to be taken to the *general army reserve*—the Sixteenth Army Corps at Baitapu—since the reserve of the Second Army, the First Siberian Corps, was indeed already at Shihoucheng.

Yet even of the Sixteenth Corps, only the 25th Division remained intact; the 2nd Brigade of the 41st Division had departed to Gongzhuling for railway protection (a successful Japanese raid on Fanjiatun).

That brigade had at the time been replaced by the Tsaritsyn Infantry Regiment No. 146, which on 25 February had likewise been sent to the Third Siberian Corps. Thus began the scattering and mixing together of units that was bound to end ultimately in chaos!

Now, on the 28th, from the Sixteenth Corps, which was stationed south of the Hunhe at Baitapu, the 25th Division under Lieutenant General Topornin was directed to the north-west, and the 1st Brigade under the commander of the 41st Division, Lieutenant General Birger, to the north on the Xinmintun road. The latter detachment was deemed necessary because our own cavalry had reported the approach from Xinmintun of four enemy columns—without giving any details as to their strength. In order to create a new general reserve, the army high command now resorted once again to its favourite expedient of cobbling together a composite division, one of the primary causes of the loosening of all organic formations.

In the centre stood, enumerated from the east, the Second [[p306]] and Fourth Siberian, First Army, Sixth Siberian, Seventeenth Army, Fifth Siberian, and Tenth Army Corps. Of these, the Fourth Siberian, First Army, and Sixth Siberian Corps held their positions on the Shahe, while the Seventeenth and Fifth Corps fell back under enemy pressure—*influenced, to be sure, by the events on the western wing.*

Meanwhile, the Japanese attacks from the west became increasingly energetic. The Rifle Corps and the detachment stationed at Sifangtai had been fiercely attacked on 28 February. The Rifle Corps had requested reinforcements, but these could by no means be given to it, for, as mentioned, the army high command had itself to think of *forming a new general reserve*.
