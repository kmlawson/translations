# Japan Prepares for Geneva.

**S. H.** People will have to get used to the new name "Manchukuo" for Manchuria. But one must not get used to the idea that this new name in any way guarantees or even legalizes this state's independence from China. Under international law, Manchuria is subject to the Nine-Power Treaty of 1922, in which all signatories pledged to uphold the territorial integrity of Manchuria and the Open Door policy. Japan was a co-signatory to this treaty. The situation under international law could at most be considered doubtful if Manchuria had broken away from China independently by virtue of a *separatist* movement. The League of Nations commission was tasked with examining once again whether such an independent breakaway could even be considered—a question that, to any impartial observer, has of course long been decided against Japan.

The reality is therefore that the mere fact of the Nine-Power Treaty already casts doubt on the sovereign rights of this new state. In addition, through the usurper's role that Japan has played in Manchuria, the League of Nations has been adversely affected. For under Article 10 of its Covenant, the members of the League are obliged to respect the territorial integrity of all other member states. Since Japan and China both belong to the League of Nations, the League Council cannot be absolved of the responsibility to have the final word in this dispute.

•

The next session of the League of Nations is expected to take place in mid-September. By then, the report of the League of Nations commission, the so-called *Lytton* Report, will also be available, which is said to take a stand *against* Japan on the Manchurian question.

It is evidently connected with this that the new Japanese Foreign Minister, *Uchida*, has taken a stance on Japan's recognition of Manchuria just at this moment. He declared in parliament that this recognition was *imminent.* His intention in doing so was undoubtedly to present America and the League of Nations with a fait accompli.

*Uchida* refused to grant China any further sovereign rights over Manchuria in any form whatsoever. To the old Japanese arguments that chaotic conditions had prevailed in Manchuria, *Uchida* surprisingly added the new argument that Japan's role in the Manchurian conflict had essentially been the exercise of a right of self-defense. This right, he claimed, was by no means limited solely to the defense of one's own territory—a not unskillful argument! Japan can and will appeal in Geneva, as it has always been accustomed to doing, to the fact that the reinterpretation of aggression as self-defense was demonstrated to it first by most imperialist states.

Regarding the employment of Japanese nationals in the new Manchurian republic, he stated that there were numerous precedents for this as well; a number of new states had requested the assistance of other countries for the development of their country, without those countries having been blamed under international law for providing the requested assistance. *Uchida* concluded by stating that the resolution of the Manchurian question could only be regarded as satisfactory by Japan if *the existing facts* were made the basis of the discussions.

•

With these arguments, Japan has done everything to place the League of Nations, whose power is weak enough as it is, in the most precarious position imaginable. This observation suffices to predict what feats of legal sophistry will once again be required in Geneva, especially since Britain and France throughout the entire conflict have never shown any intention of making an enemy of Japan for the sake of Manchuria. On the contrary: for the time being, both, and France in particular, have profited handsomely from the turmoil of war that Japan has ignited in Manchuria.

The only effective resistance mounted against Japan so far has come from Washington. Immediately after *Uchida's* speech became known, *Stimson* dispatched his ambassador in Tokyo to the Japanese government to learn more about the Japanese intentions. The ambassador is said to have left no doubt that the American government would officially object to Japan's formal recognition. In response, as we have already reported, Japan immediately dispatched a peace mission in the form of a Japanese general to America.

From Russia, it is reported that *Uchida's* speech is being viewed very calmly, and that recognition of the Manchurian government is not intended. This policy of caution is consistent with Russia's overall conduct in this turmoil, which poses a mortal danger to the Soviet Union.

•

Meanwhile, the policy of the Japanese in Manchuria continues to make progress. As is now confirmed, the Korean government is to transform the Manchurian port of Rashin (Rajin) into a large and modern port to compete with Vladivostok.

Rashin is to be connected with Kirin by railway. This railway will serve not only economic but also strategic purposes. A total duration of 15 years has been set for the execution of the reconstruction of the port and the railway line, from which the scale of the project may be inferred. The first section of the railway is to be opened in two years.

The complete organizational subjugation of Manchuria to Japan is further confirmed by the fact that Japan has dispatched a special ambassador to Manchuria in General *Mutō*, who is to take control over Japan's military and administrative organizations in Manchuria. During his transit through Korea, an assassination attempt against him was planned. In consequence, he has been assigned a bodyguard of Japanese Guards troops, under whose protection he will arrive next Thursday in Mukden, the seat of his new post.

•

In the September negotiations over the Sino-Japanese conflict, there will thus be a struggle between the Japanese, who rely on the positions of power they have meanwhile acquired and fortified in Manchuria, and the League of Nations, which can entrench itself behind nothing other than the legal dugouts that it will cobble together, after a fashion, from the articles of the League Covenant and the chapters of the *Lytton* Report. Should the attack from these dugouts even prove too troublesome to the Japanese, their power politics still has one recourse: to let Geneva be Geneva, withdraw from the League of Nations, and play the individual powers off against each other without the supervision of League of Nations delegates.
