As is well known, the sun moves from east to west, and the shadow of an upright body eludes it from west to east. Besides, every river has bends after all. Presently we are heading north-west; a bend will come in a moment, and then we shall head [[p236]] south-west for quite a while. So I place my chair right against the wall of the chart house that is currently facing the sun, and lie down! The bend arrives, the ship swings round, and I—am lying in the finest shade! Now the passengers from the other side arrive too, wanting to sit in the shade again. Indeed, there I sit! First you, now me! But I move over a little and make myself amiable. They thank me, arrange themselves picturesquely around me, and we become acquainted. So I have killed three birds with one

[Illustration: Golds with dog sled on the Amur (p. 217).]

stone: firstly, I am lying in the shade; secondly, I have achieved this without treading on my fellow passengers' toes—on the contrary, they are even grateful to me for the shade I leave them; and thirdly, I have become acquainted with them under such advantageous circumstances. For the coming days I shall carefully elaborate my system.

My travelling company consists exclusively of Russians. I am the only one on board who does not speak Russian. To make up for that, however, the others speak nothing other than Russian either. Only [[p237]] Nikolai Mikhailovich is an exception. He speaks German—not much, it is true, but a little nonetheless. The rest speak German or French no better than I speak Russian. They are going to give me Russian lessons. But Nikolai Mikhailovich speaks German. He will always help me out; I need only tell him if I want anything. Nikolai Mikhailovich, the elegant staff captain of the Guards from St. Petersburg belonging to the Semyonovsky Life Guards Regiment, had been attached by the Tsar to the staff of Vice Admiral Alexeyev in order to conduct studies during the Chinese campaign, and is now returning. We are already becoming friends. The remainder of the cabin passengers likewise consists largely of officers. An elderly colonel with his wife; a captain of the Caucasian Cossacks, Ivan Ivanovich, with his wife; Yelena Grigoryevna; a younger Cossack officer with his wife, who had been stationed in the vicinity of Tiflis and brought here a transport of Cossacks to be settled in the Amur region; a lieutenant colonel; a staff surgeon; and a Red Cross nurse. In addition, there is the priest lying below on my cushion, and a uniformed school inspector who vies with the priest in spitting under the table. Furthermore, there are several other characters who keep more in the background, however, since our company is evidently too refined for them. The deck passengers are a motley assortment. For the most part they are peasants, among them some wonderful physiognomies. Wild red or brown full beards, black hair cut under a flowerpot, and crafty little eyes. Then there are a number of gold prospectors, various petty traders, an itinerant pharmacist, a number of Cossacks from the stanitsas along the Amur, and finally also two deportees bound in chains, evidently hardened criminals. They have been requisitioned for road-building and will disembark at the very next landing stage together with their escort. There is quite a commotion down on the iron deck. Small groups have formed, eagerly discussing. Over there, a man, his wife, and three children sit together eating their supper. It consists of chai (tea), khleb (white bread), keta (dried salmon), of which each consumes about half a pound, sakhar (sugar), and vodka (spirits)—an inferior sort that is diluted with water, firstly to increase the quantity and secondly to make it burn less. That is the menu along and upon the Amur—morning, noon, and night. There is hardly any variety in it, at most game, but that must first be shot. Besides, there is no room on board for the people to boil or roast. We, too, are given fish to eat for dinner, in the form of sturgeon soup. With it comes an enormous piece of rye bread, and afterwards a glass of tea. The meal costs 60 kopecks. That is incredibly expensive! The cook either catches the sturgeon on set lines during the passage or buys the fish from the Cossacks for 15 to 30 kopecks for a sturgeon about a metre in length. A single such sturgeon can fill the bellies of twenty people! Catering on board certainly seems profitable! Apart from expensive tinned sardines, there is not even a decent zakuska. Ivan Ivanovich grumbles along on my behalf.

It is growing dark. The priest is apparently still asleep down on my mattress. I go into the cabin. A dreadful mugginess and a suffocating atmosphere rush to meet me. My mind is made up: I shall sleep on deck under the open sky. I fetch a ministering spirit and have my mattress, in the boldest sense of the word, [[p238]] pulled out from under the priest. The ministering spirit accomplishes this by first alerting the priest with a gentle tap on his thighs. Thereupon the priest turns towards the wall, the servant pulls, the priest rolls further round, the servant pulls the mattress entirely out from under him, and the priest sleeps on upon the wooden bench. Then I have it laid with the inlaid side facing up onto my long cane chair, and my bed is ready. As it threatens to turn cool at night, I pull a second suit of flannel pyjamas over my first, over that a Japanese kimono, then wrap myself in a flannel blanket and keep my plaid ready to cover myself. Finally, in order to be secured against any incivilities from Jupiter Pluvius, I put on my wide waterproof rubber coat and pull the hood over my head. Now let come what may—I shall sleep.
