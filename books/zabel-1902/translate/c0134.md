Pokrovskaya is the last station on the Amur. Four versts upstream, the name “Amur” comes to an end. There, the Argun and the Shilka flow together and form the Amur. The Shilka is the headstream richer in water, which also determines the direction of the Amur in the confluence area, although the course of the Argun is significantly longer. That the Shilka is also regarded by the indigenous

### 5. The Amur Region.

As has already been mentioned in passing earlier, one must regard the Amur basin as Russia’s true colonial territory. Even though those parts of Transbaikalia lying east of the Yablonovy Mountains belong geographically and climatologically to the Amur lands, economically they nevertheless occupy an exceptional position in that they have already been in Russian possession for several hundred years and have developed under the same phenomena and conditions as the parts west of the mountains. The Amur region, by contrast, looks back upon a relatively short period of settlement. But if one wishes to assess the economic value of a country, what matters is not so much the extent of the territories under consideration as how many people live in these areas, and what kind of people they are. A country’s mineral resources are dead assets until human hands unlock them.

Russia is first and foremost an agricultural country. This circumstance also stamps its imprint upon the manner in which Russia populates its colonial territories. The settlement of Siberia, and above all Western Siberia, unmistakably bears this hallmark. The opening of industrial enterprises has lagged disproportionately behind agricultural activity, and has mostly occurred only when the exploitation of mineral treasures was so obvious and appeared so tempting that substantial profits were readily to be expected. Despite the diverse mineral wealth of Siberia, really only the exploitation of the goldfields has so far been taken in hand. In support of the Russian’s aversion to industrial activity is also the fact that the relatively very few factories that produce the necessary working materials and tools for this flourishing industry arose only very late on the spot, [[p289]] that is, in the industrial centres. The greater part of the needs of the Siberian mining industry is still supplied today from European Russia, and even more so from abroad.

In the manner of the settlement of Siberia, therefore, various differences come to light that assign a special status to the Amur region. One can distinguish here three different types of procedure. When, after the abolition of serfdom in Russia, the Russian government proceeded to take the settlement of Siberia into its own hands, having until then confined itself to restraining the unauthorized flow of immigration

[Illustration: Cathedral Square in Irkutsk (p. 295).]

or at least directing it into certain channels, the same method was applied that had also become law in European Russia. The emigrants were settled in communes or had to join already existing communes. The latter were jointly and severally liable for raising the poll tax of their community members. The commune as such was assigned a tract of land fit for settlement, with 15 desyatinas reckoned for each member of the commune. The commune then distributed the land among its members; it remained communal land, and in the event of an increase in numbers within the commune, the respective holders had to submit to consolidations and redistributions of the communal property carried out periodically from time to time. It is obvious that this type of settlement entails great disadvantages, if only because improvements which the respective holder might have wished to make to his [[p290]] land were bound to appear disadvantageous, since the holder in question could never know whether his land would be awarded to him again in a repartition. The same system of agrarian communism which had already made a rational development of agriculture impossible in European Russia manifested itself in the same way in Siberia as well. Here it encountered often stubborn resistance on the part of the long-established peasant population, who had wished to flee from precisely this system in European Russia.
