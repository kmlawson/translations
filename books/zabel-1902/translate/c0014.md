### 3. The Gates Are Closed.

Another three-quarters of an hour’s train ride, and we have arrived in Shanhaiguan. I take leave of my traveling companions, of whom the German officers head for the German staging base, while the Russian hurries to his regiment, which is quartered near the station. As I have done so often, I would ask for the hospitality of the German staging base, but it lies nearly an hour away from the station, and since I am unfamiliar with local conditions and would face too many difficulties with my luggage, especially in the dark, I prefer to spend the night at the station. It has turned bitterly cold. I find it to be 19 degrees Réaumur below zero. In addition, a biting wind is blowing. In the station building, they are already prepared for guests; for lodging purposes, they have [[p34]] an old, fairly broad sofa, which is put at my disposal, and so I camp out in the office of Shanhaiguan station, where Herr Ries from Hamburg, by trade a sergeant in the 2nd Railway Company, discharges his duties as stationmaster.

To refresh myself a little after the long train journey, I seek out a restaurant near the station kept by a Japanese. It is furnished about as comfortably as a beer tent on a fairground back home; one sits on crude wooden benches and eats from wooden tables whose legs are simply driven into the ground. In the middle of the house, which is fitted with paper walls, stands a small iron stove doing its level best to make our stay somewhat more tolerable. At a neighboring table Russian soldiers are drinking vodka, and at another Japanese coolies are talking in shrill voices. Apart from these accompaniments, the “dinner” is quite good. Once I have this main attraction of the said establishment behind me, I turn up the collar of my Siberian fur coat, ordered from Vladivostok, pull my otter-fur cap down over my ears, and—move on to another drinking establishment. This one is somewhat more inviting. It is kept by two Frenchwomen, one of whom must once have sung beautifully, while the other, with a little imagination, might still pass today—by Shanhaiguan standards—as passable. Many cavalrymen are sitting with these hostesses. They are Russian officers enjoying themselves splendidly over champagne, vodka, and, last but not least, a great deal of confectionery. In this lively company I am evidently no unwelcome guest. I am immediately recognized as a German and celebrated as such. The Russian loves to fraternize with Germans. In his tipsy good nature he is simply priceless. The amiability and the pressing of drinks, the toasts and handshakes, the kissing among one another—all of this makes for a truly delightful spectacle for the sober onlooker. And so for an hour I feel quite comfortable in this merry circle of drinkers; there is singing, the older Frenchwoman with the voice that must once have been beautiful sings too, we all sing, and could well have sung until early morning had I not wanted to ride out to the roadstead at the crack of dawn the next day to look for a steamer to take me to Port Arthur. I intend to make this place the starting point of my Manchurian journey. I therefore took my leave early and stretched out on the broad chaise longue in the office of Shanhaiguan station.

January 31.
The next morning it is still just as bitterly cold as the evening before. Nevertheless, I pack my things onto two Chinese carts and ride to the roadstead, which lies a good hour away from the station. The route leads along a heavily traveled road through the Shanhaiguan forts, which, seventeen in number, surround the town and at the time fell into the hands of the Allies without a blow being struck, just like the Lutai forts. Running alongside the road is a small narrow-gauge field railway on which goods landed at Shanhaiguan are transported to the station. The flags of the Powers fly over the forts.

[[p35]]

After an hour’s ride on the jolting cart, I finally reach the sea. With the mountains behind me, the view opens before me onto the wide expanse of the sea, the Gulf of Liaodong. “Shanhaiguan,” translated into German, means: “Mountains by the Great Sea.” Here is the spot where the Great Wall of China meets the Pacific Ocean.

We are now only a few hundred meters from the “water’s edge”; to the right of the road stand two large temples, with a German sentry in front. That is the German staging post. Already one can see the steamers lying far out in the roadstead. Now we have arrived at the coastal dune as well—but alas, the harbor is completely frozen over. Any communication with the ships outside is impossible. From an English shack-keeper who ekes out a miserable existence in a wretched mud hut on the beach, I learn that all the passenger ships left the harbor this morning on account of the ice and steamed further out to sea. That the roadstead of Qinhuangdao is frozen over I already know, otherwise I would have disembarked there. So there is nothing left for me to do but have my carts turned around and return to Shanhaiguan.
