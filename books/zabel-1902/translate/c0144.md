### 2. Through Primeval Forest and Steppe.

One of our greatest worries is whether we can still secure a ticket for the luxury train that leaves Irkutsk tomorrow evening. Nikolai Mikhailovich, who undertook to arrange this, has already been on his way since nine o'clock and brings word that good seats in first class are no longer available. We therefore decide to travel second class, the appointments of which differ in no respect from first class, except that in first class the cushions are upholstered in red plush, while in second class they are in a fashionable shade. We immediately buy tickets through to St. Petersburg; since the railway has a zone tariff, we save over ten rubles by doing so, compared to if we had taken our tickets only as far as Moscow and tried to purchase a new ticket from there to St. Petersburg. The ticket costs 60 rubles: 42 rubles for the fare, 15 rubles for the luxury-train surcharge, and three rubles for the bed-linen fee; the linen is supposed to be changed every three days.

We feel as if we were already as good as home in Irkutsk. Our luxury train leaves Irkutsk at midnight; every single seat is taken. Several different companies provide the luxury trains, among them the International Sleeping Car Company. Our train is a Russian one, and the attendants speak only Russian. The carriages are a good deal wider than our corridor express cars, but otherwise fitted out in exactly the same way. The interior of the compartments is designed specifically for long journeys; the cushions are long and broad. Electric light, generated on the train itself, shines down from the ceiling, and the white linen covers—the attendant having already made up the berths—give us a cozy, welcoming feeling. The train departs on time, and we are just stretching out on our snowy cushions when suddenly there is a jolt that practically flings us against the wall! The Carpenter brake has gripped, and the train comes to a dead stop. An intermezzo already, right at the start of our journey! A few meters ahead of the locomotive, a huge pile of railway ties lies across the embankment. The clear intention was nothing other than to derail the train. I am reminded of the sensational novels depicting hold-ups on the Pacific Railroad. But the Siberian highwaymen—whose bands consist largely of escaped convicts, frequently joined by local peasants—are somewhat less ferocious than the Indians; their sights are set only on the baggage car. They wait with their carts in the adjacent forest, and amid the general confusion caused by a derailment, they plunder the travelers' luggage. In our case, thanks to the vigilance of the train conductor, the matter ends harmlessly enough; the passengers band together to telegraph the Minister of [[p314]] Railways so that the engine driver may receive official recognition. The next day, we hear that a similar attack was made on the postal train approaching from Moscow, unfortunately with greater success. The locomotive and two other cars were smashed and thrown down the embankment, and most of the passengers' luggage was plundered. There are thieves on our train as well. A Red Cross sister has had a trunk stolen from the train. Despite all inquiries, it remains missing. Naturally, the Russian press keeps silent about such incidents, as the censorship does not allow them through, but they are simply part of the reality of the Trans-Siberian Railway. One must not allow oneself to be too swayed by the skillful publicity the Russian Ministry of Transport puts out abroad for the Trans-Siberian Railway. On this railway, things are far from being as they are reported and as they should be, even if one sets one's expectations quite low. During the first few days, we still travel through well-cultivated regions. Zima, Tulun, and Nizhneudinsk pass by us. To afford us the opportunity for close observations of the land and its people, the train naturally travels too fast, although our luxury train is by no means fast in itself. It, too, averages only twenty-five kilometers an hour. The stops at the stations are quite long; once we waited up to three hours, albeit to let the postal train, which had been attacked en route, pass by at a siding.

On August 25, we reach Kansk. We are now already in Yeniseysk Governorate and travel almost continuously through the primeval forest, the "taiga." This does not, at least along the railway line, consist of great, mighty trees; most trunks reach only the thickness of telegraph poles. The forest is heavily thinned; in many places we see stretches of meadow on which herds of cattle, sheep, and horses graze. Farther away from the tracks, however, the forest is said to be denser still and to boast large, mighty trunks. On the train I find a rather different company of travelers than [[p315]] before: a Belgian scholar on a study tour, traveling in the company of a compatriot, a mining engineer who has set up a gold mine in Mongolia for a Belgian syndicate; further, a Russian naval officer who took part in the siege of Peking; an old English colonel who formerly led campaigns in northern India, accompanied by his wife; and a Czech professor from Prague, a thoroughly delightful traveling companion who is up for any mischief to which we spur him on in high spirits. In addition, there are quite a number of passengers who get on and off at intermediate stations. Soon we are a large company in which everyone knows everyone else without having been formally introduced, each helping the other to kill time in a respectable fashion. The gathering place is the elegantly appointed parlor and dining car, which also contains a library, though it holds only Russian works. Incidentally, I must not forget an observation characteristic of us Germans. There happens to be a German officer traveling on the train.
