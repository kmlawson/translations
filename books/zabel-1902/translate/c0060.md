The weather today is dreadful once again. Thank goodness I am the fortunate owner of a long cane chair in which one can stretch out quite comfortably—a blessing on the high seas! Lying flat, as everyone knows, is the best remedy for seasickness. The fresh air on deck blows around my nose, another small aid against seasickness, and finally, a fairly heavy canvas has been stretched over the cargo hatches today. So, I shall lie down in my "longchair"! What do I see—it is occupied, a lady is lying full length in it!

[[p128]]

A lady . . hm . . one must be polite. So I leave her lying there. Lo and behold, she gets up. I immediately dart for my chair and place my coat upon it. Then I hurry to my cabin and fetch a book. I return—someone is lying in my chair again, my coat beside it! This time it is a man; I do believe it is the globetrotter himself. No room for consideration here. Out! . . . the chair is mine, and to me it belongs! After a short while the lady returns; she sees the chair occupied and me in it. She casts languishing looks at me. Terribly sorry, in this sea state I am entirely impervious! Since that hour, I mercilessly throw anyone out of my chair who has made themselves comfortable in it during my absence, whether man or woman. This is by no means rudeness, but simply self-defense.

4 July.

Is the weather any better today? I think not. But perhaps one gets used to it. Possible! At any rate, I have the feeling today that when the steamer rises, I already make myself light, and when it plunges, heavy. Thus I imagine that I am the one producing the rocking motion—mere fancy, to be sure, but it helps. Not thinking about it helps too. Distraction, then! Good, I shall work, and in the breaks reflect on how best to annoy my fellow passengers. I think that works best if, while working, I deprive them of any hope of sitting in my chair. Since I type on a typewriter, it would be far more convenient for me to take a camp stool and sit on that. But I wouldn't dream of it! I pull my long chair up before a large crate on which I place my typewriter, and seat myself in my chair. Thus I let my fellow passengers stroll around me with envious glances, which amuses me so much that I have now been working the whole day through.

5 July.

What is this? Today there is no rocking at all! The sea is as smooth as glass, reflecting every little cloud in the sky almost without distortion. Eight hours left to Vladivostok! So I suppose I must deduct 21.6 kilometers of rocking motion from the 202.5 kilometers. The rarest people, who had otherwise crawled away into their cabins, can be seen on deck today. The awning is once again stretched over the quarterdeck, and on the left hand, Manchurian-Siberian hills can already be seen beckoning. The captain thinks it will be very hot ashore today. Siberia and hot—for the educated Central European, two utterly irreconcilable concepts!

The eight hours pass more quickly than expected. At 3 o'clock sharp in the afternoon, we drop anchor in the harbor of Vladivostok. The police boat appears. "Passports!" . . . echoes into the cabin. The passports are simply handed over; the captain stacks them in a pile, folded just as they are. "How many passengers?" asks the police chief. "So and so many," answered the captain. The police chief counts the passports, the number tallies, then [[p129]] with a forceful blow he strikes his stamp onto the back of the passports without examining them any further. From the elegance with which he performs this stamping operation, one may allow oneself to infer the extraordinarily great practice he must have in this activity. Uninspected, our passports are returned to us.

The "Daphne" comes from Hong Kong. The plague is there. The steamer must therefore go into quarantine if it has not been at least ten days underway from Hong Kong. Fortunately, this is the tenth day, and it expires at 1 o'clock in the afternoon. Strictly speaking, we should have to remain in quarantine for another hour. But the captain negotiates the hour away with the good-natured port officer. After all, it is the good old "Daphne" of Kunst & Albers, well known in Vladivostok, so that works out fine!
