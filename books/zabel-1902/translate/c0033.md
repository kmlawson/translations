Above all, however, it is ill-advised to entrust our consular representation to an English merchant who also administers three other consulates on the side. Today more than ever, Newchwang needs an able German consul who has a firm hand and knows how to assert himself. Once we have achieved this, we will hopefully also go a step further and finally reconcile ourselves to the idea of posting to China only consuls who have specialised in the country’s conditions. Our Chinese-trained interpreters are usually far better acquainted with conditions than the frequently transferred consuls, and these latter are often enough forced to be advised by their interpreters down to the smallest detail. Why have jurists who came from who-knows-where and will go who-knows-where as soon as they have served their time in China—which they themselves, unfortunately, often regard merely as a transitional period—when the interpreters can do their work better? Let the consular career in China be opened up to interpreters to the widest possible extent, and we will fare well by it. The same applies to the legation post in Peking. Sooner or later, we will have to come around to viewing the great Chinese Empire as a world unto itself, one that demands to be specially studied and treated as such. And then, I fear, events in the near future will follow in such rapid succession that we must take good care to train a solid staff of younger consular candidates specifically schooled in East Asian affairs—men who are not merely able to affix a few stamps, collect a few dollars for clearing a German steamer inward and outward, or receive higher- or lower-ranking travellers passing through, but who also learn the language of the country, do not change their posts too frequently, and understand how to study the country’s trade and provide pointers on where and how the German merchant is able to do business in China. At least, that is how the Japanese consuls do it. And in doing so, Japan is surpassing its masters.

### 3. In Audienz beim russischen Höchstkommandierenden.

21 February.

This morning, the Commander-in-Chief of the Russian army and navy in the Far East, Vice Admiral Alekseyev, arrived in Newchwang. He had announced an inspection tour to Newchwang and Mukden some time ago, and I had extended my stay in Newchwang beyond what was originally planned partly because I wanted to await his arrival. I was also granted the privilege today of being received in audience by His

[Illustration: The Chinese government yamen in Newchwang (p. 10).]

Excellency. For eastern Russian Asia, Alekseyev is almost what the Tsar is for the western Russian Empire. He commands independently not only Manchuria, the Russian expeditionary forces, and the Russian navy stationed in the Pacific, but his voice is virtually an order for the whole of eastern Siberia as well. Among the Russians, the navy indeed holds a higher rank than the land army; the naval officers are also, in general, far better educated than those of the army, and Alekseyev in particular is considered one of the most capable senior Russian officers. In any case, he is more master over his subordinates than any other foreign general, thanks to the despotic form of government of the Tsarist empire.

[[p74]]

When one seeks an audience with such a potentate, one must also have something to say. And since I make it a principle to avoid interviewing any influential personality merely to satisfy curiosity, given that the gentleman in question very quickly sees through the intention and one may then easily end up more foolish after the interview than before, I sought out a matter of concern and was also fortunate enough to be able to present a request whose granting or refusal was bound to be more instructive for me than any harmless answers to tricky questions.

The audience takes place in the Russian consulate, although the admiral, owing to the very mediocre housing conditions in Newchwang, has preferred to live in his saloon carriage at the Russian railway station. Shortly after three o'clock, his standard is hoisted on the flagpole of the Russian consulate—the signal to those gathered in the waiting room that he has just driven up. In the waiting room, just about everyone of any standing or name in Newchwang has assembled. First of all, the only two career consuls of foreign powers, the British and the Japanese, in full dress. Then a number of merchants who have this or that to present, the directors of the Russo-Chinese Bank, and so on. The honours are done by the Russian consul and the civil administrator Ostroverkhov, while the vice admiral’s political and diplomatic adviser, Mr Korostovets, assists him during the individual receptions.
