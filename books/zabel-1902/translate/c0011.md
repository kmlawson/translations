### 2. A Much-Disputed Railway Line.

29 January.

After all the hardships that expedition life entailed, traveling by train seems like pure pleasure to me. In general, one only learns to appreciate the achievements of our European civilization once one has had to do without them. Back home, it is easy for us to complain about the railway, the post, and the telegraph whenever they do not function quite as we would like. Learning to appreciate these institutions properly is something one only does abroad.

To be sure, even in China there is no unclouded joy, as I am soon to discover once again. Having looked forward to continuing my journey in a nice, heated carriage, I am sorely disappointed when, at Tanggu, I board a “first”-class compartment whose windows are partly smashed and whose walls still show the holes torn by enemy bullets. The wooden blinds have been pulled up after a fashion, but that does little good against the icy air that now whistles through the bullet holes and other leaks in the carriage. There is, to be sure, a little stove in this “saloon carriage,” but it has too much draft, so that the railway soldier—who, on account of the stove refusing to burn, gets treated every five minutes by the non-commissioned officer in charge of the train to the choicest flowers of barrack-square invective—uses half [[p29]] a can of kerosene to get the fire going. Even so, it refuses to burn. At last, the united efforts of the soldier, the non-commissioned officer, a fellow-traveling Cossack staff surgeon, and yours truly succeed in coaxing the fire to life. The two of us last-mentioned then volunteer to tend the stove—not, of course, out of charity, but from sheer self-interest. Yet our iron comrade is of little help. The pipe serving as a chimney passes out into the open through a large square hole in the carriage roof, and all the heat takes the very same route.

[Illustration: Emergency platform at Hangtou (p. 12).]

It was in this condition that the Germans only recently took over railway operations from the Russians. The stove is already a new acquisition. Before handing the line over to the Germans, the Russians removed all the heaters. Yet not only did they remove these, leave the carriages unrepaired, and allow the locomotives to get filthy, but they also transferred by far the greater part of the rolling stock, especially engines, to the section beyond Shanhaiguan, which they intend to keep permanently.

[[p30]]

Around 10 o’clock in the morning, the train leaves Tanggu station. We pass through the terrain on which the Beitang forts lie. They are occupied by small guard detachments. Above them fly the German and Russian flags. — Beitang Station! The train stops; a small detachment of German artillerymen from the heavy howitzer battery stationed in Tanggu gets out and marches over toward the fort to supply their comrades stationed there with fresh provisions, above all with water. The region really deserves to be passed over in silence. It is desolate. Between the causeways that connect the forts with one another—where last autumn, when we took the works at a run, everything was still full of water—all is now dry, frozen, and the uneven ground is covered with a white layer that is neither ice nor snow, but salt deposited from the brackish, evaporated water. The train now crosses the small river separating Forts I and II from the others. Counting the large rampart-enclosed military camps, I make out seven fortifications in all. Beside the railway embankment lies a derailed locomotive.

After about an hour and a half’s ride, the call comes: “All out!” We have arrived at the Hang He, specifically at Hangkou Station. The bridge over the river has been destroyed. One must transfer to another train waiting on the opposite bank of the river. Scarcely has our train halted when a hundred coolie arms stretch out toward our luggage; likewise, all the goods destined for onward transit, mailbags, etc., are unloaded and carried by the coolies across a fairly wide and solid temporary wooden bridge. The Russians, who had repaired the railway line as far as Shanhaiguan, chose not to subject themselves to the arduous labor of building this bridge, and I believe the Germans too will leave its reconstruction to the owners of the railway, particularly since the repairs would take so much time that in the meantime the roadstead of Taku will be open again, thereby substantially diminishing the importance of the railway for military purposes.

In Hangkou, a shortage of passenger carriages is already making itself felt. We therefore board a “second”-class carriage, which differs from the “first”-class saloon carriage in that the wooden benches have straight rather than curved backs and seats, much like the better fourth-class carriages back home. In return, however, this carriage possesses the great advantage of being excellently heated, and this circumstance more than makes up for all its shortcomings.
