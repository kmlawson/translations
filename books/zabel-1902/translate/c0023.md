### 4. By Chinese Cart to Yingkou (Niuzhuang).

After a half-hour rest over tea and black bread, I continue my journey. Since I always keep the cart at a trot and the roads are for the most part remarkably good, I cover a fairly considerable distance. I pass through an area that seems somewhat more welcoming; the expanse of cultivated land increases, and mostly bean stubble is to be seen. Since no detailed map of this region exists, I record as precise an itinerary as possible, in the course of which it strikes me that the villages across an entire large district bear one and the same name: they are all, without exception, called Taipingzhuang. I travel for nearly an hour and a half, and whenever I ask the name of a village, I hear this same single name time and again. Incidentally, similar things happened to me several times during the remainder of my journey. I have no explanation for it, but wish at least to record the fact.**)

Near the village of Chonenpu we cross the coastal river Liuli He, and then turn to the southeast, a direction we maintain for some time from then on. Near the village of Gaojiatai, around three o'clock in the afternoon, I reach a fairly wide trade road that leads north from Yingkou. Here, for the first time, I encounter long wagon trains loaded with kaoliang and beans, as well as bean cake. In winter, an extraordinarily brisk cart traffic prevails in Manchuria, the scale of which one [[p53]] can hardly imagine. The carts are heavily laden and usually drawn by five to seven animals.

In the late afternoon, after a journey of about 80 li,* I reach Shuangtaizi, on the 300-metre-wide Shuangtaizi River, across which runs a railway bridge that lies, however, completely in ruins. In the town there is a large Daoist monastery in which the Russian staging post has quartered itself. Here I shall spend the night.

[Illustration: My Cossacks (p. 32).]

To my surprise, at this remote staging post I also find a lady. It is the wife of the staging post commander, Staff Captain Grodinsky. When, as a newlywed, he had to depart from his garrison in Nikolsk to march to Manchuria and Beijing, his young wife followed him and has now sought to make herself at home with him at this remote station. In general, Russian officers' wives are very courageous; it is by no means rare for them to follow their husbands and take the field with them. I made the acquaintance of one lady who took part in the entire campaign by her husband's side, and who had been to Beijing and Shanhaiguan, and later to Mukden and elsewhere. Most of the officers' wives, to be sure, live in Port Arthur and see their [[p54]] husbands only occasionally. In any case, the presence of a pleasant young lady brings a welcome change to my nomadic life, and although we can converse only by means of an interpreter, the time passes as swift as an arrow. I am received with all the hospitality of Siberia, and the woman's managing hand ensures that I am served a dinner such as I could not wish better—and that in the interior of Manchuria on a day when I have been fired upon by *honghuzi* or regulars. For once, the interpreter this time is the son of honest German parents who had emigrated to Łódź. He bore the name of Böttcher and is almost childishly delighted to be able to speak German once again.

5 February.

My escort has been reinforced by three riflemen, who are to do some shopping in Yingkou for the captain and his wife respectively. First I must cross the Shuangtaizi He, and it takes some effort to get the animals across the ice. The actual settlement itself lies only on the right bank of the river, opposite the Daoist monastery where I had been quartered, and in its streets a fairly brisk grain market is underway. Then we continue southwards, and on this day I cover more than 100 li. The road ran between the railway line to the left and the Imperial Chinese Telegraph to the right; we pass alternately through cultivated land and wasteland, but encounter fewer villages than yesterday. In places the countryside becomes quite desolate, and one is delighted when, after hours of travel, one once again sees the railway-carriage-like houses of the Manchus silhouetted against the horizon in the distance. The flat landscape, which offers few resting points for the eye, often tempts one into the most fantastic mirages. One takes a bean stalk standing close by for an ox, and villages often appear at least ten kilometres away when one is already within about a kilometre of them.
