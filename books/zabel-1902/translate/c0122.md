26 July.
We are on the move again—only five kilometers, to be sure, but at least we are moving. The agent has replied from Blagoveshchensk that the requested steamer has departed. The mood on board is therefore considerably brighter. With the glimmer of hope in our hearts that the ordeal can now last at most three more days, we now view the whole affair more as a country outing. And indeed, after traveling five kilometers, we drop anchor again at the mouth of a small mountain brook that has clear, cool water. We make our way into the neighboring bushes and cut a large quantity of foliage, with which we build ourselves shady little spots on deck. Mitya the man builds Mitya the bear a veritable hut of leaves. We eat—nothing but fish, drink—nothing but tea, take walks, take river, shade, and sun baths—the gentlemen 100 meters downriver, the ladies 100 meters upriver. Then, resigned to our fate, we lie down to sleep.

27 July.
The program calls for a repetition of yesterday’s, once again with the very same fish soup for lunch! Yesterday morning the captain crossed the fateful bar in a boat with a few sailors and determined that the water depth exceeds five feet, so that he could easily pass right over it with his steamer. He returns enthusiastic about this discovery and declares himself able to reach Blagoveshchensk with his steamer. But the agent in Yekaterino-Nikolsk forbids him to do so, because this would be such an unheard-of novelty that, in his view, it is beyond doubt that the captain is mistaken. If this is not bureaucratic rigidity, what is?

This morning a second passenger steamer arrived, carrying mainly workers and peasants. It, too, is not permitted to cross the bar and ties up in our immediate neighborhood. Now real life begins on the bank. Hearths are built from large stones lying about, and soon something is simmering in the potato pot. Otherwise, over there they also indulge in all the amusements we can afford ourselves. But while on our bathing beach men and women bathe separately, the people over there are less inhibited and establish a family bath.

We are just taking a stroll ashore when we are summoned to our ship by the steam whistle. Naturally our eyes turn upstream. We see a small cloud of smoke and soon clearly make out the outline of a steamer. Deliverance is approaching, but slowly. The steamer halts on the far side of the bar, about nine kilometers away from us.

[[p246]]

Our captain has already gone over with a number of sailors. The newly arrived steamer has a barge in tow. The passengers and cargo are loaded onto this, and then the barge must drift across the shoal with the flowing water. After a few hours, through the binoculars that hardly leave our eyes, we indeed see the barge slowly detach itself from the large ship's hull. It takes a full three hours to reach us. That would correspond to a water speed of three kilometers per hour. The barge comes alongside the steamer, and now begins the exchange of passengers and cargo, which proceeds without any system amid shoving and jostling, leaving behind many a bruise. Among the passengers of the *Sibir*, which is now to take us aboard, are four Americans, [[p247]] who are making a journey around the world, photographing everything that crosses their path, and still have quite considerable supplies of all kinds of good canned goods, which they no longer need so badly, as they are at the end of their nightmare journey. For money and kind words they part with some of it to me, and I now have sufficient *zakuska* for myself and Nikolai Mikhailovich until Blagoveshchensk. By 9 o'clock in the evening, all the passengers of the *Sergei Dukhovskoi* are on board the barge. We set off. But that has its difficulties. For our sake, after all, the Amur does not suddenly run upstream for three hours so that we could just drift along. Consequently, the barge must be towed from the bank. Just as Yermak once did in his youth along the banks of the Kama, the sailors and the male deck passengers now tow our heavy barge from the shore. Progress is very slow; reportedly we will not reach the *Sibir* before daybreak. On the deck of the barge, a colorful throng and confusion still prevail. Cabin passengers and deck passengers are now equal, just as in church. Everyone tries to find a spot as best they can. The iron deck is covered several inches deep in sand. This forms the bed for most people through the night. One wraps oneself in blankets and places some bundle or other under one's head. Thus they sleep: master and servant, lady and peasant woman. Only the Red Cross sister is an exception. Shortly before we crossed over to the barge, she did not feel well. I therefore yield my chair to her for the night. Once she is lying on it, she immediately feels better.
