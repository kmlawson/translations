The following little story is characteristic of Russian bureaucratic formalism. Opposite the Pacific Hotel there is a garden-like plot of land enclosed by a wooden palisade. It lies vacant. There was an intention to turn it into a concert garden with a music pavilion, a kind of town park, which would be very fitting [[p138]] in this treeless place. The municipal administration granted permission to the entrepreneur, but they had not first consulted the government engineer who oversees construction matters in Vladivostok and its environs. When the matter came before him, he refused permission because no wooden buildings were allowed to stand in the quarter designated for stone houses. Poor music pavilion!

8 July.

It is getting boring in Vladivostok. I have now strolled or driven up and down the main street about ten times, have been to the theatre twice, once afterwards to a music hall, and one has absolutely no idea how to pass the time. Nothing is more terrible than having to wait in uncertainty. Neither does the Shilka rise, nor do Count Keyserling's ships arrive. I spend most of my time finishing up my correspondence with my East Asian friends.

Much to my regret, my Vladivostok acquaintance had the misfortune of being kicked and severely injured on a riding excursion by a horse galloping beside him. I therefore visit him frequently to help pass the time for both him and myself, and he has the forbearance, despite his pain, to provide me with valuable information about my upcoming journey. Thus time passes, and for me these hours of conversation are among the pleasantest parts of my stay in Vladivostok.

There is also an ethnographic museum here, small, but its contents are interesting and valuable, if only because it contains a large number of original artifacts originating from the indigenous Siberian peoples who are retreating ever further into the interior and are doomed to extinction. Material is available in abundance. But it seems to me that it does not receive proper care. Inside, the museum looks like a disorganised and dusty glass case. The only objects I found not covered with dust [[p139]] were a wheelbarrow and a spade, which are evidently accorded great reverence; for this is the spade with which, ten years ago now, the Tsesarevich turned the first sod for the Siberian railway, and the wheelbarrow with which he carted away the first earth.

It is not my intention here to give a description of this museum, which would only interest a specialist. In Khabarovsk there is said to be an even larger museum. Should anyone intend to devote themselves to a specialized study of the Siberian tribes, they must not fail to draw upon the rich material amassed here.

The museum attendant takes no tips, an exception in Russia well worth recording. Otherwise, they almost all take them, whoever they may be, if one does not go about it too clumsily—often right up to the highest officials.

9 July.

Any German coming to Vladivostok cannot possibly bypass the great trading house of Kunst & Albers. It is an establishment whose importance extends far beyond even that of the largest Chinese import house. The firm was founded shortly after the emergence of Vladivostok by two Hamburg merchants whose names it bears, and today it is a world-class enterprise of the first rank, with branches to be found in almost every major town in Siberia. Its headquarters are in Vladivostok, where the firm possesses a number of splendid stone buildings and large warehouses. It unites virtually every branch of business within itself. The export trade is, of course, of the least importance, for there is next to nothing to export from Vladivostok. But shipping, import on the grandest scale, wholesale and retail trade in palatial department stores conducted by a host of German and Russian employees, all of whom visibly share the pride of belonging to a great and distinguished commercial house—one finds all of this; in short, one gets the impression that one might receive when reading Freytag's *Soll und Haben* [*Debit and Credit*] of the great and distinguished trading house described therein. In Vladivostok alone, the firm has over 150 employees—that is to say, Europeans—all of whom also receive housing, board, and lighting from the firm, in addition to a salary that is quite high by East Asian standards. The best sign of the spirit prevailing within the firm, however, was to be found in the fact that I never heard a single derogatory remark from any of the employees about their treatment in the firm or about its management, something that is otherwise so easily the case. The firm's current senior chief, Mr. Dattan, enjoys equal esteem among Europeans and Russians alike. He is, incidentally, a Russian Commercial Councillor.
