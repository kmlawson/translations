In the circumstances described lie the causes of Russia’s so successful expansion in Asia. Over the course of centuries, its success was thus not due to systematic government policy, which set in only much later, but to the greed of Cossacks perpetually on the warpath, who found their chief occupation in the exploitation of native peoples; to the advance of the trapper, the prospector, and the merchant; and to the longing for freedom of the peasant who had escaped slavery at home. Parallels with the conquest of North America readily suggest themselves. Here as there, the continuous low-intensity warfare with the native, who is pushed ever deeper into the forests, accompanied by the no less fierce competitive struggle of all against all. It often happened that Cossacks collecting the fur tribute, the “yasak,” from the natives from different ostrogs came into conflict with one another. Frequently enough, this led to murder and manslaughter. Insofar, however, as government policy comes into consideration, intervening case by case on behalf of the Cossacks’ acquisitions, [[p186]] the dictum of the geographer Fr. von Richthofen holds true: “It is Russia’s destiny in Asia that it must everywhere and constantly seek to expand its borders in order to enhance the exploitation and security of its possessions existing at any given time.” Nomadic peoples are indeed an insecure frontier. Count York describes Russia’s restless advance in Central Asia as “the hunt for a frontier.” The image is well chosen and applicable

also to Russia’s advance in northern Asia. Here too, “one cannot imagine Russia’s forward progress as having been guided by a systematic idea of conquest. Rather, matters usually stood so that the government in Petersburg would have had to expend far more energy to halt the movement in Asia than to carry it forward. The latter took place, as it were, of its own accord, in accordance with the commercial interests of the Russians and the personal interests of the local Russian power holders, and often enough confronted the Russian government with new, unwelcome, and difficult tasks. And then the fact made itself felt that an orderly state cannot in the long run tolerate predatory nomads on its borders, because by such people the very concept of a border is rendered illusory.”

If we bear in mind these various motives and factors of Russia’s advance into Siberia, we also have in them the history of this advance, frequently repeating itself in its details, as it unfolded over the first two and a half centuries. The advance to the seacoast in the Far East proceeded relatively quickly; it took scarcely half a century. But the route by which this goal was reached ran quite far north across the Asian continent. For in the southeast Russia reached a frontier whose resistance was formidable enough to halt the advance for two centuries: in the Amur region the Russian intruders collided with the Chinese state, which was then at the height of its power and military capability. To cross this frontier by force, Russian power was not yet strong enough, nor was it yet in the nature of the Russian advance to take up a struggle against extraordinary difficulties. A change in these tactics occurred only when Russia had become a European great power and thereby adopted the tasks of such a power in the colonial sphere. Not until the mid-nineteenth century did Muravyov deliberately take up the struggle for these regions, which two centuries earlier the Russians had already temporarily annexed during their sudden “rush” across Northern Asia, but had been forced to return to their lawful owner in the Treaty of Nerchinsk. For the intervening period, a few regesta will suffice.
