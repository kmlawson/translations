8 August.
Today we even encountered a floating department store. The goods had reached Sretensk by rail. There the merchant constructed an ordinary raft, built a booth upon it, and stacked his wares inside. Now he travels from Cossack village to Cossack village, and once he has sold out his goods, he will sell his raft into the bargain in Nikolayevsk and travel back upriver on the next steamer.

[[p279]]

Among the deck passengers is a man who is still young, but shockingly hollow-cheeked. He suffers from galloping consumption, but still hopes to reach his home town of Reval. In the cold, wet weather, the poor fellow shivers so pitifully that it is heartbreaking to see. Although the passengers assist him as best they can, he is already so weak that he will scarcely reach home.

This afternoon we observed a very interesting phenomenon. The river, which is bordered almost along its entire length by low hills, suddenly makes a sharp turn and flows in a nearly closed curve along steeply sloping, brightly glowing white hillsides, on which small puffs of smoke can be observed. These are the so-called Burning Mountains, past which we are traveling, and which are found in several places in this region. The Russians explain the phenomenon by the assumption that a seam of coal is gradually burning away. This view is correct to the extent that a coal seam is indeed present, running along the wall at about three-quarters of its height. However, [[p280]] the fire is sustained mainly by naphtha gases escaping from the coal seam, which evidently ignite spontaneously. For in winter, neither smoke nor, at night, the formation of flames is said to appear. Now it is very interesting to observe how fire and water have worked together to cause the river positively to eat its way into the mountain. Even now one can clearly see that the river valley originally ran fairly straight. The loop, which later expanded into an almost closed circle, formed only gradually. The question is which force is the primary one and promotes the erosive activity most strongly, that of fire or that of water. Probably four different factors combine to produce the gnawing effect: the flowing water of the river, precipitation, fire, and the expansive force of freezing ice. The process may perhaps be conceived as follows: the fire begins first; so long as a sufficient quantity of oxygen can reach the coal seam, the fire digs into the mountain. The rain carries sand downward from the overlying layers of soil; part of this sand remains lying on the edge of the cavity, and the cavity partially fills with water. When frost arrives, the water freezes, and the surface of the steep wall above and below the cavity likewise freezes; and when a thaw sets in, the soil has been loosened by the expansive force of the ice, so that rain- or meltwater coming from above has an easy task washing the loosened layer into the river. Because the soil situated above the naphtha-bearing coal seam now also tumbles down, and the residues left lying in the combustion cavity, together with the underlying layer of sand, are washed into the river, the coal seam is exposed once more; and when the escaping gases ignite again under the influence of the spring sun or as a result of electrical discharge, the same process can begin anew. In any case, however—whether this theory be correct or not—it could be established that the erosive power of the river is not the primary force. This is evident from the fact alone that the river does not simply undermine the soil so that it would have to break off [[p281]] downward under the force of gravity. Rather, the bank sloping against the Burning Mountain runs parabolically, that is, in the same way as the slope of a sandhill formed by constantly letting dry sand run, say, from a funnel onto the top of the hill, so that the sand can follow the law of gravity downward evenly along the slopes. The river thus has enough to do simply washing away the earth carried by the other forces
