In short, this time too Japan had kept its eyes open with regard to Manchuria and sent two warships to Newchwang to protect the settlement. Since the operations in China were regarded as a joint undertaking, and Japan was perhaps the most loyal of all the powers when it came to subordinating itself to a unified supreme command, the Japanese saw nothing amiss in their ships at Newchwang being placed under the Russian commander Alekseyev, who was their senior in both rank and age. The day of the engagement came; fighting went on for an entire day, Yingkou was even bombarded, and during all these engagements, as well as those that followed, it did not occur to the Russian commander even once to call upon the Japanese subordinate to him—who were merely awaiting his orders—to take part. That is typically Russian! In the face of these developments, the Japanese, who throughout the joint operations had never introduced the slightest discord into the concert of the powers and had conscientiously avoided all friction, confined themselves to withdrawing their ships with the onset of the cold season and lodging a diplomatic protest against the nature of Russian conduct in Manchuria. Thus the Russians had in fact succeeded once again in squeezing the Japanese out here as well, and they could now trumpet to the world that they alone had saved Newchwang and Manchuria, although the Japanese would gladly have helped, and perhaps the English as well. The next step that Russia took after this “rescue”, however, was to claim permanent possession of slant-eyed Manchuria from China, and it ultimately met with compliance, if only in the sense that a highwayman meets with compliance from the princely father of his abducted bride.

On 9 August, a total of seven Russian, two Japanese, and one British warship lay in the harbour. The commander of the Russian gunboat “Ovotschne”, de Collongue, had been appointed temporary civil governor of Newchwang, but a change was already made in the administration on 10 August. The former Russian consul in Newchwang, Ostroverkhov, was appointed civil administrator, while the aforementioned de Collongue became town commandant.

On 18 August, the consuls received the following letter from Civil Governor Ostroverkhov: “In accordance with the instructions I have received from the Commander-in-Chief of the province of Kwantung and of the forces stationed therein, I have the honour to transmit to you herewith a copy of the translation of the ‘Regulations for the Imperial Russian Provisional Civil Administration of the Port of Newchwang’, which was confirmed on 27 July (9 August by our calendar, that is, four days after the capture of the city) by His Excellency Vice-Admiral Alekseyev.”

I am in a position to publish the text of this official document, which may be of historical interest; it reads:

“Following the occupation of the port of Newchwang by Imperial Russian troops, the administration has been established as follows:

1. The highest administrative official is the Chief Administrator, who is appointed by the Commander-in-Chief of the province of Kwantung and the troops stationed therein, and confirmed by imperial edict.

2. An administrative council with an advisory vote is attached to the Administrator. This council shall be convened, if necessary, and composed as follows: the town commandant, a representative of the consular corps, a representative of the foreign merchant community, a representative of the Chinese guilds, the Commissioner of Customs, and the district physician. This administrative council shall be presided over by the Civil Governor or, in the event of his absence, by the town commandant, who is also to deputise for the Civil Administrator in cases of illness. Furthermore, a municipal council consisting of representatives of the Chinese merchant community is attached to the Civil Administrator, to be convened in matters concerning native merchants and residents. Two secretaries are assigned to the Civil Administrator, as are the following officials, who are heads of the various administrative departments: 1. the chief of police, 2. the tax inspector and treasurer, 3. the president of the municipal court, and 4. the district physician, in addition to the necessary staff of interpreters. The town commandant has been appointed to defend the port against any contingencies and to secure the proper transit of goods by land and river. All troops stationed in the locality are subordinate to him, with the exception of soldiers employed in police service. The rights and duties of the commandant shall be regulated by special orders of the Commander-in-Chief.

3. The Civil Administrator has the right to issue martial laws, to levy poll taxes on the natives, and to dispose of the movable and immovable property of the Chinese government. He decides on the appropriation of funds that are allocated or collected to defray administrative costs and municipal expenses, and maintains relations with foreign representatives.
