It would, after all, have been the most natural thing for the two of us, as the only Germans, to have become acquainted with each other first. But no, each skirts around the other; strangers to one another, because no opportunity has yet presented itself to be mutually—introduced! Only later, at the end of the journey, are we introduced to each other through third parties. In the evenings, things are generally very lively in the lounge; there is a piano in it, a lady plays the violin, the professor sings Czech songs, and we hold a musical soirée. On the night of August 25, we reach the Yenisei, which we cross on a mighty bridge of iron construction at Krasnoyarsk station, which gleams in electric light. The mighty river reaches deep into Chinese Turkestan, and along its course lie numerous settlements extending almost to the Arctic Ocean. The next day we pass the stations of Achinsk and Mariinsk; in the afternoon we reach Taiga station, which lies in the middle of the densest primeval forest and takes its name from it. Here a branch line connects, leading up to Tomsk, the capital of Western Siberia. Tomsk is the most populous city in Siberia, the seat of a university and various technical schools. It is the most important commercial center of the western parts of Siberia. While the railway has so far essentially followed the old Siberian cart tract, which crossed the Urals near Yekaterinburg and then led via Tyumen, Tobolsk, and Tara to Tomsk and continued from there, from now on the line keeps further south, crosses the Ob—one of Siberia's great rivers alongside the Lena, Yenisei, and Irtysh—on a massive bridge near Krivoshchekovo, and runs via Kainsk to Omsk, where it crosses the Irtysh. The taiga character of the landscape now comes to an end, and the vast Kirghiz steppe comes into its own. The journey [[p316]] through the steppe is very exhausting; the stations are far apart, often consisting of only a few small houses. Otherwise, however, the gaze sweeps across the flat landscape without finding any points upon which it can linger. Countless herds graze within pens, and in vain does one look out for a native Kirghiz. It is truly characteristic of these regions that one can travel for days through the steppe without catching sight of a single native. At intermediate stations we repeatedly pass oncoming trains. The passenger trains frequently carry a dozen carriages of emigrants, who enjoy extraordinarily cheap fares and various other forms of care during their journey. At every station they are provided with hot water free of charge; there is usually also an opportunity for cooking, and local peasants offer provisions for sale. Even the religious needs of the emigrants are catered for, as prie-dieus—one might almost say chapels—have frequently been set up in some corner of the third-class waiting rooms, in which divine service is held. The stations all look more or less alike: wooden buildings, wooden platforms, and never lacking the characteristic water tower and the wood yard that supplies the engines with fuel. From Omsk onward, the stations already make a distinctly European impression. From now on we also find itinerant booksellers at the stations, and even German books and newspapers, which are eagerly bought and read. To be sure, they are not newspapers from the German Empire, but German papers from St. Petersburg and Moscow. Of literature from the German Empire, however, one does not get a high opinion if one is to judge by the stocks found at the station booksellers. It is almost exclusively the travel reading published by Otto Janke that serves as the representative of German literature. I pick out German translations of Russian authors and eagerly study the novels of Tolstoy, Dostoevsky, and Turgenev.

There are also stalls at the wooden stations where one can buy souvenirs, mostly manufactured in the Urals. At one station stands a stall with magnificent carvings of mammoth ivory, which had been preserved in the earth for several thousand years. Now and then, peasant women offer for sale very artfully modeled figures made of kneaded, rock-hard dried, and painted bread dough.

We pass Petropavlovsk, cross the Tobol near Kurgan, and in the night of August 28–29 reach Chelyabinsk station. Here the Trans-Siberian Railway ends. Here begins the Zlatoust–Samara Railway, which connects the Trans-Siberian Railway across the Ural Mountains with the European rail network. On the journey between Kurgan and Chelyabinsk, we passed an obelisk that marks the political border between Asia and Europe and is at the same time intended as a monument to Yermak. Chelyabinsk already lies in Europe; the political boundary thus extends beyond the natural boundary, the Ural Mountains, and incorporates into Europe administratively a tract of land that geographically still belongs to Asia. —
