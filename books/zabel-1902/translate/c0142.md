These, however, are difficulties that cause the Russian engineers such headaches that for years now they have been telling the world what enormous technical difficulties they have to overcome on the Circum-Baikal railway, and what capable men they fundamentally are to accomplish such a feat. Yet even on the plains, their work holds up remarkably poorly to expert criticism. The railway on which we have just arrived is also quite carelessly built. Already now, barely a year after its opening, the embankment has subsided in places to such an extent that, despite the slow speed, the carriages continuously sway to and fro. Operation is therefore conducted only with great caution, and at every turn some sort of sloppiness comes to light that severely compromises operational safety on this section.

Once the morning mists have dispersed, the clear green expanse of water lies before us; a chain of hills and mountains rims the lake. In hazy outlines one can see the other shore lying opposite; in this region the lake is at its narrowest. The landing stage is protected against the swell by a massive wooden breakwater built out into the water, against which the spray of the surf dashes up nearly ten meters high. Moored alongside it is the steamer, the icebreaker *Angara*, which will take us to the other shore. The much larger four-funneled icebreaker *Baikal* is still awaited from the other side. It is no longer used at all to carry passengers, but solely for transporting freight trains. The railway tracks inside the steamer are linked at the landing stage with the tracks on land. A bascule bridge, which can be raised and lowered by an elevator depending on the water level of the lake, forms the movable link between shore and hold, over which the freight trains are shunted into the train ferry. Since our luggage has been checked through to Irkutsk, the railway administration takes care of the loading, and the passengers need only proceed to the elegant salon of the steamer, which is built on the model of an ocean-going vessel, in order, after four hours, to disembark on the western side at Baikal station, opposite Listvyannichnaya, at a similarly equipped landing stage. A brisk wind blows against us from the southwest. The waves come dead ahead; they are short, high waves that set the icebreaker, which as such has no keel forward but is flat-bottomed, into violent pitching motions. As a result, more than ninety percent of the passengers are seasick. Such an epidemic outbreak of seasickness I have never yet witnessed. Particularly among the deck passengers, who generally possess less willpower, one can see veritable corpses, who only begin to revive somewhat as the waves grow lower, the closer we approach the western shore of the lake. We pass an island from which the white masonry and tall tower of the Posolsky Monastery “of the Holy Transfiguration” shine picturesquely across. At Baikal station begins the so-called Central Siberian Railway, which brings us sixty kilometers to Irkutsk in four hours. When we land, our train is ready and waiting, and a veritable rush on it begins. Yet preparations have already been made for this. passengers have gathered does the stationmaster give the signal for the carriages to be opened. Now it is a case of: “First come, first served.” Not until four o’clock in the afternoon—customs clearance, the embarkation at Mysovaya, the disembarkation at Baikal, and the transfer of the luggage had delayed us that long—do we continue on our way. The railway runs along the left bank of the Angara. This is the voluminous outlet of Lake Baikal, connecting it to the river system of the Yenisei. In this way, the waters of the Selenga, the Tunguska (the so-called Upper Angara), and the numerous small coastal rivers emptying into the lake reach the Arctic Ocean. The Angara has an almost torrential gradient; it is considered the clearest and most uniformly cold river on earth. From various side valleys, its transparent, faintly green-tinted water, through which one can discern every little pebble on the fairly deep bottom, receives fresh inflow. Toward eight o’clock in the evening—it has already grown dark—numerous lights, indeed a veritable sea of lights, appear to our right on the other bank of the river. Irkutsk station is situated on the left bank of the Angara, but directly opposite, on the other side, lies the city with its more than 50,000 inhabitants, the capital of the Governorate of Eastern Siberia and, after Tomsk, the largest city in Siberia. A troika carries Nikolai Mikhailovich and me across the long wooden pontoon bridge over the Angara to the city with its numerous white houses, its golden and blue domes, the former residence of Muravyov.
