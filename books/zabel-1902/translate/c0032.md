In the year 1897, the following ships called at the port of Newchwang: 165 British, 71 German, 62 Chinese, 52 Japanese, 31 Swedish-Norwegian, 3 Dutch, and 1 (!) Russian. In the year 1898, there anchored in Newchwang 312 British, 211 Japanese, 144 German, and no Russian ships. In the last financial year, 1899, the figures had shifted as follows: 384 (!) Japanese ships, 316 British, 220 Chinese, 94 German, 46 Swedish-Norwegian, 7 Russian, and 2 Danish and 2 American ships each. The enormous increase in the Japanese share at the expense of that of the other countries is admittedly attributable primarily to the fact that during these years a truly outstanding market for beans opened up in Japan, and the Japanese naturally send their own ships to import this product into their country. Of the beans to the value of 8,691,236 taels that were shipped in 1899, approximately 93 percent went to Japan alone. On the other hand, the flourishing of Japanese trade in Newchwang must also be attributed to the energetic activity of the Japanese government. Apart from Britain, Japan is the only country that maintains a career consul in Newchwang, and Japanese consuls are known for working for their country's commerce, and working vigorously—something that often cannot be said of those of other nations, who regard their position more as a representative one. But not only that. Japanese students, disguised as Chinese, [[p71]] traversed the country and thus became acquainted with trade at its source. As a result, the Japanese colony in Newchwang is quite substantial, and given the business acumen and energy of this island nation, it would not surprise me if before long Newchwang becomes a port in which the Japanese element predominates. That German trade in particular could be so severely displaced in Newchwang I attribute mainly to the fact that the German Empire is inadequately represented there consularly in every respect. The administrator of the vice-consulate is an old man, an English merchant who also administers the

American, Norwegian-Swedish, and Dutch consulates. Even so, in a quarter of a century he has not been able to make a success of himself. That such a representative is not fit to set German trade in Newchwang back on its feet requires no proof. Newchwang is by no means a lost cause, and Germany would certainly have paid more attention to the place had its attention been properly drawn to it. But the acting German vice-consul did nothing to conduct himself in a manner appropriate to his position during this important and critical time. Throughout the entire period of the Russian occupation, when the other consuls, especially the Japanese and British, were sending home dispatch after dispatch, Mr. Baudinel was absent from almost all conferences of the consular corps; and when, on the occasion of a visit by Vice Admiral Alekseyev to Newchwang, it was a matter of safeguarding German interests (Cossacks had raided a German hotel), he did nothing other than write a subservient letter to the all-powerful Russian, in which he begged his forgiveness for not being able to pay [[p72]] his respects to him. I myself and other residents of Newchwang, during an audience we had with Alekseyev, saw this letter lying in the wastepaper basket (!). An Englishman drew our attention to it. Truly—shameful!

Unfortunately, Germany adheres to the principle of deciding to install consuls—especially career consuls—only after German interests have crystallized in a given place. The Japanese do it differently: as soon as a port is newly opened, they first place a consul there, and indeed a career consul, who studies the locality and local conditions and then draws commerce in his wake. The German Empire can learn a great deal from young Japan out here. In any case, we must endeavor to free ourselves from the principle that we have done enough when, in any place where the appointment of a consular representative has become an absolute and urgent necessity (for we simply will not make up our minds to take this step any earlier), we hand the consulate over to a merchant, who can never match even a mediocre career consul. What we need out here are career consuls, commercial experts as it were, and above all in vulnerable places such as Newchwang—men who know how to protect their own standing and that of the German Empire and German trade.
