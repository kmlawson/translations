Thereupon, the other two gentlemen's further concerns are discussed. Of interest in this regard is the Vice Admiral's solemn declaration that Russia will never compete with Newchwang through Port Arthur—an assurance received not entirely without skepticism, especially as the Admiral asserts on the other hand that the railway connecting Newchwang and Port Arthur would not be opened to public traffic before a year and a half has passed. Until that time, Russia itself requires the line exclusively for military purposes and for bringing up the necessary materials to complete the track. It is a rather comforting assurance for Newchwang that it is to do without the overland link to Port Arthur for another year and a half. Mr. Bush objects, however, that he cannot then understand why the railway is nevertheless already carrying general freight for private individuals once again. The Vice Admiral disputes this fact; Mr. Bush maintains it and substantiates it with examples. Personally, I have also seen carloads of bean cake being transported on the Mukden–Port Arthur line, and I know that other goods have come up from Port Arthur for which freight was paid, and at an extraordinarily high rate at that. Two possibilities therefore remain: either the railway officials are officially forbidden to transport private goods, but do so anyway and pocket the money, or Russia does not take the prohibition against using the line to transport merchandise belonging to private individuals all that seriously and maintains it only officially in order to enforce it in specific cases when it suits them. The latter assumption is probably correct, and the former cannot be ruled out. What can be clearly and distinctly gathered from Vice Admiral Alekseyev's remarks, however, is that Russia intends to maintain the current status in Manchuria for at least another year and a half, whether it decides to scale back its demands in response to the protests of the Powers or not. Its policy in this matter will be this: as long as the protest persists, it steps back slightly and contents itself with whatever small advantages are conceded. But once the energy of the protesters wanes, it will find its way back all the more surely, and then no one will pay attention. That is what Russia is counting on. We are always poking fun at England's jealousy in watching to ensure that Russia does not expand too quickly; we make fun of it and have no idea what debt of gratitude we actually owe England for this policy of acting as the world's gendarme, and specifically of standing guard against the Tsarist Empire. Perhaps later on we will finally bring ourselves to recognize that this was one of the many magnificent services England has rendered to the world at large, even if in the belief and with the intention of serving exclusively its own interests.
