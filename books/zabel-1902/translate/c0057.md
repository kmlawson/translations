What interests me in particular about the yen right now is the circumstance that mine are running out. I must therefore betake myself once again to a bank and change money. This activity always fills me with dread, because I know in advance that I will get annoyed, and that is something one generally avoids in the East. The annoyance, however, stems from the gruesome way one gets one’s throat cut by the banks when changing money. For this is how it is done.

First, the method of exchange customary with us! Suppose I come from France, have superfluous francs, and need English money for a journey to England to be undertaken shortly. Then I consult the exchange list, look up how many francs go to a pound sterling, take my francs, carry them to the banking house, and ask whether the firm will change my francs into pounds at the day’s rate, which it will gladly do. The banker deducts perhaps a quarter or at most, at the very most half a percent in exchange fees, and the matter is settled.

Now the Asian method. I have Mexican dollars that I want changed into roubles. The Mexican dollar is the currency in common circulation in China. Any sensible person would now think that the bank would fix an exchange rate between the Mexican dollar and the rouble. But by no means—the bank would not earn enough that way! So it slips an intermediate currency in between and first calculates the difference between the Mexican dollar and this intermediate link. (The exchange rate, of course, already contains a handsome profit for the bank.) Only then does it calculate the rate between the sum already converted into the intermediate rate and the roubles due on that sum. In that is contained the second high exchange profit. In Shanghai this intermediate link is called the “tael”; in Japan it is the yen. The bank is thus not content with a single exchange fee, but takes a double one, so that the bankers may become rich men all the faster.

[[p123]]

And what sort of percentages the banks operate with can be seen from the following. I go to a bank in Nagasaki and ask for the exchange rate of the Mexican dollar to the rouble. They explain to me quite simply: the difference between the Mexican dollar and the yen is five percent, the difference between the yen and the rouble is also five percent, so for 100 dollars you would get 90 roubles. So this band of robbers operates at ten percent! In Shanghai I was able to get roubles for dollars with a difference of at least only two percent. Only yesterday at the Russo-Chinese Bank I changed 100 dollars and received 96 roubles for them after all. This morning I return there, bringing along a whole bag full of silver dollars. There is no particularly great value in it, but 500 dollars in silver is quite a bagful. I had not wanted to drag it around with me the day before. I want to have my dollars changed into roubles, each and every one. They calculate the sum for me; it is much lower than I had calculated it to be. Yes, they explain to me, the silver dollar has fallen by four percent since yesterday. Today it stands at 92 to 100 roubles. The rouble has made a big “jump.” To that I tell the bank clerk he may kindly let his roubles “jump” as much as they want to jump, take my 500 dollars under my arm again, pack them into my rickshaw, and change them in Vladivostok at Kunst & Albers, who charge somewhat more civilised percentages. What makes the Asian banks a public menace to the harmless traveller is the undeniable fact that, in addition to their interpolated intermediate rate, these institutions also have two different rates that are applied depending on whether the banks are buying or selling. If, for instance, one goes into the bank and wants pounds for one’s dollars, one has to pay a sum for the pounds that is considerably higher than what one would receive if one wanted to change the same sum of pounds into dollars, quite apart from the loss caused by the double conversion, which would be the same in both cases. Therefore, traveller, should you ever set your feet in these parts, beware of the banks! They have corrupted the entire monetary circulation in East Asia.

What varieties of currency one has to deal with in East Asia in the small area between Hong Kong, Shanghai, Chefoo, Tianjin, Port Arthur, and Nagasaki, I should like to mention briefly: first, Hong Kong dollars; second, Chinese dollars; third, Mexican dollars; fourth, cash; fifth, Haikwan taels; sixth, Kaifang taels; seventh, roubles; eighth, yen. That the banks make their cut with such a multiplicity of currencies within what is, viewed from the standpoint of travel and trade, only a very small circle, one cannot hold against them; but *how* they make it is nothing other than simply . . . . shameless!
