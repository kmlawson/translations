## Chapter IV. Through the Liaodong Peninsula.

### 1. By the Manchurian Railway to Port Arthur.

23 February.

Every morning at 9 o'clock a train departs from Newchwang in the direction of Dashiqiao, the junction station for the Mukden–Port Arthur line. I do not wish to linger in Newchwang any longer, but intend to reach Chefoo as quickly as possible so that I can send several reports home from there, as I do not trust the Russian post. The day before yesterday, therefore, immediately after my audience with Alekseyev, I sat down and repeated in writing my request for a proper passport for travel through northern Manchuria, asking that the passport be sent to me in Chefoo.*) This morning I depart. After much running back and forth at the Russian railway station an hour away from Newchwang, I finally manage to have a ticket issued. It cost nothing. Although I arrived a full hour before the train's departure, issuing the ticket, signing and countersigning, and whatever else had to be done took up so much time that I almost missed the train. At last I am seated in [[p78]] a third-class compartment (others are not available, as all the first- and second-class carriages are occupied by officers and civil officials). As long as the trip costs nothing and one must be glad to be taken along at all on a railway serving exclusively military purposes, there can be no objection. Besides, quite a number of officers who do not have a private compartment are traveling in the same carriage. We pass through a treeless but well-cultivated plain; the train stops at several small stations until at last in Dashiqiao it is: "All change!" as the carriage in question continues on to Mukden. In German, Dashiqiao means "big stone bridge." And such a bridge does indeed exist.*)

::: notes
*) As was to be assumed from the outset, I have not yet (1902) come into possession of the passport and can hardly hope to do so any longer. At the same time, this confirms my assumption that I would never have received permission to travel through Manchuria if I had begun my journey from Port Arthur, as I had originally intended.
:::

Here I have a layover of about an hour, as we must wait for the train coming down from Mukden. I leave my luggage in the care of my boy, who sits down on it, while a few hundred coolies loiter about and try to snatch the pieces of luggage out of his hands unless he beats them off. The unemployed coolies are an outright plague in Manchuria wherever they have to deal with Russians. An incredibly disrespectful rabble! And the Russians are to blame for it. In Dashiqiao, for example, four or five soldiers have been equipped with knouts and tasked with keeping the bothersome coolies away from the trains. They sweep among them like madmen, but the coolies are usually nimbler than they are and skillfully dodge the blows, thoroughly enjoying the spectacle of these raging Rolands. The Cossacks clearly find amusement in it as well, and the whole thing makes the impression of a comical game of tag to the delight of the soldiers and the Chinese; if a blow does occasionally land, the Chinese takes no offense, but it certainly inspires no respect for Russian authority.

It happens to be the time of the Chinese New Year. I am reminded of this when I see a long New Year's procession with all manner of masquerade coming along. A number of people in whimsical theatrical costumes—their faces painted, some disguised as women—approach. They are all walking on stilts strapped to their legs. The procession proceeds to the Russian station and the officers' quarters to perform its dances there. The Chinese regards New Year greetings as a welcome opportunity to beg, and the Russian is generally not stingy on such occasions. My boy, the Shandong man, explains to me that these are people from Zhili, as the New Year stilt procession performed here is customary among them. Manchus and Shandong people would never do such a thing.

The train from Mukden pulls in, my luggage flies into the compartment, and I immediately occupy an entire bench arranged to fold down. On it I spread out my blankets and furs, and my bed for the night is ready.
