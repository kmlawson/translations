#### 9 August.

My work is progressing quite briskly, so briskly that I have already run out of writing paper. When we put in at a Cossack settlement in the afternoon to take on wood, I go into the local village store intending to buy some paper. Writing paper has just run out. I therefore buy grey wrapping paper, carefully cut it to size for my typewriter, and continue writing on that. In a pinch, even the devil eats flies!

[[p282]]

10 August.

We are lying off Albazin, the historic Albazin of Tolbuzin and Beuton; at least, the present-day Albazin has been rebuilt on the very site where, in 1561, the first outpost of Russian power in the Amur Valley was founded by Chernigovsky. We are thus on historic ground. The village lies on the bank, which rises quite high. One can still discern the remains of the old fortifications that were once so heroically defended against the Chinese. Reverently, much as we at home might visit the battlefield of Leipzig, my Russian fellow travellers inspect these sites so rich in memories. We linger by Tolbuzin’s grave and by the monument erected to him and his Cossacks, as well as to Beuton and his streltsy, by a grateful Russia. The importance of present-day Albazin does not rise above that of the other Cossack villages along the Amur.

11 August.

It is Sunday once again. This is noteworthy if only because the arduous journey on the Amur has now lasted a full three weeks. The day brings little variety. It is very warm. I feel sluggish and yawn, which is notoriously contagious. That is probably why I see nothing but yawning faces today. From this I gather that the day seems terribly boring to other people besides me.

[[p283]]

12 August.

It is raining as hard as the heavens can pour. It rained all through the night, and water is dripping through the cabin ceiling onto the table, onto our legs, onto our papers. Although this introduces a little variety into the general monotony, it is not exactly pleasant. We therefore complain to Herr Neumann, and he sends us a sailor to putty up the cracks. This is duly done, but not properly; the rain softens the

putty again, and the water drips merrily through the ceiling just as before. Now we vigorously demand a remedy and let Herr Neumann hear a few flattering remarks about his ship, which he takes amiss. Furthermore, the sailors begin throwing wood against the cabin wall again, and our renewed complaint about it bears no fruit, because Herr Neumann is cross with us. But he is mistaken if he thinks he can defy us. Thank God there is a complaint book on board! Every captain and every shipowner has an immense respect for it. mitted to the authorities, and a justified complaint can become quite unpleasant for the captain and shipowner. And so, Herr Neumann, we request the complaint book. His face falls, but he has to hand the book over. We will not write a complaint into it, although that would have been only fair and proper, but we will, in a sense, keep the complaint book in our custody for the time being as a hostage for decent treatment. The effect is instantaneous. Herr Neumann suddenly hits upon the clever idea that the rain leaking through the cabin roof can very easily be prevented simply by laying the waterproof tarpaulin across the cabin roof, and secondly, that the banging about with the wooden logs can also very easily be stopped by personally giving a sailor, who happens once again to be hurling wood against the cabin wall, a blow with the rope's end.

[[p286]]

In this way we suddenly have everything we could possibly wish for. The complaint book, however, is locked away for the time being and will not be surrendered until we are in Sretensk. Ever since we asked for the complaint book, Herr Neumann and the “commandir” have given us a wide berth, as far as the space on the “Kharbin” permits, and any wishes we express are promptly fulfilled. Indeed, a complaint book is an extraordinarily sensible institution!

13 August.
Our steamer does make rather slow progress; it runs at only about 15 kilometres an hour. The current of the river on the upper reaches is considerably stronger than before, amounting to nearly five kilometres, so that we advance at a speed of little more than ten kilometres. Towards midday we are overtaken by a small tugboat belonging to the postal line, which is towing a barge behind it, similar to the one on which we were towed across the bar, only with the difference that the barge has cabin accommodations where the passengers [[p287]] are housed. When we put in at Pokrovskaya at noon, the tugboat is just casting off again.
