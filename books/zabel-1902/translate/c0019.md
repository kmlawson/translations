### 2. On the Draisine.

2 February.
This morning my draisine journey begins. I have my luggage loaded onto this strangest of all vehicles in which I have ever travelled, then wrap myself up thoroughly in furs and woollen blankets, have two ropes tied to the front of the draisine, harness four coolies to them who have to pull, and promptly at 8 o’clock in the morning the journey gets under way on the lonely line. I am completely alone, accompanied by no one except my Chinese servant. The two of us are perched high atop the trunks, the revolver sits loosely in my belt, and my rifle lies beside me. Now let the robbers come.

What trouble it is with the coolies! The fellows exert themselves like the strongmen of Hamburg and yet make no headway. Finally I give up the tiresome shouting of “kuai-kuai” (“forward, forward!”) and resign myself to my fate that, after all, we shall proceed at nothing faster than a walking pace. Fortunately, the fierce wind of recent days has died down. From my high perch I let my eyes wander across the country, but what can be seen is no more than a rather dreary salt steppe to the right and left, now and then a few villages, a couple of ploughed fields, a frozen puddle. Before me the dull, yawn-inducing railway line that stretches out of sight, and otherwise nothing, not a bird, not a sound in nature except the squeaking of my draisine's ungreased wheels.

The railway line itself is also passable only in sections. In several places the track has been torn up, and we then have to drag the draisine over the bare ground to the next spot where we can put it back on the rails. For that matter, the railway is poorly built. Often there are gaps of several decimetres between the sleepers and the roadbed; the ground has subsided that much in certain places. Of the bridges and overpasses along the entire line, not a single one has been completed; instead, the tracks have for the time being been routed around the spots in question. Only those bridges that were indispensably necessary have been finished.

After about two hours, my four coolies go on strike. They have had nothing to eat yet, they say, and refuse to go any further. Nothing remains for me but to pay the men off and fetch new coolies from a nearby village.

After a while a draisine comes towards me. It is a Russian patrol from Goubangzi station, which I hope to reach today. The soldiers halt; one of us had to lift his draisine off the track to let the other pass. In order to initiate communication, I show the soldiers an identification document that Losev wrote into my diary, but not one of the men can read, even though it is in Russian. But since they evidently possess the requisite respect for the written word as such, they lift their draisine off the track and let mine pass. After an almost endless journey, during which, however, no untoward incidents arise, I finally arrive in the early afternoon, around 3 o’clock, at Goubangzi, the junction station where the line forks in the direction of Mukden and Newchwang. With joy I see the white-blue-red flag gleaming already in the distance. There the second battalion of the Second [[p45]] East Siberian Rifle Regiment is quartered. The station lies in the open field; no hill rises nearby, not a tree is to be found; only to the west in the distance can the Guangliangshan ridge be seen.

The first thing I do is report to the Russian supreme commander. It is Colonel Belatsky. Astonished questions as to how I came to this region, who

I was, what I wanted here, amazement that I had gotten through unmolested; only yesterday on that same stretch between Dalinghe and Goubangzi a Russian wagon transport was attacked by more than a hundred well-armed bandits, the entire region is constantly being made unsafe by Chinese gangs and regular troops, it is completely out of the question for me to travel further. I ask that they might then provide me with an escort or allow me to travel from stage to stage with the patrols. That cannot be done without orders from above. Had I not called upon Colonel Modl, then? That I had prudently not done; he certainly would not have let me travel. In short, the result is: I am welcome as a guest at the station, but for a continuation [[p46]] of the journey the colonel cannot take responsibility, unless under military escort, with a “convoi”. Otherwise I would simply have to turn back. For that matter, the colonel himself does not speak a word of German, but the regimental doctor, Dr. Petersen, acts as intermediary. He is a most charming gentleman, and with his help I finally succeed in persuading the colonel to telegraph to Colonel Medl at Chenchenzi that a German étranger had arrived unexpectedly at the station, intended to travel overland to Port Arthur since the sea route was closed, and requested after the fact the grant of a convoi, as further travel without a convoi was impossible for security reasons. — I myself do not even know what a dangerous journey I made today, and in the course of all these negotiations I acquire a positive respect for myself, yet I think to myself: Come what may, at all events I am right in the middle of Manchuria, have seen many of the things I wanted to see, and if they want to see me out again by one route or another, they cannot very well blindfold me after all. And even if they do, well, that will do me no harm now either. So I resign myself to my fate and am not at all displeased that I am to take a day of rest tomorrow at the warm station, seeing that things do not move so quickly with the Russian telegraph.
