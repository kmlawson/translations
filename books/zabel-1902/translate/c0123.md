#### July 28.

As the Sunday morning breaks across the land, I awake. It has turned very chilly; the iron deck and the layer of earth have chilled me through and through. Nikolai Mikhailovich’s orderly is just waking up as well; his master is still asleep, like most of the other companions in misfortune. After all, we have a field kitchen with us! So we are going to make tea. The guardsman chops some wood found somewhere or other, and on a stove standing by the bowsprit we boil water for tea. Then the newly purchased provisions, about which Nikolai Mikhailovich knows nothing yet, are unpacked; I have my eye especially on a tin of Brunswick liver sausage. When the tea is ready, Nikolai Mikhailovich is woken up. How his eyes widen when he sees our preparations! An unexpected feast at an early hour! Yesterday evening, in the excitement, no one had had anything to eat, of course. How good it tastes to us now! The orderly also gets a generous brandy.

Overnight we have already come considerably closer to our new steamer. Even so, we have covered at most three-quarters of the distance. About a quarter of a kilometer ahead of us on the bank, we see a long chain of people—our hauling gang! It is 10 o’clock in the morning by the time we lie alongside the *Sibir*. We have thus taken thirteen hours for a distance of nine kilometers. Consequently, we traveled a kilometer in a little under an hour and a half, or to be exact: we covered 11½ meters per minute. A week ago today we departed from Khabarovsk. We [[p248]] [Illustration: On the towed barge across the bar (p. 230).]

[[p249]]

have covered 366 versts since then. As a result, from Khabarovsk to the point where we reached the *Sibir*, we traveled at a speed of 2¼ kilometers per hour. These are speeds that, in the realm of travel by modern means of transport, can undoubtedly pass as records for slowness.

The *Sibir* at least outwardly deserves the name of passenger steamer. It has a proper sun deck, compartments for different classes, a large saloon, though admittedly only a single washroom and a single toilet for all the cabin passengers. That both are soon in an almost unbearable state is due less to the steamer than to the passengers.

My ticket is for cabin number 5. Going in search of the number in question with the pleasant sensation of finally being able to sleep in a cabin, I find it already occupied once again. But I am fed up now with perpetual consideration. Consequently, I fetch the captain. In the meantime, however, four animatedly arguing parties, whose tickets all bear number 5, have already gathered in the cabin, which contains only two berths. I have the fortune of being the fifth with number 5. Meanwhile, I chime in shouting and scolding vigorously in German, heaping the bitterest reproaches upon the captain. But my opponents are—ladies, who from the mere fact that they are ladies construct the right to claim every comfort entirely for themselves at the expense of their male fellow travelers. There is simply no more ruthless creature on earth than the traveling Russian woman. One must fight tooth and nail against her if one does not wish to be flayed, but after all, no one really holds it against you. In recent days the drama with my deck chair started up again, just as on the *Daphne*. At first I remained polite. But I could not get by with that in the long run, because my politeness was interpreted as good nature. So if I felt like lying down on my chair and someone was already lying in it, I asked them, whether male or female, to get up, because I needed the chair myself now; only to the latter did I occasionally permit sitting on it as well, on a corner at my feet. Which they were quite glad to do.

On the cabin question, the captain finally settles the matter by evicting third-class passengers who have already settled themselves comfortably in the second-class saloon, and accommodating us surplus men there together. In itself, nothing can be said against this. Only one thing is disagreeable to me. It turns out, namely, that my sofa berth lies right between that of the Orthodox priest and that of the school inspector. That does not suit me at all. I can almost say “fortunately” someone was left over who had not gotten a place either: namely, the colonel. It therefore works out very well that I can place my spot in the cabin at his disposal. Now he has the pleasure, and I sleep once again on my deck chair outside under the sun deck. On this long journey, my chair has become one of my few true friends.

[[p250]]

Around 10:30 we are already in full steam. The *Sibir* is a sternwheeler, a model typical for the upper reaches of the Amur. We are now traveling through the Lesser Khingan, whose hills hem in the riverbed. China to the left, Russia to the right. But not a single Chinese junk is to be seen, only Russian flags, on the banks both on this side and the other. No doubt about it, Russia here as well as there! Late in the evening we drop anchor near the picturesquely situated Radde, named after the famous explorer of Siberia of the same name.
