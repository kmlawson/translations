# Book 1. Through Manchuria.

[[p21]]

## Chapter I. From Peking to the Eastern End of the Great Wall.

### 1. From Peking to Tanggu.

Peking, January 27, 1901 (the Kaiser’s birthday).

Peking, future international garrison city! The occupation of the Legation Quarter is already underway, and individual plots are already being surveyed and plans drawn up for barracks buildings. In the entire quarter between the Hatamen and Qianmen on the one side and Sir Robert Hart Street on the other, there is no longer a single house that does not bear the marking: “Occupied”—by this or that legation. The German legation is particularly conspicuous. Walking down Legation Street, one sees that almost the entire quarter lying between the German mission building and the Hatamen bears the inscription: “Occupied by the German Legation.” Recently, notices have also been posted with the remark: “Building site of the German protection force.” Only here and there has a foreign power smuggled itself in, its seal to be found on some small Chinese property. To make up for this, however, the German minister has in turn requisitioned plots in foreign sectors. Later on, matters can easily be sorted out; there will be swapping back and forth until everything is neatly rounded off. It would not be bad at all if this policy were applied on a grander scale. Even now, after all, England leaves its leased territory at Weihaiwei virtually lying fallow. Some people who hear the grass grow whisper that [[p22]] England intends, should all of Shandong ever become German, to offer Weihaiwei as a present to the German Emperor or use it as an object of exchange à la Heligoland. That, of course, is nonsense. To be sure, it would not be unwelcome if we, too, held in other spheres of interest some small, relatively worthless scrap of land that we could, when the opportunity arose, dispose of profitably and at good value. In any case, the eventual arrangement among the powers within China would not be nearly so perilous for us in such an eventuality if we took precautions for it in good time. Whoever holds the most at the moment of partition will, by all human reckoning, also keep the most.

Since the domestic concerns of the future international fortress right next to the imperial palace in the sacred city of Peking hold little interest for me, however, and I have in particular a strong aversion to taking part in the garrison life developing in Peking over the coming months with all the unpleasantness of military routine, I decide on a side trip to Manchuria, from where rumors of war and alarms of battle still penetrate even as far as Peking. There I hope still to find a rewarding field of work. So today I pack up the bare essentials for the journey, inform the most faithful of my Chinese boys, Wang Faxian, that tomorrow morning at 7:30 we must arrive at the Peking railway station opposite the Temple of Heaven together with our baggage, and instruct him to prepare for an extended journey. As usual, he has no objection.

January 28.

Anyone who has closely followed Russia’s policy in East Asia over recent years could hardly be in any doubt that Russia would exploit the turmoil of 1900 to secure some advantage for its own private account. Year after year one sees Russian advances in fields where they would never have been expected; the financial community in particular found the extraordinary prosperity of the Russo-Chinese Bank rather surprising. Yet with us in Germany, the constant refrain regarding the question of “Russia in East Asia” is: “quieta non movere!” When the masses are in motion, however, letting them continue to flow wherever they please simply will not do. In my view, it is impossible for us to remain content with adopting an attitude of “laisser faire, laisser aller” toward Russia’s East Asian policy. On the other hand, among the commercial powers that share interests in East Asia, the saying goes: “Russia is the one and only enemy we all have in East Asia.” It is absolutely essential to take a clear stance on this question for once. Russia has withdrawn its entire military force to Manchuria. No one knows what it is doing there, what its political intentions are, or what its commercial-political desires and accomplishments may be. The British press claims that Russia has annexed Manchuria. No one knows whether it is true. Manchuria is sealed tight against news. I shall attempt to penetrate the secret. This task will not be easy.

[[p23]]

Ever since its complete restoration, the railway from Peking to Tianjin and beyond has been in German hands. Four companies of the Railway Regiment were assigned the task of bringing the line into operation. It is well known that all the nations took part in repairing the railway line. Count Waldersee regarded restoring this link between Peking and the sea as one of the first and most important tasks remaining to be accomplished upon his arrival, and so the work was pushed forward zealously and finally completed in mid-December 1900. The work was delayed most by the reconstruction of the destroyed bridges, especially the large Peiho Bridge near Yangcun, the building of which had fallen to the German railway battalion under Major Bauer. The line is secured by numerous staging posts of all nations, which stretch along both the railway track and the highway, permitting a completely safe and hazard-free journey from Peking. The first posts along the railway, counting from Peking, belong to the Japanese. Those that follow are mostly German or French.
