Should anyone intending to make this trip ever get hold of these pages, I might perhaps earn their gratitude by disclosing the only method I know to avoid this exploitation, at least in Nagasaki. There exists a considerable number of Japanese money-changers there. Their exchange rates naturally follow those of the banks as far as *whole* dollars, roubles, yen, etc. are concerned. When it comes to fractional coins, however, they call a *halt*. It is therefore best to have a coolie bring along a large [[p124]] bag into which one tosses the exchanged one-, three-, five-, and ten-kopeck pieces into which one converts one's dollars. For the smaller the coin, the greater the profit! For instance, in 20- and 15-kopeck pieces I received as much as 98 kopecks to the dollar, and in copper coins even 101 kopecks to the dollar. Compared to the exchange rate of 92 roubles for 100 dollars, this latter rate already represented a gain of twelve roubles on 100 dollars, which one can readily obtain in return for the small trouble of counting out the copper coins. In Vladivostok, every shopkeeper will gladly change the small coin. To be sure, this experiment remains effective only so long as it is not practiced too often. For if many people get wind of the secret and scour the tills of the small Japanese money-changers in Nagasaki for Russian copper coins, the law that goods become more expensive with rising demand will come into effect here too. Therefore, let whoever reads the recipe here not betray it to anyone else!

### 3. From Japan to Siberia.

2 July.

I slept on board the *Daphne* for the first time last night, and abominably at that. Fifteen years ago, the *Daphne* was still a first-class passenger steamer. One can really see from her what progress travel comforts have made in that span of time, or indeed how passengers' expectations have grown. If one winds one's demands back to the standpoint of fifteen years ago, then one can after all endure it on the *Daphne*. Old Captain Nissen, incidentally an experienced mariner well known along the entire coast of China, positively loves his *Daphne*, maintaining that she is a lucky ship. When one raises an objection, for instance that this is down to him because he is a lucky fellow, he contradicts with an ophimetic tilt of the head and asserts, “No...o, dat’s down to the ‘schip.’” Well, if I find myself feeling somewhat less uncomfortable on board his lucky ship, that is due less to the “schip” than to him. For he is truly an amiable captain, a real greybeard full of chit-chat. This voyage will be his last; then he will return in honour to Europe as a pensioner.

At 12 o’clock noon sharp, the *Daphne* weighs anchor. The last bales are stowed in the ship’s hold; they bear the inscription: “Export goods, from Hamburg to Vladivostok.” The official from Kunst & Albers, who has put a white Russian reserve officer’s cap on his head, waves a final greeting to the steamer from his agency boat; the manoeuvre of turning the steamer in the narrow fairway takes some time yet, then we steam past the French, Russian, and Japanese warships and out of the harbour. Forward flies the flag of Hamburg, on the signal mast the house flag of Kunst & Albers, on the other mast the Russian flag, and at the stern the German. Thus German-Russian is now the banner.

Outside, the weather is bad. The sun disappears, a brisk breeze springs up, and it rains. The captain, however, fears only fog, and prophesies a great future and boundless riches for whoever invents the binoculars through which one can [[p125]] see through the fog. The *Daphne* has her cabin accommodations aft; above them is the deck for the twenty passengers the steamer can accommodate—quite comfortable, of course, if one takes the standards of fifteen years ago as a baseline. The captain has even partially converted the deck into a palm grove, and the Chinese steward has hung his supply of bananas, brought from Hong Kong, from the highest pole supporting the canvas awning. Strolling, if not exactly under, then at least beside palms, and with alluring fruits of the south above us, we thus steer into Siberian waters. To be sure, it seems one is not meant to walk beside palms with impunity either, for the joy of walking does not last long. The wind becomes too violent, the rain drips through the light awning, and in the end it has to be taken down because of the wind. So, off to the cabin! Outside, however, it is storming, the rain pours down, the *Daphne* rocks and dances, the sea rages and demands its victim ... and gets it, too, sometimes right through the cabin window.

I have a cabin to myself. That is a great advantage. To be sure, I cannot imagine how anyone besides myself could have found room in it. On the other hand, it is also the rearmost in the ship. Directly beneath me, the propeller turns and the steering gear rumbles. On the side where the cabin adjoins the ship's side, it slopes down—a veritable upside-down garret room. The berth is very narrow. It is a so-called transverse bunk. When the ship rolls, I bang alternately headfirst against the ship's side and feet-first against the inner wall. But the cabin also possesses another, very wicked quality. Since it forms the extreme end of the vessel, whenever the ship pitches (and it pitches constantly), the cabin rises about a metre and a half into the air each time, and then sinks a metre and a half below the normal waterline, thus moving in oscillations of about three metres up and down, roughly fifteen oscillations a minute. A voyage of just over three days and three hours makes 75 hours. Forty-five metres a minute makes 2,700 metres in an hour, and in 75 hours 202,500 metres—that is, 202.5 kilometres added to the distance between Nagasaki and Vladivostok, which I travel free of charge! Really, then, forty yen is too little for the ticket!
