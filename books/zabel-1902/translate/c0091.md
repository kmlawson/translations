1681. Ignatius Milovanov, who undertakes an exploratory expedition to the lower Amur valley on the orders of the voivode of Nerchinsk, discovers on the left bank of the Amur near the mouth of the Zeya the Chinese town of Aigun, which had meanwhile been built as a bulwark against the Russians.

1683. Grigory Mylnikov is commissioned with a hundred Cossacks to travel down the Amur to bring reinforcements to a Russian settlement on the Amgun, a left tributary of the lower Amur. Near Aigun, he and his Cossacks are captured by a strong Chinese military force and taken to Beijing. (Here the prisoners formed the nucleus of a Russian colony that remains there to this day, though it has since become almost completely Mongolized.) In the following year, two of the captured Russians are sent to Albazin with a letter demanding the immediate evacuation of the place. The Russians resolve to hold the post.

1685. A Chinese force of allegedly 18,000 men attacks Albazin, which is defended for ten days by about 500 Cossacks under Tolbuzin. The Russians are granted free withdrawal to Nerchinsk; the Chinese burn down Albazin and the Russian settlements in its vicinity, withdraw downriver, abandon the town of Aigun built on the left bank of the Amur, and build a fortress of the same name three kilometers [[p189]] downriver, now on the right bank of the Amur, garrisoning it with a strong force; the greater part of the Chinese force returns via the Sungari to inner Manchuria.

1685. Ivan Vlasov, voivode of Nerchinsk, who does not agree to the evacuation of Albazin, sends Tolbuzin back, reinforced by a newly arrived Cossack detachment under a German named Beuthen, with orders to rebuild the ostrog. In the spring of 1686, a strong Chinese army appears before Albazin once again. The Russians, however, hold their ground and weaken the Chinese through sorties. In the course of one such sortie, Tolbuzin falls, and Beuthen continues the defense, which is so stubborn that throughout the winter the Chinese conduct the siege only very perfunctorily and conclude an armistice in May 1687, especially since peace negotiations have also been initiated. At the end of August, they abandon the siege entirely, and militarily the Russians remain masters of the situation. Through two special envoys, they demand that the Chinese enter into negotiations on a definitive settlement of the border. In fact, the cessation of hostilities against Albazin is due to an explicit request expressed in Beijing as early as 1686 by the envoys Nikifor Venyukov and Ivan Favorov. In the same year, the Russian peace negotiator Golovin leaves Moscow accompanied by a regiment of Streltsy. Meanwhile, negotiations over the venue of the conference drag on for quite some time.

1689. Not until the summer of 1689 is an agreement reached regarding the location, and two first-class mandarins leave Beijing toward Nerchinsk, where the negotiations are to take place. Although it has been agreed that the mandarins' escort should not be larger than strictly necessary for their protection, the Chinese entourage, which is almost entirely military, is nevertheless estimated at around 9,000 men. Under the pressure of these measures and as a result of Golovin's lack of diplomatic talent, an extraordinarily unfavorable peace for Russia is concluded at the end of August of that year, in the drafting of which two Jesuits brought along by the Chinese as interpreters—Fathers Gerbillon and Pereira—render them good service. Although Albazin has in fact not fallen, Russia surrenders all its possessions in the Amur basin in this first treaty it concludes with China. The border is initially to be formed by the Gorbitsa River, while its continuation to the sea is determined only in general terms. In the west, the Argun is accepted as the border. Albazin is razed, the Russian settlements on the right bank of the Argun are relocated to the left, and crossing the border is threatened with the death penalty. It is permitted, however, for people in possession of passports who pursue exclusively commercial purposes. With this, Russian expansion to the south and east, toward the Amur valley, is paralyzed for a period of over 180 years.

1697. Kamchatka is taken possession of by Vladimir Atlasov. Now the whole of northern Asia is in Russian hands. (The history of the subjugation of Kamchatka is one of the most unpleasant and protracted, partly because of the increasingly rampant lawlessness of the Cossacks, partly because of the rebelliousness of the Koryak and particularly the Yakut population. The voivode of Yakutsk undertakes every conceivable attempt to restore peace and order. Atlasov himself, who was received in Moscow with high honors, but on the return journey cannot resist the temptation to plunder a wealthy Russian merchant, is imprisoned in Yakutsk as punishment, but later, when everything goes wrong in Kamchatka, is sent back there with extensive powers as the only person who can still restore order. Because of his cruelty, he falls victim to the fury of the Cossacks, a fate that a good number of his successors must share.)
