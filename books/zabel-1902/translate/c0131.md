The steamer does not leave at four o'clock, and I declare to Mr. Neumann that under no circumstances will he receive my luggage freight now. He is very sad about this and assures me that he will make up the lost time by sailing right through the night tonight. In the end, it is all the same to me as long as he reaches Sretensk after eleven days, as promised. If he keeps his promise, he will receive his luggage freight, otherwise not another kopeck. At six o'clock in the evening the steamer is actually cast off, the engine begins to work, but despite this we still do not set out, for Mr. Neumann has loaded too many [[p272]] [Illustration: On the banks of the Shilka (p. 279).]

August 6.

By eight o'clock in the morning we are already 150 versts from Blagoveshchensk. The entire distance to Sretensk is 1,160.5 versts. During the night, the occupants of the salon were awakened by an absolutely dreadful banging. The boiler room is located on the same [[p274]] deck as our salon, and the wood being replenished is tossed into a hatch situated right next to the back wall of our bedchamber. Once the boiler room is full, another layer of wood is stacked directly against the outer wall of our cabin. But the sailors go about this with incredible recklessness, hurling the heavy logs directly against the wall. Nikolai Mikhailovich has already lodged a very forceful complaint. This afternoon, while Nikolai is taking his midday nap, wood is being loaded again, and with the same persistent malice the sailors once more fling the wood against the wall against which the officer is sleeping.

[Illustration: Chita Station (p. 284).]

Now he is already threatening the sailors that he will drum on their thick skulls with the logs if they do not proceed more cautiously.

It has been raining through the day, for the first time in a long while. Now the water will surely rise soon as well, although that is pretty much all the same to us now. Today we have already passed over shallows where the sounding lead indicated little more than 3 1/2 feet of water depth; after all, the Amur cannot get much shallower than that.

During breaks I go upstairs on deck, squeeze through the deck passengers, and visit Mitya, who feels very comfortable in my chair and, out of gratitude, offers me sweets until his little wife insists that these must first be earned. Nikolai Mikhailovich and Elena Grigoryevna also play "Vint," on account of the lack of space in such a manner that she sits in the cabin, he on a camp stool outside the cabin window, and they pass the cards to each other through the cabin window. The funnel of our steamer has no spark arrester; glowing fragments of wood therefore spray out of it in a high column, creating a magnificent display of fireworks in the dark. To be sure, the deck passengers suffer from the sparks: they burn holes in their clothes, and repeatedly one sees a hand hurriedly reach for some part of the body where the sensation suddenly arises: "It's burning!" Along the way we repeatedly encounter [[p278]] large timber rafts carrying emigrants. These people have reached Sretensk by rail with their farming implements and livestock from a more westerly part of Siberia, built themselves the raft, and are now travelling downstream in the cheapest way in the world. In the evening they moor and mostly sleep somewhere in the sand of the shore. This evening we are sitting in our cabin by a stearin candle.
