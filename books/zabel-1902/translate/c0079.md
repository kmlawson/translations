As has already been mentioned, the Russian fishing company also intends to establish large-scale fishing operations along the coast of Sakhalin. The fish primarily under consideration for this are herring and sprats. When an unbiased observer first hears talk of the abundance of fish in the waters around Sakhalin, one does not at first believe the seriousness of what is heard, until one has seen photographs and other reliable evidence confirming the truth of the statements. Thus it is indeed a fact that at the time of the herring run on the southeast coast of Sakhalin, when a shoal of herring approaches the shore, millions of these fish are often cast ashore by the waves, remain lying there, and [[p166]] pile up into walls that are often as high as a man, which have to be shoveled back into the sea by the Russian criminals deported to Sakhalin so that they do not fill the air too greatly with putrefaction.

On the Kurils and on northern Ezo, lying opposite Sakhalin, the Japanese likewise engage in herring fishing on a large scale. But this fish is by no means used as food; rather, the catch, numbering in the many millions, is thrown into large boiling cauldrons in which the fish oil is extracted and poured into vats to serve as cooking oil in Japan, China, and Korea. The boiled-out flesh, on the other hand, is exposed to the sun on the beach, dried, loaded onto ships, and finally used as fertilizer for Japanese rice fields. Consider that: herring as fertilizer! One can buy a pood of herring on Sakhalin for one ruble; a pood is roughly forty German pounds. The Russian company expects great success from the herring business and hopes to be able to hold its own with honor on the European market.

Sprats are also said to occur in immense quantities, especially on the northern coast of Sakhalin. In the next few days, the company's director of fisheries will travel to Sakhalin with an [[p167]] expedition of fishermen from Odessa to carry out the preliminary work for taking up fishing on a large scale.

In Vladivostok, I had the opportunity to taste the Siberian herring. As far as I may trust my taste in herring, it is a fine, fat herring, well able to stand comparison with the product we are accustomed to at home.

The salmon fishery on the Amur, which the company likewise intends to take up, also seems very promising to me. The Amur in itself is one of the most fish-abundant rivers in the world, and the Sibiryak who lives along the Amur draws the overwhelming majority of his food from it. Sturgeon is found there in great quantities, yielding a very good caviar that the Russians claim is scarcely inferior in quality to Volga caviar *). At certain times of the year, however, the importance of the sturgeon is pushed entirely into the background by the armies of salmon that ascend the Amur and its tributaries during the spawning season. Three varieties occur there, migrating at different times. First comes a large variety that reaches up to three meters, then comes a small variety measuring only about an ell, and last of all a medium variety. All three are caught and eaten; however, from the first run only a small part of the back is actually used, while the rest is dried and used as dog food or fertilizer. The second variety is said to be finer than the first. It forms, in fact, the daily staple of the Amur Sibiryak. Finally, the third variety, the medium-sized one, is said to be the finest and not inferior in tenderness to our best domestic varieties. I have eaten it in every form of preparation: boiled, fried, smoked, and fresh with oil and salt. In all forms it is equally noble and flavorful. It is this variety, above all, that is being targeted.

To give a vivid picture of the Amur's abundance of salmon during the migration season, I would note that the Amur Sibiryaks—and by this I include the entire river basin of the Amur—do not catch the salmon with much art or elaborate preparations, but simply build brushwood weirs or fences extending a few meters into the river from the shore. The salmon are halted on their migration by these barriers, and the Sibiryak arms himself with a hand net with which he scoops the fish into his boat. They are then killed, gutted, and hung in the smoke, or merely dried **).

That the Sibiryak could ever suffer hunger seems, given this abundance of salmon, simply out of the question, and yet it happens often enough. The reason is to be found in the fact that the Sibiryaks, in their all-too-typical laziness and carelessness, often do not make sufficient use of the first and second salmon runs in the hope of the third, and then in the end come up short if the latter happens not to turn out plentiful enough to cover the entire winter supply. [[p168]] In any case, after all this, one is certainly justified in assuming that rationalized fishing could produce results here, too, that are simply incomprehensible by European standards. As I hear, a factory is soon to be established in Vladivostok that intends to prepare exclusively canned fish. If this factory is properly managed, it could inflict severe damage on European as well as American competition. The East in particular is a region in which disproportionately more canned goods are consumed than, say, back home, because very many items cannot be obtained fresh. If a domestic East Asian canning industry now emerges, it will undoubtedly achieve great success, at least in the fish trade; and what is more, it will also be in a position to open up the European and American markets for itself.
