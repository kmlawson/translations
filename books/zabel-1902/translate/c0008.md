The railway line now runs closer to Peking than was previously the case. Given the great aversion to the iron horse that the Chinese have hitherto displayed, they refused to permit the tracks to approach close to the city, even though the railway—built, to be sure, mostly with British capital—enjoys Chinese state protection and is, in name at least, a Chinese state railway. It bears the proud name "Imperial Chinese Railway." Now the line has been extended and runs directly into the city of Peking itself—not, indeed, into the Tartar City, but only near the central southern gate of the Chinese City. Yet the station, or rather the site where the station is one day to stand, lies precisely between two of China's exalted sanctuaries, between the Temple of Heaven and the Temple of Agriculture, to the side of the road that leads in a straight line toward the Imperial Palace. To bring the railway into the city, a breach was made in the city wall. That in itself is an insult of the worst kind to the good city of Peking. By this, but above all by the fact that the locomotive now takes its path right between the holiest sanctuaries of China, in which only the Emperor is permitted to offer sacrifice, the *feng shui*—the auspicious harmony of the place, to which the Chinese attach such great importance—must be thoroughly ruined. If the Chinese were able to do as they liked, they would undoubtedly treat this railway line after the restoration of peace in exactly the same way they once treated the first railway, Shanghai–Wusong, which they bought up, tore up, and shipped to Formosa to let rust away there.

I cannot help thinking of this as I ride through the breach in the wall in a well-heated carriage, with the temperature outside at about 10 degrees Réaumur below zero, and as we now head out into the desolate country between Peking and the sea. This road has become historic; on it the greatest and most important operations of several campaigns have taken place, and of all the roads along which I have travelled in China [[p24]], the land to the right and left displays most of all the picture of destruction, of devastation. Yet the forces of reconstruction have already come into their own again. Thanks to the industrious and order-loving spirit of the Germans and Japanese, who resemble each other in this respect, neat station buildings have already been erected everywhere upon the ruins of the old ones; in the vicinity of the stations one finds clean, tidy little cottages providing quarters for the soldiers; and gradually a brisk bustle is reasserting itself among the Chinese who have returned, are rebuilding their houses, and are beginning once more to till their fields. Striking too is the petty trade that has revived along the railway line. Whole crowds of Chinese come flocking up, offering pastries, or eggs, or fruit, or the perfumed American cigarettes that enjoy great popularity among the soldiers in the absence of any others. The Chinese is very quick to spot an opportunity to do business, and above all he likes to trade with Europeans, because he gets better prices from them. The *coolie*, who has little to lose, has probably rarely been better off and has never worked for such high wages or percentages as during the period of the occupation of Zhili Province.

We pass all the places where skirmishes took place; we arrive at Langfang, well known from the Seymour Expedition. The landscape is the same to the right and left, fields and yet more fields, the entire countryside steeped in monochrome gray, with only a few withered branches stretching toward the sky here and there, usually marking the burial grounds near villages. Finally, toward midday, we reach a large bridge: the reconstructed railway bridge at Yangcun. The Peiho is frozen, several junks are trapped fast in the ice, and above it, assembled makeshift from salvaged iron parts and wooden beams, spans the Peiho Bridge. We have arrived at Yangcun Station. A pleasant, fairly large station building. It is decked with flags and wreathed in garlands; a large "W. II." is painted on a paper sign. It is still the decoration from yesterday's celebration of the Kaiser's birthday. At all the stations along the line, flags have been flown and greenery put up, as far as any was to be had. The foreign staging posts have decorated themselves as well.

Opposite the station building, a German flag flutters over a row of barracks, beside it the flag of the Red Cross. A field hospital! At the station, bustle and activity reign, as everywhere. Mingling with the soldiers of the railway regiment who operate the train are the infantrymen stationed here on staging duty. The staging commander is personally at the tracks, as he likely is every day and for every train. The railway is to him what the newspaper or the pub is to us back home. At the station he hears the news from Peking or Tientsin; there he greets old friends and comrades and makes new acquaintances. He recounts what news he himself has gathered, and travelers passing through unpack what they know. Fact and fiction—all jumbled together. And there is more gossiping on such occasions than at any ladies' coffee circle!
