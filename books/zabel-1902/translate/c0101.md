Of course, I will not travel the entire distance to Khabarovsk on the passenger train; instead, I will get off at Nikolsk, where the Manchurian line branches off, and continue tomorrow afternoon on the express train that leaves Vladivostok in the morning.

The large clock on the Vladivostok station building, which looks exactly like the clock at Bahnhof Friedrichstraße in Berlin, shows 3:25. The conductor gives a long, drawn-out blast on his shrill whistle, and the engine answers just as drawn-out. The conductor whistles a second time, now short and sharp, and the engine replies once more, just as short and sharp; then the train slowly sets itself in motion. This time I am sitting inside, leaning back in a corner of a first-class compartment upholstered in red plush. In the Far East, the white man always travels first class if he respects himself and expects the same from others. Everything feels pleasantly familiar and cozy. The soft upholstery, which is not at all as lumpy as the mattress in the “number” of my gostinitsa; the brightly polished window panes; the tidy little children of an officer who sit opposite me with their mama, dressed in a chic Parisian gown of the latest cut, on their way home after shopping in the city—purchases that are now unpacked and inspected once more; and then the agreeable rocking of the railway car! With an indescribable feeling of bliss, I close my eyes to capture the fleeting moment. In a single second, I run through all the various means of travel I have used during my stay in the East: first the railway—ah, the railway!—as far as Genoa, then the fine, large passenger steamer of the Hamburg-America Line, and in between Somali dugouts, Ceylonese dugout canoes, Malay boats, Chinese sampans, steam pinnaces, and steam launches, once even a torpedo boat; from Shanghai onward a large troop transport steamer, then up to Tanggu a former Yangtze steamer, then once more the railway; during the campaign a pony or a mule or a Chinese mandarin cart, or even more frequently shank’s pony; then the railway again, but in what a state! Next, on the journey through Manchuria, came the draisine pulled by Chinese coolies, then the Chinese farm cart, then another stretch of railway, this time third class to be sure; later in Shandong the donkey and the mule litter; from Chefoo to Amoy a German freighter, from there to Hong Kong a Japanese mail steamer; then in South China the sedan chair and the Chinese junk; in Shanghai predominantly the pony carriage and, as in Japan, the rickshaw; in Vladivostok the troika, usually with only two horses, however; and now the railway once again, a soft corner in a first-class compartment—ah, one only learns to appreciate the achievements of civilization after having been deprived of them for so long!

Indeed, I am in very good spirits despite it all, and it does not vex me in the least that this dreadful slow train, having crossed the Svetlanskaya, now creeps along the shore of Amur Bay [[p204]] at the speed of a cyclist taking his first ride on a country road. For a good while the route follows the sea; the scenery could almost be called picturesque, and now and then it truly is, when through the shrubbery covering the hillsides the summer residence of a wealthy Vladivostok merchant peeks out here and there, or when on a curve—of which there are very many—one’s gaze rests on one of the barracks of the Ussuri Railway Regiment, and with the help of a little imagination, construes from it a resemblance to the charming Miramare near Trieste.
