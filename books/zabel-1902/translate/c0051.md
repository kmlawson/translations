Once my business affairs had thus been placed on a legal footing, Canton could hold me no longer, and I decided to spend some time looking around the interior of the country again. I therefore engaged Ho Amei’s former Chinese expert, a well-travelled man, as a guide, hired a Chinese junk, which I equipped as best I could with European necessities and above all provisioned well, and then set out on an extended expedition into the interior. Since long-distance travel in South China can be undertaken only by water and piracy is still quite in vogue, the consulate deemed it appropriate to notify the Viceroy of my travel plans. And indeed, on my travels I did not have to endure any harassment whatsoever. Wherever my junk appeared, flying from its mast the German flag given to me by the commander of the German gunboat *Jaguar*, which was anchored in the river off Canton, I was received with respect. Preparations had been made everywhere for my coming. The Viceroy had informed all authorities and instructed them to assist me in every way and to protect me. As soon as the flag came into view, emissaries duly appeared from station to station from the commanders of the numerous Chinese war junks that perform patrol duty on the rivers, as well as from the mandarins of the numerous towns I passed, to present me with their superiors' visiting cards and offer me a military escort, which, however, I declined in most cases. Very frequently the mandarins also appeared in person and repeatedly invited me to visit them in their name or lodge with them. I invariably declined this, however, and confined myself to entertaining the distinguished gentlemen with coffee or cocoa, which they sipped with relish, or at least to sending my visiting card to their residence via a boy in return for their visit. Since the mining districts of particular interest to me lie in the basin of the North River (the Beijiang), it was only natural that I made my way to these parts, and I chose them all the more gladly as the objective of this journey of mine because it was an area that, for the researcher—and particularly for one whose attention is directed more towards economic and transport-policy questions than geographical ones—was for the most part still virgin territory. I travelled up the North River as far as the mouth of its largest western tributary, the Lianjiang, then turned into it and followed its course as far as the district town of Lianshan, the transit port for overland traffic between the basin of the North River—that is to say, Guangdong Province—and the extraordinarily productive province of Hunan lying behind it. The journey proceeded rather slowly. Since the prevailing wind was generally against us and Chinese river junks cannot tack for lack of a keel, which is unusable given the often very low water level of South Chinese rivers, the crew had to haul the boat from shore along almost the entire route using a long rope fastened to the [[p113]] very top of the mast. I cannot describe to you here in detail the particulars of this interesting journey, and wish only to tell you very briefly that I reached the border of the Miao territories. These begin at the corner formed by the three provinces of Guangdong, Guangxi, and Hunan. By Miao are meant well-known remnants of the original Chinese population, who have preserved themselves in considerable purity in their customs, dress, hairstyle, etc., and in particular have not yet been subjugated politically by the Manchu dynasty. The people, for example, do not wear queues, but rather hair tied up high, which is indeed the original Chinese style, since the queue was only imposed upon the Chinese by the Manchus. There was no purpose in my penetrating further into the Miao territories, as my Chinese companion's linguistic knowledge failed him and I was also not adequately equipped for such an expedition. However, I have resolved to investigate these regions especially at a later date. That the yield will be worthwhile, I have no doubt. The scientific yield of even this journey was not insignificant; in particular regarding the economic and political aspect, I succeeded in making some interesting observations. From a purely geographical standpoint, too, the trip was very interesting. There one finds extensive hill country—one might almost say mountainous country—with extraordinarily characteristic landforms, lush meadows, forest, water, and romantic valleys of erosion reminiscent of parts of Saxon Switzerland. Geologically, too, there is much to be gained here, just as in South China generally numerous geographical problems still await their solution. As you know, Richthofen's *China* still requires completion with regard to South China*). Among other things, on the border of Guangdong and Guangxi I discovered a cave approximately 15 kilometres long, through which an underground river runs that is navigable for nearly half its course. I studied the Yuanshan district somewhat more thoroughly. It is very rich in ores of all kinds; in general, on the voyage one frequently comes across places where coal is found, some of it of surprisingly good quality. The region, as I have said, was still untouched by Europeans. In time, however, one will inevitably have to turn to studying the hinterland of Canton more closely if one does not wish to rely for one's knowledge exclusively on the statements of Chinese middlemen, who naturally have an interest in keeping trade with Europeans in their own hands.
