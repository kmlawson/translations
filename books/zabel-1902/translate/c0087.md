The Cossack plays an important role in the history of Russian expansion. It was Cossacks who conquered the Khanate of Sibir; we find them as the first Russian pioneers on the borders of India, the first on the Sea of Okhotsk, the first in the Amur region, the first in Manchuria. Their sotnias fought against the Chinese at Tianjin and marched victorious into Beijing alongside the others.

In foreign eyes, Cossackdom is often regarded as an expression of the despotic form of government, as the bodyguard of tsarism, belonging to it as Jesuitism belongs to the papacy. What is true in this notion is that Russia's Asian successes followed on the heels of the Cossacks; yet the Cossack was always the representative of untrammeled independence, self-reliance, and personal freedom. In essence, he has remained so down to the present day, even though an invigorated state authority has meanwhile imposed a stricter organization upon him—one that, however, still makes ample concessions to his original character today. The homeland of the Cossack is Little Russia, the cradle of the Russian people. In the course of the struggles of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, this had fallen to Lithuania, and the systematic suppression of the Orthodox Church as well as of Russianness in general induced numerous adherents of the Muscovite dynasty to migrate to the lower reaches of the Dnieper. Here, the islands formed by the Dnieper rapids*) provided a particularly welcome refuge, and the river-pirate trade became the most obvious source of livelihood. The influx of recruits that the Dnieper Cossacks received over time must have been very substantial. For soon we find swarms of Cossacks in constant struggle with the neighboring Golden Horde, and this perpetual warfare led the Cossacks to adopt the martial qualities of their neighbors as well. Thus, from original [[p183]] river pirates, they became an equestrian people, the feared lords of the steppe, and in their continuous advance they soon reached the mouth of the Don. Their nautical recollections from the Dnieper were not yet forgotten, and in small boats—mostly mere dugouts with side planks—they rowed out into the Black Sea and plundered Turkish vessels just as they did the Russian and Tatar ones coming down the Don. Yet despite this, they did not disown their descent from the Muscovite race, in whom the inclination toward agriculture predominates! They settled in small communities on the territories wrested from the Turks and Tatars, each choosing its ataman from among its own. They tilled their land, and on the side plundered ships and carried out raids into neighboring Tatar territories: even today it still happens that some Cossack village far off on the Amur gathers its male population one fine day and undertakes a raid into neighboring Chinese territory, which is robbed and plundered.

Thus, in essence, the Cossack has remained true to himself through the centuries. And the struggle that developed quite naturally from proximity to Turks and Tatars arose later from proximity to Ostyaks, Kyrgyz, Kalmyks, Samoyeds, Yakuts, Golds, Daurs, Buryats, Kamchadals, Chinese, etc. Far from being willing tools of despotism, they were, on the contrary, the bearers and pioneers of national independence. And the reason why these independent acquisitions of the Cossacks never grew into independent Cossack states lies partly in the lack of organization among the Cossack communities themselves, and partly in the influence of the Orthodox Church, which was intimately linked with the tsardom. The Cossacks represented the impetuosity of a young race that, fully conscious of its strength, seeks an ever wider field for the application and exercise of that strength, and gladly takes advantage of its distance from government authority to give free rein to its wild energies. The government, however, was always clever enough to harvest the acquisitions of these Cossack swarms as a gain for the state, and to take the administration of the newly won tracts of land into its own hands. This was facilitated by the direct geographical contiguity of the newly acquired territories with the motherland. Thus it came about that separatist cravings, such as we encounter, for instance, in numerous English colonies, could not emerge at all, despite the wildness and lawlessness of the conquerors, who were always pioneers only on the frontiers, never attained a consolidated settled existence, and surrendered the conquered and traversed regions to a peasant population that followed hard on their heels, accustomed to sedentary life and authority. Thus the government could unconcernedly let these unruly bands have their way for the time being—though, to be sure, only until the moment arrived when the borders of the empire had been pushed forward to bulwarks that were initially insurmountable: the sea or resilient state structures. Then came the need to train these uncivilized, themselves semi-nomadic Cossack hordes in obedience and settled life, and to provide them with an organization that made them useful members of an orderly commonwealth. This was ultimately achieved, albeit with difficulties and often only after fierce struggles.
