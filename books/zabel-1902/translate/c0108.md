For all these reasons, it is necessary for Russia to pay redoubled attention to the Amur region. Precisely because it offers far more favorable conditions in many respects than the rest of Siberia, it is essential that the Russian peasant transplanted here be taught how to turn them to advantage.

[[p216]]

The southern Primorskaya is rich in land suitable for settlement. To be sure, the climate presents various difficulties, though fewer than the more central parts of Siberia. There are probably few regions on earth that exhibit such considerable temperature differences as the Primorskaya, reaching as much as 100 degrees Celsius: in winter cold down to –40 degrees, in summer heat up to +60 degrees, with an annual mean of about +1 degree. The plant and animal life is constituted accordingly. A cross between the tropics and the Arctic zone, without the gentle transitions of the temperate zone and without the temperature moderation brought by warm ocean currents and winds, as in Europe, where southern France lies at approximately the same latitude we are now passing through. After the harsh winter has held the earth in chains for seven months, the hot and yet far from dry summer strives to make everything good twofold and threefold in five months. At the same time, the climate changes with such regularity that one can determine almost to the day the maximums and minimums, as well as the cessation and return of frost, and the freezing over and reopening of the rivers and harbors. The flora, just like the fauna, produces

[Illustration: Khabarovsk (Museum): Marine Animals Department (p. 211).] [[p217]] products of both the tropics and the far north. Cedars, cork trees, palms, foliage plants, and tigers, antelopes, ibises on the one hand, and oaks, birches, dwarf pines, and sables, polar bears, elk on the other hand, are the extremes.

The indigenous population constitutes only about ten percent of the total population of the Maritime Province, where fewer than 230,000 people live in all, despite its expanse from Posyet Bay to the Bering Sea. In that part of the Primorskaya belonging to the Ussuri basin, there live about 180,000 people. This part of Siberia is relatively among the most densely populated. But to be sure, a large portion of the Russian inhabitants, who make up 72 percent of the population (the remaining 28 percent being divided among Koreans, Chinese, Japanese, and non-Russian Caucasians), lives in the towns and does not enter into consideration as an agricultural settling factor. The settlements naturally followed first the course of the Ussuri and several of its small right tributaries. Among them, one of the most significant is the station of Iman, with the settlement named "Grafskaya" in honor of Muravyov. Here, before the opening of the Ussuri Railway, was the transshipment port for goods using the waterway to Khabarovsk and the Amur ports and the overland route to Vladivostok. When the railway was then built, Grafskaya was the point where the South Ussuri Railway, begun from Vladivostok, met the North Ussuri Railway, tackled from Khabarovsk. The port still possesses a certain importance today and is connected to the railway station of the same name by a branch line one and a half versts long. But to be sure, as a result of the railway construction, the settlements have moved toward the railway line running further east. As long as the settlements are still as small and as mobile as they are now, such migration is easy. Indeed, they are neat little houses that we pass, with well-tended gardens in front. In many cases, their newness is still apparent. The first thing in a new settlement is usually the church, which generally also stands out for its elegance against the low log cabins of the peasants.

Over the years, Russia has done a great deal to populate the Ussuri region. It has drawn immigrants, particularly from southern Russia, more recently also from the Baltic provinces; likewise, it has made favorable offers to the Russians and Galicians living in America, and a portion of these has resettled to East Asia. In spite of this, however, one can discern no real method in Russian colonial policy. In many ways, it looks like the play of a small child who drops his doll when a new one comes within reach, and then tears the hat off the good old doll to put it on the new one. As explained above, this part of Siberia opens toward the sea, across which it can most conveniently and cheaply be provided with supplies. The completion of the railway changes little in this regard, since sea transport, no matter how low the special tariffs may be, will nevertheless always remain more practical and cheaper for many goods, and besides, the existing commercial organization is not designed for a rational retail distribution of goods that might arrive by rail. The commercial centers are all situated on the sea. If the gateway—in this case, access from the sea—is therefore closed by import duties that bring no economic benefit to the colonial territory, which does not yet produce anything for export, [[p218]] and are intended exclusively to benefit the southern Manchurian ports, Dalny above all, the entire hinterland of Vladivostok and Nikolayevsk is suddenly forced to pay a surcharge on its imported goods that is in no way justified by any possibility of making up for it on the other side through increased revenues.
