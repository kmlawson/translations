But despite my good intentions, sleep proves impossible until midnight. For around 10 o'clock in the evening, after it has grown dark, we tie up at the postal station of Lugovoy, which consists of nine houses. Here wood is taken on board. The massive logs are laid across two wooden carrying poles and borne aboard by pairs of strong men. They then lower one of the carrying poles so that the entire load, which often weighs over a hundredweight and a half, drops straight down into the iron hold. The crashing makes such an incredible din that half-asleep one imagines one hears the trumpets of the Last Judgment sounding. At last, around midnight, the sailors finish loading the wood. I am just falling asleep. But I wake up once more, [[p239]] because a raindrop falls on my nose. I therefore pull the hood completely over my face and also turn up the rubber collar so that the water running off the hood has to stream down it. The rain runs off me, and I can sleep.

July 22.
We have been under way again since daybreak. The sun wakes me. I feel the need to wash. The washroom is located below, next to the cabin. For all the cabin passengers there is only a single washroom. It is of course occupied, and three other fellow travellers are already waiting outside the door. Turns are taken in order. Very kind of these ladies and gentlemen that each requires only about two minutes for their ablutions! Because I value thoroughness more than speed, I take longer. But I make haste and manage in five minutes. Outside, five men are already waiting again; evidently unable to imagine what I could be doing in the washroom for so long, and therefore anxious that something might have happened to me, they are pounding on the locked door with their fists. As I step out, I protest this behavior very energetically. I can afford to do so all the more boldly because they do not understand me anyway. The priest and the school inspector, who are next in line, go straight in together and are both done after just two minutes. —

The river widens noticeably. At times it is more than ten kilometers wide and shallow. Navigation is therefore difficult. But since no accurate river chart of the Amur exists, because the river continually changes its channel owing to silt deposits, soundings must be taken constantly. From morning till night, therefore, a sailor stands forward at the bow, casting into the water a long pole attached to a line looped around his hand, and calling out the water depth in feet in a loud, monotonous voice. Our steamer draws 4½ feet of water. Often the depth approaches this figure alarmingly; then the vessel slows to half-speed until the shallow spot is successfully cleared. The sailor’s monotonous calling becomes a steady music to which one grows so accustomed that one only takes notice when he stops calling because he can no longer find bottom. The sun is well disposed toward us. My system proves its worth. There is no longer any room for the priest and the school inspector in the shade of the wheelhouse. They have therefore stretched a plaid blanket across the railing of the paddle box, sleep in the shade of the blanket, and now spit directly into the Amur. On deck there is also a little black bear belonging to the captain. He is very comical and his name is Mitya. The young Cossack officer is also named Mitya, and since his wife is very fond of him, she transfers this affection to the little bear Mitya as well. She feeds him sugar and scratches his fur so that he rolls onto his back and sprawls out with all four paws in the air. Because it is very hot, Mitya the man fetches a bucket of water, and Mitya the bear splashes around in it. Thus Mitya the bear and Mitya the man become friends too. Only now is the young woman completely happy.

The happiness will not last long, however; for this afternoon we are already supposed to exchange our steamer for a second one, which is to meet us [[p240]] from Blagoveshchensk and then turn back, because our steamer cannot cross the bar. The expected steamer is also said to have better accommodations for passengers than ours. The captain has the written order for the captain of the oncoming steamer in his pocket.

At noon today there was fish again, this time boiled. Nikolai Mikhailovich treated us to a tin of sprats from his trunk. In this way we had a zakuska.

The drinks on board have run out, the bottled beer as well as the lemonade, the seltzer water, and the kvass. If we are thirsty now, nothing is left for us but tea or Amur water. A ridiculous state of affairs!

Around 2 o'clock in the afternoon we put in at the Cossack village of Mikhailovo-Semyonovsk to discharge passengers and mail and to take on wood. On the bank, the village women are waiting for the steamer with all kinds of wares they wish to sell: butter, milk, bread, kvass, cucumbers, blueberries, fresh caviar, white cheese, potatoes, etc. They find a roaring [[p241]] sale everywhere, and our ladies, assisted by their husbands, also take part in the shopping. Mitya the man leaps ashore even before the gangplank has properly touched the bank and dashes toward a bowl of blueberries he had already secured from above. Nor is Mitya the bear forgotten. He receives a large piece of fresh bread, which he evidently finds delicious. Half an hour later he is whimpering because he has got a bellyache. People proceed very cautiously with their purchases. Before buying, everything is tasted, especially milk and butter—the latter with a knife, whereas the milk bottle is simply put to the mouth, an operation performed on one and the same bottle from various quarters until someone decides to spend 20 kopecks for the remainder. While the clatter of wood-loading proceeds, one strolls through the long, sprawling village of some 700 souls, over which a handsome church towers.
