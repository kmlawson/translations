But the rumor that I was a person of high rank traveling after the “German minister” and absolutely having to catch up with him had evidently spread with the speed of the wind. Everyone treats me with exceptional respect. Even the colonel who wanted to evict us has himself introduced to me. He is too much of a military man to dare inquire into my state secrets in any detail. All that matters now is that I accept the respect shown to me from all sides with the requisite dignity.

[[p301]]

Another carriage is attached, in which the colonel and the stationmaster now take their seats. In terms of punctuality we are already such hardened sinners that we no longer think anything of it when the train departs at 10:00 a.m. instead of 8:00 a.m. In any case, we are departing, and that is the main thing. At first the line runs along the Shilka. Over there lies our good old “Harbin,” that confounded tub; the “komandir” waves from the bridge, and Mr. Neumann also waves farewell to us with the recaptured complaints book. Our journey proceeds slowly. Not until the afternoon do we arrive in Nerchinsk, the scene of that shameful peace by which Russia in its day was forced to return the Amur region to China. The railway bypasses the town at a distance of three kilometers, although there was no reason at all why it should not have been laid closer. But the engineers who built the railway demanded that the town pay them a “subsidy” of four million rubles, in which case the line would be built right up to Nerchinsk. The merchants of Nerchinsk refused to pay the four million rubles, and so the railway was simply not brought close to the town. Other towns were wiser, and the railway engineers are rich men today. Heaven is equally high everywhere, and even calculated from Nerchinsk, the Tsar is still far away. Nerchinsk station boasts a well-stocked buffet where we get excellent Russian food. A true relief after the miserable ship’s fare of recent weeks! Besides, it has its good side that the train runs so slowly: all the better shall we sleep. And so, Nikolai Mikhailovich and I lie down on our sleeping cushions, dream away the torment of the last few days—which was great—from four o’clock in the afternoon until two o’clock the following afternoon, and in this way save the cost of supper and morning tea.

August 19–20.

The region of Transbaikalia through which our railway line passes is well cultivated. The contrast with the Amur regions is readily apparent. Large peasant villages alternate with isolated homesteads. Herds of cattle numbering several hundred head graze on the lawns that are a lush green after the rain. Forest, farmland, and meadow succeed one another. During the night we passed the station of Kaidalovo, where the Trans-Manchurian Railway, still under construction, branches off, running from here to the Manchurian border and then via Hailar and Qiqihar to Harbin; there it forks into a southern line to Mukden, Newchwang, and Port Arthur and Dalny respectively, and an eastern one to Ninguta, Pogranichnaya, Nikolsk, and Vladivostok. In the afternoon of our second day of travel we pass Chita, the capital of Transbaikalia, and cross the Yablonovy Mountains near the station of Yablonovaya in a tunnel roughly 300 meters long, which my fellow travelers look upon as a wonder of the world, and of which they have been speaking for days as the great forthcoming event. On the Vladivostok–Petersburg line, this is the only tunnel. After leaving the valley of the Shilka, we ascended the valley of the Ingoda, which together with the Onon forms the Shilka, up to the watershed, the Yablonovy Mountains,

[[p302]]

and now find ourselves already in the river basin of the Yenisei. In the valley of the Khilok, the railway leads down the gentle heights of the mountains and, near the charmingly situated Verkhneudinsk, reaches the valley of the Selenga, which gathers its waters in Mongolia to pour them into Lake Baikal. The landscape near Verkhneudinsk is enchantingly beautiful; one has a wide view to the south, into the Selenga Valley, the very valley into which the caravan route from Peking via Kalgan through Mongolia and via Kyakhta runs. In winter especially, endless camel caravans move along this road, bringing Chinese tea overland from Hankou on the Yangtze in Central China to the principal tea mart, Kyakhta. Here the Russian wholesalers are established; the tea is repackaged and goes from here to Europe as so-called caravan tea. The so-called Russian tea that we drink in Europe is thus, after all, nothing other than Chinese tea.
