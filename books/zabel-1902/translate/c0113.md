I have just come from the General’s, where I went for tea. It was delightful! Long live Russian hospitality! On my way there, I felt rather uneasy. For I had discovered that I had no formal attire in my trunks that could have met Russian expectations. In Russia, the social master key is the frock coat. Now, while I had indeed taken my frock coat with me to East Asia “just in case,” I had never used it anywhere. The only item of clothing that one absolutely needs [[p227]] over there—that is, in southern East Asia—is tails. One appears at dinner every day in tails: an English custom, sheer nonsense, but one does it all the same so as not to run the risk of being looked at askance. When I then traveled north, I sent my frock coat home from Hong Kong. Now I need it. Yet I have already adopted many a thing from the Russians that strikes me as good and convenient, among others the word “nichevo!” A magnificent word! Fits every situation in life! Used in exactly the same way as “maskee” in the Chinese ports. Strictly speaking, it simply means “nothing.” And yet with it one says everything. Have you eaten, slept, or drank poorly, then say “nichevo!”; have you failed at something, has your rival driven you from the field, have you received a reprimand, lost at gambling, has your wife run off with another man or the cashier with the cash box, have the directors of the bank on whose board of supervisors you sit gone fraudulently bankrupt, have you once again failed to win the grand prize in the lottery, or has anything else unpleasant happened to you, then do as the Russian does and simply say “nichevo!” All sorrow is gone, and you breathe a sigh of relief as if a great weight had been lifted from your heart. “Nichevo” is the lid you put on the pot into which you have previously tossed everything that troubled you; it is the very sponge that appears in the familiar imperative, “Pass a sponge over it!” In Russian, all of that is “nichevo.” The Russian is entirely tuned to “nichevo.” That is why one finds in him so much divinely blessed lightheartedness. And so—my dear lady, my black coat is already floating through the Suez Canal—nichevo!—isn't that so—nichevo?—yes, nichevo!—ha—ha—ha—ha—everyone is immensely delighted that I already speak Russian so well! How the journey agreed with me, how one slept. There is the little daughter: come, say “bonjour, monsieur!” Indeed just as pretty as her mother! No, not quite yet, perhaps it will come in time! The French governess smiles piquantly. Over there, the black-lacquered panel with the massive golden Chinese characters comes from a large temple in Ninguta. On the railway there was also a very young deportee working who looked so pale, and Madame bought his freedom out of pity. Can such a thing really be done? On that point, the best information can be given to us by the gentleman who is just entering and kissing the lady's hand. He is a senior official of the Governorate of Sakhalin, where the deportees have recently been sent. Of course it cannot be done. Yet the man was soon released and then compulsorily settled. In this, the 1,000 rubles will stand him in good stead. Indeed terribly kind of the general’s wife to give a poor, sick exile 1,000 rubles! New guests arrive: several young staff officers, then one of the generals who had traveled with us just yesterday. Today a Commander’s cross hangs from his collar. And over there is the nice old gentleman with the white beard and the long cigarette holder. Well, then almost all of us are together again. One young officer is still missing, still on duty—bah, he'll turn up soon enough! Nichevo! Ladies and gentlemen, may I ask you to step out onto the veranda!—

So we move out to the veranda. The way leads through the dining room. It is very simply furnished. A large table, a number of chairs, a Japanese folding screen, a wide fireplace, in one corner an image of the Madonna with a burning red votive lamp above it. The [[p228]] drawing room, too, is very simple. A small sofa, two armchairs, an enormous camel-hair carpet—of Tianjin manufacture—, a small table, a wide fireplace, in the corner an image of the Madonna with a burning red votive lamp above it. The veranda is spacious. In the middle stands a richly appointed tea table. In the background, two white-gloved orderlies wait for nods from the hostess. “Prenez place!” Wherever you please, please! First zakuska and a little glass of vodka! But please, another right away! Caviar? Or would you prefer some fresh salmon, with

salt, oil, and onions! Ah... here is a very special delicacy: Mongolian cheese, tastes like genuine Gervais double crème from Normandy. Indeed excellent, exquisite! Sheep’s milk cheese, of course; “madame” brought it back from Manchuria. The orderlies present iced coffee mixed with Fürst Pückler ice cream and whipped cream in champagne glasses on which even the condensation is freezing. The French governess serves wine. “Rouge ou blanc?” The white is sweet, the general explains, tastes like Marsala, grows in the Crimea; the red does too, slightly tart, replaces Bordeaux perfectly! I must try the white, and then the red. I decide on the latter. From the other side, bread and cold cuts are served.
