The *Nikolai* is, of course, built in all its parts exclusively for its special purpose. Forward it carries the thick, gray-painted gun, which can easily be swung and turned by the gunner using a handle, as deemed necessary. The gun is already loaded; at the muzzle the tip of the harpoon protrudes, and the thick rope to which the harpoon is attached wraps around a steam winch, which serves the purpose of "giving line" to the struck fish or hauling it in. The rope disappears into the hold of the vessel, where it lies carefully coiled, long enough to give the whale more than 400 meters of slack when, struck by the harpoon, it plunges into the depths. The most interesting instrument of the entire machinery is probably the harpoon itself. It has the respectable weight of over fifty pounds and consists first of a thick iron shaft that fits snugly into the gun, but has an eye corresponding to its length in which an iron ring can slide back and forth. When loading, this harpoon shank is pushed into the gun, while the ring, which slides toward the harpoon's tip, remains outside the gun and holds the attached rope. Upon firing, the ring then slides to the harpoon's rearmost end. The tip of the harpoon consists of nothing other than a pointed cast-iron grenade, filled with powder and [[p159]] fitted with a fuse, which is screwed onto the actual body of the harpoon. On this body are the barbs, which are tied together during firing. However, the cord is stripped off upon penetration into the whale, and as soon as the whale pulls, the barbs spread apart. Through this, the fuse of the grenade is simultaneously pulled taut; the grenade bursts inside the whale and generally kills it quickly, less by the grenade fragments tearing up the whale's innards than by the tremendous pressure exerted by the powder gases inside the animal's body. To be sure, the whale is a powerful fellow; often the harpoon does not strike the right spot, and the grenade therefore does not burst near the vital organs. Thus it happens that the fish thrashes about in the water for a long time. It is then tired out by playing the lines, given as much line as it wants, but at intervals the line is slowly hauled in again, just as when angling one must tire out a large fish before hauling it out, only with the difference that here steam winches do what the hand easily performs there. Once the whale has been tired out, if it does not die of its own accord, it is lanced in the manner described earlier. A well-aimed thrust to the heart almost always kills it on the spot. Once the fish is dead, it is hauled in with ropes and secured alongside one of the sides of the ship, tail forward. In this way it is brought to the station, goes onto the flensing slip, and then into the boiler.

Apart from the equipment for whaling, the *Nikolai* is a small steamer like any other, except that, beyond the coal it needs for itself, it has no accommodations for carrying cargo. The remaining space is fitted out exclusively for the comfort of the ship's crew. And as far as these accommodations are concerned, it cannot be denied that they would do credit to a private yacht in which one could embark on a voyage around the world. The cabin furnishings are almost elegant and bear no comparison whatsoever with those of the *Alexander*. Nor can one begrudge this elegance to the men who, year in and year out, must regard this little vessel as their home. The captain is a Russian German, the actual whale-gunner a Norwegian. He is new on board, as is the captain, who was previously a mate on the *Alexander*.

It is quite questionable whether we will already be able to bring in a "big fellow" tomorrow, for firstly one does not know where the whales are currently staying, secondly one does not know whether the new captain will find them right away, and thirdly one does not know whether the new gunner will shoot the whale well if we do find one. By this time tomorrow evening we will be somewhat better informed regarding these three points.

* * *

On board the *Nikolai*, July 13.

It is 8 o'clock in the morning. The captain wakes me. We have already traveled over twenty miles and are now entering the region where one can find whales if one is lucky. I immediately ask anxiously whether any have already been spotted, but the captain smiles with stoic calm and remarks that things do not move quite so fast after all. Very well, we eat breakfast leisurely; then [[p160]] a camp stool is set up for me on the bridge, a pair of binoculars is pressed into my hand, and we scan the open sea. Here and there a flock of wild ducks takes wing; others let us come quite close and then suddenly dive under, only to resurface once we have passed. At times a tree trunk floating in the water and looking like a whale makes fools of us; once, even the whale-gunner puts on an interested expression.
