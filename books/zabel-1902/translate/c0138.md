The barge on which the deportees are housed is in any case a considerably better and more comfortable means of conveyance than the rafts on which the voluntary emigrants travel down the river. Today, despite the great shortage of industrial laborers, the deportees are employed only in the mines belonging to the Crown. Siberia as a land of deportation proper already belongs to a bygone era. Colonial conditions meant that the deportation system was having a detrimental effect on free immigration, and thus the island of Sakhalin was chosen as the place of deportation, where the deportees are employed in road construction and coal mining. Lately there has been much talk that the Tsar intends to abolish deportation entirely. Given the shortage of labor, this measure would have a rather drastic impact on Siberian mining and, on a smaller scale, might well appear just as hasty and produce just as unpleasant conditions as the emancipation of the serfs once did. It is deeply regrettable that feuilletonists determined at all costs to write sensationally have depicted the Siberian deportation system in such lurid colors that, despite their otherwise quite commendable works, little benefit has been generated for an objective assessment of the system. Books such as that by Kennan are only suited to confusing still further the already very vague notions of Siberian conditions, and Tolstoy's novels about Siberian deportation likewise often produce impressions that do violence to the actual facts. I share the view of a large [[p296]] number of objective-minded Siberian men who by no means consider the lot of the deportees so deplorable in comparison to criminals interned in prisons. On Sakhalin, for instance, the officials assigned to guard the deportees are often completely powerless. Yet because each is dependent on the other, almost patriarchal relations have developed, which an outsider can scarcely comprehend at all. Socially, too, a criminal who has served his sentence faces far fewer obstacles to his further advancement in Siberia than under narrow-minded European conditions. In Vladivostok and the towns along the Amur, there are a good many highly intelligent and industrious people who were deported to the Far East on account of past missteps, and in those parts it is generally regarded as bad manners to ask people one has just met how they happened to come to Siberia. Profound social distinctions between new immigrants and former deportees or their descendants do not inherently exist, and if it has recently been claimed that voluntary immigrants in Siberia are protesting against continued deportation, this is attributable to the agitation of political zealots who, for the sake of [[p297]] their theories, set aside economic and state interests. Deportation has acquired a very considerable colonial-economic importance for Siberia in a positive sense, and retains it to this day. Yet if one wishes to adopt an exclusively humanitarian standpoint, it is, in my view, far more humane to readmit a wrongdoer who genuinely intends to become a useful member of human society into the community of others almost without prejudice, and to afford him the opportunity to do so, than for such a person—as happens with us in Europe—to be permanently struck from the ranks of respectable people, or of those who might ever become so again, on account of such a dark stain in his past. Deportation is one of those questions that is rarely treated *sine ira et studio*, and it is therefore all the more fitting to push back against the literature that believes it has found in Siberian deportation ready-made material for sentimental novels. On the other hand, it hardly needs special emphasis that the application of deportation to so-called political criminals is an outgrowth of despotism, in the condemnation of which the educated of all civilized nations are united.

Darkness has fallen, but we continue traveling for several hours after sunset. I am packing up the last of my things in the cabin and have just stepped out onto the deck when a sound catches my ear that gives me a pleasant jolt. It is the shrill whistle of a locomotive. Shortly thereafter, our steamer rounds the last hill obstructing our view, and to the right and left on both banks we see a glittering sea of lights approaching. It is Sretensk: on the left, the town; on the right, the railway station. The brief whistling and clatter of a shunting train drifts delightfully over to us. Heaven be praised! The connection to civilization has been regained. It took us four weeks to travel from Khabarovsk to Sretensk, without having stopped for even a single minute longer than was strictly necessary. Soon the *Kharbin* also ties up at the bank, and the first thing we do upon going ashore is to head to the hotel to consult the timetable. Tomorrow morning at eight o'clock our train departs, which will take us first as far as Lake Baikal. At the hotel we find the first newspapers of recent date, among them a non-Russian periodical—and of all things, the *Journal amusant* [Amusing Journal], complete, of course, with various strokes of the censor's brush. With the censor, then, Franco-Russian friendship seems to come to a halt.
