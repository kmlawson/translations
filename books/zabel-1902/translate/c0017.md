Fortunately, I meet a non-commissioned officer who speaks broken German; I ask him about the station officer, to whom I wish to present my recommendation, and the non-commissioned officer points to the saloon car. Just then, an officer with a thick, black, shaggy fur cap on his head steps out of the car; the non-commissioned officer tells me: “Speak English to him,” and I walk up to the officer. I address him in English and hand him the card from the station commandant in Shanhaiguan. The officer takes the card from me, reads it, and says: “Why don't you speak German to me? If you address anyone here in English, you won't get an answer. Besides, we [[p40]] know each other, from Tientsin—don't you remember our dinner on the railway bridge anymore?” Had I been blind? Why, it is a good acquaintance from Tientsin, Staff Captain Losev of the Railway Regiment, who in his time restored the Tanggu–Tientsin line and later repaired the Tanggu–Shanhaiguan railway. We shook hands. “Well, where are you headed, then?” “Through Manchuria.” “Do you have a passport, then?” “No, I'll manage to get through.” “The railway ends a few kilometers further on; how do you intend to make your way then?” “Then I'll take a Chinese cart.” “Well, suits me. Quick, where is your luggage? Put it right in the car here; the train is leaving immediately. Come in here, please.” He opens the door to the saloon car, which serves as his quarters, as there are otherwise no living accommodations available. It is a long car, quite comfortably furnished and divided into two fairly large compartments, one of which is occupied by the staff captain, the other by the mechanical engineer Konievsky. In each of the two rooms stands a handsome wooden bedstead with a proper bed. Other adjoining rooms are also present, a stove that heats well warms both compartments, and soon the servant brings in the steaming samovar; a glass of good tea—“chai,” as the Russian says—is meant to compensate me for the privations and discomfort of the past night. Outside it is cold, with a biting wind blowing, and the air lies leaden-gray over the landscape.

We have a bare half hour's ride. I am heartily glad to have found the staff captain, for I was still afraid that I would be sent straight back from Jinzhoufu to Shanhaiguan. The further I penetrate into the country, the more I can hope that I will be tolerated there. In any case, luck has been on my side. For this morning the staff captain intends to undertake the transport of the first four engines across the frozen Daling River. The river is about 500 meters wide; the bridge across it was completely destroyed by Chinese troops. Indeed, the railway from Jinzhoufu to Newchwang and Mukden, respectively, is for the most part destroyed. Losev's task is to restore it.

Meanwhile, the weather outside has changed remarkably. The leaden atmosphere has drawn closer and closer, and soon the storm is lashing against the windows of the car, sweeping thick dust, indeed outright sand and small pebbles along with it. When the train stops and the call comes: “All out!” we try to protect ourselves against the fine dust as best we can. It is weather such as I have never experienced before and would not care to experience again. The storm, still increasing in force, lashes the skin dreadfully; it is as if one's cheeks were being rubbed with sandpaper in twenty degrees of frost. Without pressing a handkerchief tightly against one's mouth and nose, it is impossible to breathe at all; at times the storm becomes so fierce that it is impossible to keep upright against the pressure. I therefore prefer to lie flat on my [[p41]] face behind the railway embankment for a time and wait until the storm blows itself out. Even the weather-hardened Russians must at times yield to the fury, and for all the discomfort of the situation, it is rather comical to see a whole row of fur-clad figures offering their backsides to the foul weather in a stooped posture. Little by little it clears somewhat; high dunes have formed behind the railway embankment, and the sand has to be scraped out of the tracks to clear the rails again.

The bridge over the Daling River itself is a small masterpiece. For beyond this river they have no engines; consequently it is necessary to get some across before one can think of an efficient repair of the railway line. The river is wide and has a considerable gradient, so that it usually does not freeze over completely. For this reason the water had to be checked at the rapids with brushwood and beams, so that it could freeze more easily. Once it was completely frozen over, long beams were laid across it as railway [[p42]] ties, the rails were spiked onto them, and our four engines are the first to be transported across the river one by one in this manner. The crossing proceeds smoothly. Only at the riverbanks must a helping hand be lent and the bed of ties be levered sideways to achieve the necessary curves. It is indeed a pleasure to see these weather-hardened and stalwart figures at work. This much is certain: the Russian is a good field soldier.
