Port Arthur is a naval port and a military station in the truest sense of the word. In the harbour, warship lies alongside warship, and the streets teem with soldiers of every branch of service. There are said to be 10,000 men stationed in Port Arthur in land troops alone. In any case, one sees massive complexes built up with barracks and quarters, along with long rows of magazines and reserve storehouses, well supplied with all kinds of war materiel—quite apart from the troops and depots located in the ten or so forts surrounding Port Arthur.

[[p84]]

The deep water of the harbour is bounded by a long spit of land extending from the hills situated west of the harbour entrance. Seen from the opposite heights, these resemble a sleeping tiger, and with a little imagination the spit in question can be claimed as the tail of this tiger. Consequently, among the non-Russians of Port Arthur it is also generally called the “tiger’s tail”. It runs essentially in a south-west to north-east direction. The water behind it—that is, north of it—is rather shallow, and there is an intention to fill in this part of the harbour in order to reclaim building land.

Port Arthur itself, the town, clusters around the aforementioned hill on which the lighthouse stands. The main complex lies to the south, though houses and streets in the valley already stretch right around the hill. The plan is to move the small residential and commercial quarter—which is to be retained merely to meet local needs—entirely away from the harbour area, specifically to the northern slope of the hill. In general, the intention is to separate government, military, and residential or commercial quarters strictly from one another. Commercial firms will presumably be tolerated only in such numbers as are necessary to meet the needs of the colony itself. In any event, Alekseyev spoke the absolute truth when he assured that Russia did not intend to create competition for Niuzhuang in Port Arthur. In Port Arthur, certainly not. But elsewhere instead. More on this later, however.

Viewed as a fortress, Port Arthur ought to appear impregnable. That the Japanese nevertheless succeeded in occupying Port Arthur during the Sino-Japanese War was due to corrupt Chinese administration and betrayal. In any case, the Japanese did not succeed in capturing the town from the seaward side either; rather, they had to take the overland route and come from the north. Because of the surrounding hills crowned with forts, warships wishing to bombard Port Arthur would have to fire with heavy howitzers if they wanted to hit the town. It is a military position of the first rank, prepared by nature and developed by engineering. Good roads have also been provided everywhere; above all, excellent connecting roads lead across the heights from fort to fort. In short, everything has been done to make Port Arthur impregnable. At any rate, one cannot escape this impression when standing on Lighthouse Hill and looking down upon the harbour, the throng of houses, the forts, and the barracks. Russia possesses a position here that commands respect, but in the end it has done little itself to create it. Almost everything was already there; the improvements Russia has carried out here do not bear such a proportion to what it found that one could sing Russia any particular hymn of praise with respect to Port Arthur. The railway alone is an exception.

Let us descend again from Lighthouse Hill to the town! As noted, everything is essentially still as it was in Chinese times. Only the Chinese signboards have vanished, and in their place we see Russian inscriptions everywhere. In the street, the heavily fur-clad *izvozchik*, the cab driver, adjusts his cap, and the Polish booth-keeper, in distinctly Jewish German, advertises his felt boots or his cigarettes.

Along the north side of the harbour runs the railway; on the east side is a “bund” some 100 metres long, the only street in Port Arthur laid out [[p85]] and constructed—that is, paved—as such. Along this “bund” are the “offices” of several major European firms. The American Trading Company maintains a branch office there, as does an English import firm. In addition, the street contains a large Russian shop and a restaurant, the finest in town. The rest is occupied by the complex of the Russo-Chinese Bank. In fact, the entire street frontage belongs to the latter institution, and it would long ago have built itself a palace there commensurate with its monopoly and the amount
