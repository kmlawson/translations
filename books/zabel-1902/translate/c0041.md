All the fine "loot" from the Summer Palace near Beijing has gone the way of so much on earth in this manner, and in Port Arthur that way leads through the brothel to the Russo-Chinese Bank, every month, often in thousands of dollars. While I am in Port Arthur, a highly sensational incident happened to take place: a Russian officer shot at one of these ladies because she would not marry him. Fortunately, the shot struck only a less noble part, but despite this proof of love, the beauty did not marry her hot-headed suitor after all. She has no need to, she says. Shocking! says the Englishman. But that is simply part of Port Arthur's character, and people there speak of such matters as casually as we would say "Cheers!"

The Russian is exceptionally sociable, and the number of Russian taverns and restaurants in Port Arthur corresponds accordingly. One generally eats well there, while matters are rather more moderate when it comes to drinking. Everything, however, is unreasonably expensive. Russian cuisine has something delightful in the so-called *zakuska*, the "side dish," all kinds of piquant, fine delicacies enjoyed with bread and butter. In every restaurant one finds a buffet richly stocked with them. Regarding forks, the Russian is not so fussy. Everyone helps themselves with the same fork. It is clean again once it has been thoroughly licked off. Then it passes into other hands and mouths.

One can also find a theatre in Port Arthur, a real, permanent theatre. We visited it yesterday evening. Here one meets all the world, but especially the demimonde. During the intermissions, vodka is drunk and *zakuska* eaten at an especially well-stocked bar; it is swarming with officers, and in the upper galleries with soldiers, who, as back home, pay half price. The actors—and even more so the actresses—who find favor receive lavish presents: silversmith work, gold watches, jewelry, etc., precious and splendid, just as in Petersburg. The acting is excellent, in Little Russian dialect, accompanied by pleasant singing and superb national dancing. One almost forgets that one is in the Far East, at the southern tip of Manchuria, and is reminded of the harsh reality only when one is outside again, when the *izvoshchik* offers his droshky and one [[p89]] [Illustration: Daoist temple on the northern Liaodong Peninsula (p. 62).]

26 February.

"Do you know Dalny yet?" asks my host from the Russo-Chinese Bank in Port Arthur. "I'm sorry, I have not yet had the pleasure. Who is he?" The host laughs and says: "It is not a 'he' at all, it is an 'it,' but a very interesting 'it' that you must see under all circumstances." The door opens to the large bank office where the customers are attended to, and a tall gentleman enters the private office, his still-youthful face framed by a full beard. The green-trimmed uniform fits his body comfortably; on the large-visored officer's cap I see the round civil service cockade, and on the collar a green facing with a crossed hammer and anchor. "Oh, look how well that works out! Mr. Timm—Mr. Zabel! Mr. Zabel does not know Dalny yet, just imagine!—would you take him along? You are traveling back this evening, aren't you? Oh, you could find no better guide for Dalny than Mr. Timm." "Then it's agreed: this evening at 5 o'clock at the station. I will wait for you in front of my carriage, the second to last on the train. You have to walk around the back of the train; the carriage has only one door."

Well, good heavens, how on earth was I supposed to know Dalny? What strange ideas people have about an inquiry-minded stranger, as if he ought to know everything intimately right away, just because he has seen a little more of the world than an ordinary settlement colonist. When I was in Kobe last year and had been there for only two days, I met a gentleman on the street. I asked my companion: "Who is that?" "What, you don't know him yet? Why, that is so-and-so." After two days I am supposed to know all of Kobe!
