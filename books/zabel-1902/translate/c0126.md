our onward journey, about a kilometer upstream on the right bank of the Amur, we find large stone buildings. They are Russian barracks. The fairly densely populated Chinese bank of the Amur between Aigun and Blagoveshchensk presents the same sight as the town of Aigun. Only rubble and ruins, as well as gardens and fields overgrown with weeds, bear witness that industrious countryfolk once lived here who supplied Blagoveshchensk with fruit and vegetables. For a year now, vegetables in Blagoveshchensk are said to have become prohibitively expensive. The poor captain who stopped at Aigun at my request [[p255]] and thereby enabled me to gain a deeper insight into the secrets of Russian colonial policy later received, incidentally, quite a proper reprimand.

Towards two o’clock in the afternoon we reach the confluence area of the Zeya, and from the flat banks the domes and gilded crosses of the churches of Blagoveshchensk already gleam towards us from afar. There, where the Zeya and Amur flow together, lies the city whose name in German means “the Promise.” Originally, Blagoveshchensk was founded as a Cossack post under the name Ust-Zeysk. But when Muravyov had concluded the Treaty of Aigun, he reported this “glad promise” from Ust-Zeysk to the Tsar, and its present name dates from that time. The city has also kept its promises in other respects, in that it has become the center of a thriving gold industry that developed along the banks of the Zeya and on the upper reaches of the Bureya, yielding on average better results than the gold production along the lower reaches of the Amur.

Blagoveshchensk has two hotels, among which we divide ourselves. I move into the Hotel Rossiya, where I have to pay three rubles for the “number,” and in addition, as in Vladivostok, ten kopecks each for towel, pillow, sheet, soap, etc. The finger-deep dust covering all the objects in the room costs nothing. Dusting, however, costs a tip of 20 kopecks. As I hear, it was none too clever of me to choose this particular hotel. The other one, which was founded by a French émigré family and is called “Mon génie,” is considered the better of the two. In any case, the “côtelette à la maréchal” to which Nikolai Mikhailovich invited me this evening is exquisite. Today I also deliver my letter of recommendation to Kunst & Albers, who are undoubtedly the premier firm in town and own the finest building in all of Blagoveshchensk. The manager, Mr. Kloss, a Russian-German, receives me with his customary amiability. Tomorrow morning he will pick me up at my hotel, and then we shall take a drive through the city.
