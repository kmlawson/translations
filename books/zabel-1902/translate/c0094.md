Muravyov, who had been chosen personally by Tsar Nicholas I for his responsible post, departed Petersburg for Okhotsk in January 1848 with the promise to attend especially to the Russian possessions on the sea and to visit Kamchatka; Nevelskoy followed in August from Kronstadt. A year later they met again on the coast of the Sea of Okhotsk. Both had accomplished much in the meantime. Muravyov had continued the journey from his residence in Irkutsk and reached the sea at Okhotsk. Meanwhile, on the personal initiative of the Tsar, it had been decided to select a new port on the Sea of Okhotsk, since the port of that name was iced over for most of the year and offshore sandbanks hindered navigation. At the same time, steps were also to be taken to investigate the estuary of the Amur more closely. Nevelskoy was entrusted with both tasks, and Muravyov hastened to be personally present for these important investigations and at the same time to fulfill his promise regarding Kamchatka. He carried out the visit to the latter in the summer of 1849 and was delighted by the situation of the port of Petropavlovsk on the Bering Sea, which forms Avacha Bay here. He designated the port as a naval station and had it fortified with a number of batteries. He then met with Nevelskoy near Ayan on the Sea of Okhotsk. The latter had in the meantime likewise visited Kamchatka and Petropavlovsk, in order then to explore the north and west coasts of Sakhalin Island. He succeeded in making the discovery, highly important for Muravyov's plans, that Sakhalin is not connected to the mainland by an isthmus, but is an island—separated from the mainland, to be sure, only by a narrow strait four nautical miles wide—a fact that had hitherto eluded all explorers, including La Pérouse, Krusenstern, and Broughton. The great significance of this finding lay in the fact that one was no longer dependent solely on the approach through the Sea of Okhotsk, which was iced over for most of the year, when wishing to reach the mouth of the Amur, but had a second route available, namely through the Gulf of Tartary, whose waters remain open for a far greater part of the year because of the strong current running through it. After this discovery, Nevelskoy had also sailed a short distance up the Amur, thereby simultaneously dispelling a second erroneous notion that stemmed from an extremely clumsy expedition by Lieutenant Gavrilov, misleading in its results, which Nesselrode had been especially fond of citing in his campaign against Muravyov's policy. Gavrilov had claimed, namely, to have found the mouth of the Amur impassable for seagoing vessels, because its depth varied only between one and a half and three feet. "Since, moreover, Sakhalin is a peninsula, the Amur has no value for Russia," Nesselrode lectured.

On the basis of his reports and with the support of his friends in St. Petersburg, [[p193]] Muravyov was able to push through that the garrison of Okhotsk be transferred to Petropavlovsk and, furthermore, that in place of Okhotsk a port, Petrovskoye, on Schastye Bay near the mouth of the Amur, be newly occupied. To be sure, the latter shared the bad quality of the other ports on the Sea of Okhotsk in that it remained icebound until June. Consequently, Nevelskoy, who continued his exploratory work after Muravyov's departure and consistently aligned with his intentions, sailed some 25 versts up the Amur and founded on its left bank the river port of Nikolayevsk—the first Russian settlement on the Amur in more than 160 years! He also established that China exercised no sovereign rights whatever over the resident Gilyaks.
