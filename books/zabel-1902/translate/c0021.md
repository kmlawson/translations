In these negotiations, both between the Chinese and the Russians and between the Russians and myself, two interpreters play the principal role: a Chinese who speaks Russian, and a Russian who speaks German. In general, the Russian soldier speaks no second language, and it should come as no surprise that there are many who cannot even speak proper Russian, since their mother tongue is Kyrgyz, Turkestani, Little Russian, Turkmen, Kurdish, or some other idiom. The Jews, on the other hand, generally speak German, and for the most part, [[p49]] at the subsequent Russian staging posts where I address the commander in German, he first calls for a Levisohn, a Jesaias, or a Judassohn in order to be able to communicate with me with his help. So it is in Goubangzi, too: a Polish non-commissioned officer of the Mosaic faith, and even more of Mosaic speech, interprets what the colonel has to say to me in a thoroughly comical manner of speaking. Almost always, his speech begins with the words: “The high and mighty, highborn noble gentleman, he has sayed: . . . .” Then follows what he “has sayed.” Incidentally, the Jews in the Russian army are said to be good soldiers.

They are said to conduct themselves well during their arduous five-year term of service, and I saw a good number in non-commissioned officer and sergeant positions. They are considered the most educated among the soldiers because, unlike most of their comrades, they can write.

### 3. A Skirmish with Honghuzi.

1 February.

At 8 o'clock in the morning we stand ready for departure, my trunks are ready, and my ten Cossacks are already mounted on their small Siberian horses; naturally, my carter [[p50]] with his cart is missing. It takes repeated summonses to induce him to finally appear after half an hour, and when he happily trots up, he has two miserable mules and an old nag hitched before the cart. My Chinese boy, who is already familiar with my particular standards in this regard, therefore turns the team right back around immediately and eventually returns after another half hour. He has now picked out the best stock the peasant has in his stable, and the cart is at last in proper order and harnessed with two neat ponies and a strong mule. The trunks are lashed down onto the wagon, then a place is prepared for me, likewise one for my Chinese boy, and the train indeed sets into motion around 9 o'clock: ahead three Cossacks, then the wagon, and finally the rest of the riders. But scarcely are we outside the gates and have taken our leave of the Russian officers when the driver and, in addition, three other Chinese leap onto the cart with us and block my view. I ask what on earth these fellows want. It is then explained to me that these are the *mafus* (grooms) and the people who are to look after the cart. Such is the Chinese: a fellow for every beast, and yet another for the cart. Despite their protests, I chase the troublesome company down from the cart, tolerating aside from the driver only one other man, who speaks Russian and can at least be used as an interpreter. The region through which we travel is designated by the Chinese as Guangliang, and the mountain range stretching away to our left they call Guangliangshan.

The route leads us at first in a predominantly easterly direction across the railway line, whose alignment it essentially follows; indeed, we cross the tracks for the first time before their junction. The northern branch runs on in the direction of Mukden, though it is not fully completed, and the southern towards Yingkou. Aside from the railway telegraph, a second telegraph line also runs through this area: the telegraph of the Imperial Chinese Telegraph Administration. The railway line is no longer passable; the rails have been lifted from the sleepers, and these have been hauled off or lie thrown together in heaps beside the embankment. All of the road overpasses are destroyed. The region makes a rather desolate impression; the few villages through which one passes are in an unusually pitiful condition, even by Chinese standards, the houses built for the most part only of gaoliang straw and smeared with mud, the enclosures surrounding each homestead likewise consisting only of mud, and the roads are so dusty that our little cavalcade is virtually continuously enveloped in a cloud of dust. Not a tree, not a shrub, relatively little tilled land, but in its place frozen marshes and salt steppe across which the railway embankment runs. The country is so flat that when travelling to the left of the embankment, one sees nothing of what lies to the right of it, and vice versa. If to this picture one adds the insecurity prevailing all around us, one can form a fair idea that living—or rather vegetating—here permanently cannot count among life's greatest pleasures, and that such a desolate country provides the ideal refuge for all manner of bandits and riffraff, whether in or out of a uniform jacket.
