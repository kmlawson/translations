This latter applies primarily to salmon fishing and the herring catch. If the journey through the tropics has hitherto placed an insurmountable obstacle in the way of transporting these fish, this obstacle too will soon be removed—not, to be sure, by the Siberian Railway, since its freight rates would be too high and, after all, in midsummer it also runs through tropical climates (tropical, at least, as far as temperatures are concerned)—but rather by the construction of refrigerated vessels, which will be equipped like the ships with which splendid Australian beef is likewise brought fresh to England through the tropics and the Red Sea. It is hardly an exaggeration to say that this could give rise to strong competition for our domestic fishing industry. Yet it remains questionable whether the dangers inherent in this are not amply outweighed by the fact that in salmon and cheaper herring one obtains an enrichment of our inexpensive and nutritious staple foods. Were the importation to be taken in hand by a benevolent company that does not seek to earn too much in the process, it would seem to me by no means utopian to express the hope that fine Siberian salmon might become a cheap staple food among us, despite the objections of the delicatessen merchants. The precondition for this would, of course, be that deliveries were uninterrupted and regular, and that is precisely the point where even the best Russian company proves mortal. So things will probably not move quite so terribly fast with the new staple food after all.

### 3. Farewell to the Pacific Ocean.

July 15.

The fine days of Gaidamak are now over! Since this morning we have once again been afloat on the open sea. We slept the night on board and then had our tea on the bridge. Toward 9 o'clock in the morning we are already approaching the mountain island of Askold, where the lighthouse keeper is once again sitting under a cap of fog, sounding his foghorn and firing cannon shots, while down below we glide along in the most glorious sunshine. Askold lies off the mouth of the coastal river Suifen, which forms the geographic boundary to the south between the Siberian Maritime Province, the Primorskaya, and Chinese Manchuria, although the political border runs further south, [[p169]] and includes within Siberia the Suifen Valley as well as Possiet Bay, which lies directly to the south on the border of Korea and likewise harbors a good port. In the days of the Russian conquest of these territories, Askold was the stronghold of the Honghuzi, who from here harassed the Russian settlers with raids and incursions and also dug for gold on the island. But when Russia's military power grew stronger on the Pacific coast, this robbers' nest was cleared out, and today a gold mine flourishes here, owned by a Vladivostok merchant named Lindholm and, to my knowledge, the only gold mine in this part of Siberia where quartz gold rather than alluvial gold is extracted. In order to secure operations against predatory attacks and also to supervise and keep in check the workers employed in the mine, a military guard has been stationed there.

Soon, not too far off, we see white masonry gleaming in the morning sun; one more turn around a headland, and through the opening narrow gap, gradually growing wider, we see parts of the panorama of Vladivostok emerge, until we reach the narrow harbor entrance itself and the whole picture now unfolds—an impression marred only by the fact that we know all too well how severely our illusions must suffer as soon as we are jolting over potholes in a dusty troika once again and having to pay four reichsmarks if we want to bathe away the dust. The harbor basin proper has been bestowed by the Russians with the beautiful name "the Golden Horn." The choice of this name is no coincidence; it is meant to evoke Constantinople. It reflects a policy of which Peter the Great was the first successful representative—after whom, after all, the bay on which Vladivostok lies is named—namely, the policy of creating outlets to the sea for continental Russia. The harbor is the gateway to the wide world. That was why Peter created a new Russian capital at the mouth of the Neva; hence Russia's eager endeavor to gain access to the Mediterranean through the Bosporus*); hence the struggle with England for a port on the Persian Gulf; and hence Russia's successful striving for a port on an ice-free sea in the Far East. Before anyone thought of Port Arthur and Dalny, Vladivostok represented the fulfillment of these wishes, and since the winter of 1893–1894 it has also been possible to keep the harbor's fairway open with icebreakers, so that at a pinch Russia could have been content with this outlet. Thus, here in the Far East, Russia received a new Constantinople, as it were, as a provisional installment on its desires regarding European Stamboul. For the stranger, however, the *tertium comparationis* between ancient Constantinople and Vladivostok, the "Ruler of the East," seems at first to lie in the fact that both cities, viewed from the outside, are magnificent, and viewed from within, are dreadful.
