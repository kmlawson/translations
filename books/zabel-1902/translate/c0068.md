Besides, not every American ship captain has such bad luck; the mere large number of ships captured annually proves that the business still brings in money after all, and that the number of vessels that are not caught is probably far greater than that of those seized. For the Russians, too, are like the people of Nuremberg when it comes to hanging. Naturally, there is much romance in this fish poaching, and if one does not let oneself be caught, the profit is rich compensation for the dangers endured. The captains are therefore usually daring fellows, and this petty war between Russian cruisers and American fish marauders could furnish ample material for a thrilling sea novel.

I wish to recount only one such brief episode, which dates from the very latest times and may prove that with the Lloyd passenger steamers, not all the romance of sea travel has yet vanished from the oceans of the world.

One of these captains, together with his no less daring crew, with whom he was accustomed to share the profits, wished to pay a visit to the seal islands in the vicinity of Kamchatka. For a particular species of seal occurs there—and only at a certain time of year*)—whose pelt is of quite extraordinary value. The Russian government protects these animals through special regulations regarding closed seasons, the age of the seals that may be killed, etc. Every spring, on the first steamer of the Volunteer Fleet—which calls there regularly four times in all during the summer on its circuit through the Sea of Okhotsk—the government sends a detachment of soldiers who have nothing else to do but see to the observance of the statutory regulations by the authorized Russian fishery lessees and to keep foreign mariners away from the valuable islands. In addition, there are several cruisers in these waters that continually lie in wait for any unauthorized persons.

The aforementioned captain, however, dares to land on one of these islands nonetheless and, with his crew, begins a fierce slaughter among the seals basking on the beach, which cannot reach the water so quickly. They are in the thick of their work; a large part of the catch has already been skinned, and the pelts are packed aboard the schooner. Then suddenly, in the distance, a wisp of smoke appears on the horizon—a Russian cruiser! The latter soon notices the American ship as well and now bears down upon it under full steam. The American captain has pushed off from shore with all haste, hoisted every sail, and away they go into the open sea. But the warship cuts him off. The American, whose ship sails excellently, hopes to slip through and steers straight for the warship. The latter at first believes the American recognizes the hopelessness of his situation and is coming to surrender without further ado, so as to escape the threatening guns. But what is this? Having come at full speed to within about fifty feet of the cruiser, the captain shouts a filthy swear word across to the Russian and suddenly veers sharply away, in the direction from which the warship had come. It all happens with such speed that the cruiser's captain hardly realizes what is happening. Before long, however, the cruiser also swings round, and off it goes in pursuit of the schooner at full steam. Now comes the turn of the usual cannon shots. Twice the Russian fires a blank, then a solid shot through the schooner's rigging. He does not want to fire any more. For although he would be able to shoot the wooden vessel to pieces, the share that the captain and officers are entitled to receive from the proceeds of the prized goods is far too valuable to them. As a result, a veritable race develops on the open ocean between these two unequal craft, in which the outcome remains undecided for a long time. Finally nature comes to the Russian's aid: the wind slackens a little, and the Russian is already running parallel to the schooner's bow. Thereupon the schooner attempts a new maneuver: he turns his stem directly toward the flank of the cruiser, as if he meant to ram her to the bottom. Hardly has the cruiser time to sheer off slightly when the schooner's bowsprit collides with the little warship's anchor crane. With that, however, the American has ensnared himself. The bowsprit of his vessel is boarded immediately, soldiers leap over onto the schooner, and meeting no resistance, the Russian captain takes possession of the American ship. The search revealed a nearly full cargo of the valuable seal pelts—a handsome profit for the captain and officers of the cruiser! The schooner was then placed under a Russian guard of six men, moored alongside the cruiser, and they dropped anchor in a not too distant bay. The Russians rejoiced over the good catch. But they had rejoiced too soon. For the next morning, as day began to dawn, the schooner had vanished without a trace!
