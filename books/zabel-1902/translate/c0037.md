The train is quite full. It swarms with Russian officials wearing uniforms similar to military dress, but distinguished from the active military—who wear oval cockades—by their round cockades. They are all figures in whom one can immediately recognize the weather-beaten toughness that simply goes with this harsh climate. One often sees wild, disheveled beards, but mostly above them a pair of good-natured little eyes gazing shrewdly out into the world.

The distance to Port Arthur is approximately 360 kilometers. Nevertheless, our train is not scheduled to arrive there before 9 o'clock tomorrow morning; it thus takes exactly 21 hours to travel this short distance. Of the dawdling pace of Russian railway operations, one can [[p80]] hardly form an idea with our home notions of punctuality. Stops are made at every station, often for up to an hour. A hundred times the carriage door is slammed open and shut; the journey is far from pleasant.

The landscape, by the way, is by no means devoid of charm. At first, one travels along the foot of the mountain range that cuts through the Liaodong Peninsula and ends only at Port Arthur. The heights, though treeless and bare, are nevertheless interesting simply because they are heights and mountains—one learns to be modest in China! Every now and then, steep knolls rise up directly from the plain before them, often crowned with a temple, lending the Manchurian mountain landscape something characteristic. The villages here no longer bear a specifically Manchurian character; one sees pointed roofs again, as in Zhili and Shandong, a circumstance indicating that we find ourselves in a land where Chinese colonists strongly assert their influence. After a while, the railway cuts through the mountains in rather sharply cut passes, and that makes the journey even somewhat romantic. It is 6 o'clock in the evening. Night begins to veil the landscape. I stretch out on my wooden bench and fall into a restless sleep, disturbed less by the rattling of the carriage than by the endless slamming of doors and the long, noisy stops at the stations.

February 21.

At daybreak I am up again. We are still four hours away from Port Arthur. Now, for the first time, I notice properly constructed overpasses and bridges again. Hitherto the track had always been routed around the site where an overpass was eventually to stand—proof that this railway too must have been built in considerable haste, and that they are only gradually beginning to put the finishing touches to the work.

Incidentally, right here in the south of the Liaodong Peninsula numerous terrain difficulties had to be overcome. The region is very mountainous, especially at the narrowest point of the peninsula, where it narrows like a spit of land and the mountains approach close to the sea on both sides. The southern tip itself, on which Port Arthur lies, widens again.

It seems to me as though the train travels at greater speed the closer we get to Port Arthur. There is a fair amount of life at the stations; officials, and in some cases officials' wives, board the train, traveling into the city to run their errands and do their shopping; the compartment fills with all kinds of chattering and—curiously enough—book-"browsing" folk. Once more, the high walls of a 23-meter-deep cutting (this figure was given to me by a Russian railway official traveling with us) tower up on both sides, shutting out light and view; then we slowly descend into the valley of a small river, and already we see the first houses of Port Arthur appearing on the heights before us—barracks buildings, of course. Following the course of the river, we turn around the last outlying hill, upon which stands a tall lighthouse, and then pull up before a wooden ramp in the midst of an immense swarm of screeching, clamoring, and brawling coolies, armed for the most part with bamboo poles, who immediately storm the compartments and set [[p81]] about snatching the travelers' belongings from their hands without any invitation, until here too the Cossacks appear with their knouts and—this time to the blessing of traveling humanity—whip the coolies down from the carriages and the platform. This is Port Arthur, railway station!
