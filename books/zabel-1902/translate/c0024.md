Halfway there, we encounter a Cossack patrol scouting the railway line. No incidents of an unpleasant nature occur.

After about 70 li, we pass under the railway embankment; the overpass is destroyed, but the rails are still in place. From here on, the region becomes livelier again. We meet several caravans of carts; to our left in the distance, we can already see the blue mountains that lie before the Liaodong Peninsula and continue into it. The Chinese call this mountain range Liangjiashan. From this point on, it was said to be only 100 li to Yingkou.

After about 30 further li, I reach the great Liao River, specifically at the town of Tianzhuangtai, the first town I have seen since Chenzhousu. Here, a company of the Fourth Rifle Regiment is stationed on railway guard duty. The staging post commandant with whom I lodge is still a young man, who was stationed for a long time in Petersburg and was then transferred to Siberia. He raves to me about Paris and about [[p55]] European life; he has travelled through a large part of Europe and now sits here at a lonely staging post, living on his fond memories, growing bitter and languishing. He longs to return to European life and to his books. He is quite well-versed in the literature of his country as well as in German and French literature, and feels his present seclusion from the world to be a cruel fate.

6 February.
I set out early in order to arrive in Yingkou as soon as possible. At first we travel along the ice of the Liao River (the Chinese call the river the Dalan River), then we cross over to the other bank. The railway had already turned slightly westward some 20 li before Tianzhuangtai. It avoids crossing the wide river, remains on its right bank, and finally ends right by the river near its mouth, opposite the Chinese city of Yingkou. Following the bend of the river, my route leads slightly eastwards. The lines of wagons grow more frequent; often there are some 40 or more Chinese carts rolling past us heavily laden. The drivers have an immense respect for the Cossacks. As soon as they see them approaching, they steer clear in a wide arc, and on such occasions it happens several times that two or three carts drive over one of the numerous earthen embankments and overturn, much to the Cossacks’ delight. The closer we come to Yingkou, the more numerous the wagon trains become; right up to the houses of the European settlement one can see the high clouds of dust they throw up. Around noon, I arrive at the extensive [[p56]] buildings of the Russian railway, the “Chinese Eastern Railway,” which are now barely an hour away from the city of Yingkou. At one o’clock, I make my entrance into Yingkou with my cavalcade, stared at with curiosity by everyone I meet. I am directed to the German hotel, owned by a former German ship captain named Decker; I dismiss my Cossacks with a tip and a few bottles of vodka after reporting them to the town commandant, and rejoice at the journey now behind me and the rest that I shall seek here. Only at the hotel do I learn that I am the first European since the beginning of the Boxer unrest to have arrived overland in Yingkou—or, as Europeans are accustomed erroneously to call it, in Newchwang.*) My journey seems to cause quite a sensation among the citizens of Yingkou.
