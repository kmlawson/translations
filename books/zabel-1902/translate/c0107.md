Just as in the fur trade, the Chinese has forged ahead of the Russian in many other matters as well. This, too, is partly due to the fact that the living and working conditions under which the immigrant Russians find themselves here are of a different nature than at home. The Russian peasant is simply, first and foremost, a peasant. Here he has suddenly been transplanted into a kind of Cockaigne, where many a thing grows directly into his hands that previously had cost him the sweat of his brow. I have already mentioned the phenomenon of Russian peasants parceling out the land allotted to them, leasing it to Chinese, playing the landlord themselves, and living off hunting. Others, again, cover their food needs by fishing, while still others live by felling firewood for the steamers plying the rivers. Yet another part is employed in railway construction; in short, the percentage of actual tillers of the soil of Russian nationality remaining after this sifting is unlikely to be all that high.

Thus it is the Chinese who takes the agricultural labour off the Russian's hands. He is a cultivator by tradition; his methods are generally more practical and allow a parcel of land to be utilized more fully with less labour. Their settlements therefore soon attain a certain prosperity. Unfortunately, it cannot be determined numerically how much of the grain ground by the seven steam mills located in the Ussuri region has been produced by Chinese and how much by Russians. This is difficult to ascertain if only because the rent paid by the Chinese is frequently in the form of allowances in kind, so that he does not come into contact with the mills at all. The percentage, however, is likely not [[p215]] small.

Indeed, it is precisely here, where no *dira necessitas* compels the Russian peasant to remain true to the ancestral habit of agriculture even as a colonist, that it is interesting to observe how, transplanted into conditions that are in part more favourable, he deviates from his habits without, however, being able to master the altered conditions. In general, the Russian peasant from the Volga or Don region who is transplanted to Siberia knows only how to grow his rye or his barley. In addition, he understands a little pastoral farming. Now it must, to be sure, be admitted that, according to general observation, this southern tip of Siberia is not particularly well suited to livestock farming. The animals suffer extraordinarily from the bites of a vicious fly and die en masse from the so-called Siberian disease, which in all probability is nothing other than the rinderpest that also frequently occurs in Chinese Manchuria. But especially in agriculture, the Russian immigrant, if he is not altogether too lazy for it, is conservative. He is very difficult to win over to innovations, notably also to the introduction of machinery. Nor does it occur to him to learn from the culturally superior Chinese, although he sees that the latter achieves excellent income from vegetable farming and frequently even from fruit growing and gardening. He leaves the field to him, as long as he can fish and hunt in the summer and in the winter has dried enough fish and enough meat to lie on the warm stove bench and let his brick-tea soup slip over a piece of sugar he has placed on his tongue beforehand. But if, as happens more often than one would think, he has not shot enough red and roe deer over a summer or has missed the salmon run—well, then he simply starves through the rest of the winter, and if that rest is too long, he begs the well-to-do for help. Exactly the same conditions as in European Russia!

To be sure, there are also exceptions. In particular, the best experiences have been had with immigrants from the Baltic provinces. These people also command significantly higher wages than the peasants from the southern Russian provinces. But the average is just as described. I made this observation consistently throughout the rest of my journey, where I heard it shared with concern by sensible Russians. Will Russia be able to carry out its colonising plans with human material of this kind? The Russian peasant is incredibly backward culturally; there is no doubt that in standard of living, skill, industry, and capability—that is, simply culturally—he ranks below the Chinese; the Chinese coolie lives on average in better quarters than the Russian woodcutter and certainly fares no worse. Only in physical strength and brutality in its application is the Russian peasant superior to the Chinese; yet the latter is the more solid character. He also preserves his nationality, whereas the numerous Koreans living on Russian soil have all become Russian subjects, partly, to be sure, because they are political refugees who would face the death penalty upon their return home.
