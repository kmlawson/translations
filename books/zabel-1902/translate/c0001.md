# Foreword.

The expeditions and journeys that provided the material for the present work were not yet on the itinerary of my second departure for East Asia, which took place in early August of the year 1900. The decision to cross the southern part of Manchuria owes its origin to a spontaneous impulse that only occurred to me in Beijing when, after the conclusion of the preliminary peace at the beginning of the year 1901, I saw myself at the end of my activity as representative of the *Vossische Zeitung* [Voss's Newspaper] in the Chinese theatre of war, while my appetite for enterprise was not yet satisfied. Only a few days separated the decision and the start of its execution. Under these circumstances, there could naturally be no question of scholarly preparation beyond the knowledge of the regions to be traveled that I had gathered during my stays in East Asia, and for a proper assessment of the subsequent chapters, I consider it necessary to confess that the task I envisioned upon my departure from Beijing initially appeared to me to be a purely journalistic one. If I express the hope that the reader will find in this work somewhat more than a travel narrative suited to the taste of the educated newspaper reader, the reason lies primarily in the—at least initially—rather planless design of these journeys, in the course of which the objectives expanded along with the growing tasks. I hardly need emphasize that, as soon as circumstances permitted, I also engaged very thoroughly with the questions treated here on a theoretical level, and that the diary format corresponds to the journals kept on the journey only in its form, its factual skeleton, and its dates, whereas the actual elaboration was the work of the past year.

If I have retained the diary format, stylistic reasons were primarily decisive for this. Furthermore, the questions treated here are so far from being common knowledge among readers that one is forced to assume only a fairly slight familiarity with them. A systematic presentation, however, easily tempts one into a doctrinaire tone in such cases, whereas the diary format permits a more artistic treatment of the material, which in this version is brought far more vividly before the reader's eyes and therefore sticks with them more easily. Whether the execution corresponds to the intention, I cannot judge for myself; nevertheless, the aforementioned reason was primarily decisive for me in justifying a departure from the otherwise customary form of presentation—which, of course, is by no means to say that I allowed myself any poetic license regarding the subject matter treated that might give rise to doubts about the seriousness of the scholarly treatment of the material.

[[p11]]

Then, too, the diary dates protect me against the reproach that individual judgments and observations which I report as such have meanwhile been overtaken by events. History has certainly marched on since those days, and the reader will in certain cases, particularly in those sections dealing with Russia's position and intentions in southern Manchuria, be aware that more recent events have occurred that partly supplement and partly confirm my own observations. In the work itself, the reader's attention has been explicitly drawn to this at individual points by means of notes. I therefore kindly ask that this circumstance be remembered when reading in such cases.—

For an understanding of certain questions concerning connections with China and the recent Chinese campaign, the remark may perhaps be useful that the present work is in many respects a continuation of my work *Deutschland in China* [Germany in China], published by the same house. This applies particularly to the political and economic sections, for a closer understanding of which a recourse to the first, more theoretical book of the aforementioned work should prove expedient, while the descriptive chapters of *Deutschland in China* find their direct continuation in *Durch die Mandschurei und Sibirien* [Through Manchuria and Siberia].
