Yes, Russia is vast! Had the Trans-Siberian Railway been executed as originally planned, namely along the northern bank of the Amur and the Shilka, the distance from Vladivostok to St. Petersburg would have amounted to 9,877 versts—that is, since one verst equals 1.067 kilometers, 10,538.76 kilometers of direct railway line. The connecting line through Manchuria, which was carried out instead of the Amur project, reduces this figure by several hundred; nevertheless, even then the distance is still over 10,000 kilometers. Indeed, Russia is vast! Astonishingly vast by our standards! People admire this vastness, and the Russian himself admires it most of all. It is above all to this giant body that Russia owes its prestige in the eyes of nations. People marvel at its size and generalize this astonishment to Russia’s standing in other matters as well, since generalizing about things one does not know thoroughly is, after all, simply part of human nature. Yet one ought really to marvel at Russia only as one might marvel at a human being with a particularly long body, without giving thought to his other moral and intellectual merits. As soon as one recovers from this purely external impression, however, and grows accustomed to the fact that this size is ultimately nothing more than a purely external attribute, like shortness in a dwarf, one will presumably proceed by degrees to examine the giant body’s heart, kidneys, and brain. fairly obvious starting point for this examination, however, is usually already provided by the ratio between the extent of the land and population density. In our case, the fact is already quite sobering that Russia in Asia possesses fully a third of the continent, yet on this third lives only a forty-second of Asia’s total population. By way of comparison, China may be cited, which occupies roughly a quarter of the Asian continent while housing half of Asia’s population. Or, to put the ratio differently, on a piece of land where 28 people live in China, only a single person lives in Russian Asia. In this calculation, Asiatic Russia is taken as a whole. The disparity becomes even more glaring, however, if we consider Siberia alone. The land area of Siberia comprises roughly three-quarters of Russia’s total possessions in Asia, yet Siberia’s population in number barely reaches a quarter of the total population of Asiatic Russia. Therefore, on the same patch of earth where one person lives in Siberia, an average of nine people live in the rest of Asiatic Russia. If, however, we correlate Siberia with China in terms of population density, the resulting ratio is 1:252! The population density of Siberia is roughly such that there is one inhabitant for approximately three square kilometers: barely five million people live in Siberian Russia. In his work *Die Erde und ihre Bewohner* [The Earth and Its Inhabitants], the statistician Reclus distributes these five million among no fewer than 59 different races, among whom the Muscovites even retain an absolute majority in numbers. Furthermore, the population of Siberia—which in head count thus remains roughly one and a half million below that of London—is divided by religion into Greek Catholics, Shamanists, Buddhists, Mohammedans, Jews, and unclassifiable heathens. Seen in the cold light of day, the brain of this giant body of Siberia is thus quite tiny and heterogeneous, and it will probably require a very considerable influx of blood from Europe before this bit of brain will be in a position to govern the gigantic limbs properly. Whether the Trans-Siberian Railway, which is intended after all to become the main artery of this invigorating bloodstream, will actually be able to fulfill this task?

Once before, all these lands for which the Tsar’s word is law, and across which the sun travels on its way to Moscow, served a single mighty name. Over there in the Suifen Valley once lay the fabled kingdom of Bohai, whose star sank before the realm of Manchur; this realm of Manchur, however, perished in the gale that swept the hordes of Genghis Khan across the Asian kingdoms and steppes just as it did across Europe, overturning thrones and blowing away borders here as there. To be sure, the differences between the two meeting invasions were great. The yellow tidal wave came swiftly, overwhelming everything, sweeping everything along, but then receded—from Western Europe at least—almost as swiftly as it had come. The counter-current covered in centuries the same path that the first wave had inundated in a few years, and—held its ground. There was, however, no system in either of them, and the movement from west [[p176]] to east in particular, when viewed in its course, appears so unstoppable only because it met with too little—one might almost say with no resistance whatsoever—that could have halted it. In any case, the Russians had an easier time conquering Siberia and advancing to the Pacific than Genghis Khan had in throwing the European realms off balance.
