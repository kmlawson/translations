## Chapter V. Over 2,000 Kilometres up the Amur.

### 1. On the Black Dragon River.

Apart from the waterway, there is no connecting road suitable for travel purposes between Khabarovsk and Sretensk, the easternmost station of the Trans-Baikal Railway. Highways have been constructed only in stretches, while the regions in between can be traversed only on horseback and with pack animals. This mode of transport is maintained exclusively during the transitional period between navigation and sleigh traffic on the Amur, and even then only for passengers and urgent mail. For anyone who can bear the cold, the most pleasant trip is the winter journey on the ice by sleigh. Of course, that is a matter of taste. I can well imagine that many a person prefers the steamer journey. To be sure, when I consider that over 2,000 kilometres of travel by water lie ahead of me—strictly speaking, it is 2,066 versts to Sretensk—and when, on the other hand, I look around my tugboat and reflect that this uninviting tub is supposed to be by far the largest and best of the various steamers to which I shall entrust myself, a feeling comes over me as though my heart were being dragged down to the sole of my left boot by a heavy weight. For I have also taken a closer look at the interior of my new lodging and have arrived at the conviction that the most tolerable place to stay on board is wherever one can let the most fresh air blow around one’s nose. It smells horribly of human flesh! Every little spot seems to be taken. It was natural that I should first look around for the little spot where, in the view of the shipping agent who filled in the number on my ticket, I was to lay my weary head. This [[p235]] little spot is not in a cabin, but in a dark room which is already very cramped in itself, is moreover filled by an enormous wooden table, and otherwise represents the ship’s saloon. What must the cabins look like! There was no sign of a bed or anything similar; instead, on the wooden bench that ran around the table along the partly crooked walls, lay mattresses covered with glossy leather and stuffed with something hard. If you stretch out on them, you have roughly the sensation of lying on a wooden plank bed. If only one could at least have settled down on it! But no, that was not possible. For in the spot intended for me someone else was already lying—with long, flowing hair, by the way—a poet?—no, merely a priest! The poor fellow presumably assisted today in the midday heat at the two-hour outdoor church service. He who serves grows weary, so let us let him sleep his fill first! I am about to retreat so as to postpone taking possession of the place due to me until later. Just then the curly-haired man stirs, produces a short snoring sound, then a sound that sounds like the “ch” in “machen”, and with a sudden sideways turn of his curly head, spits under the table. Horrified, I follow his movements: that seems to be where the others spit as well; one man alone could not have been responsible for all that!

It practically spins me right out the door, and I climb the stairs again toward light and air. If you are not careful, you bump the crown of your head going up, and your forehead going down. The stairs lead onto the deck. That is the place where the steerage passengers travel. “Tween-decks”, what a euphemism! On the “Sergei Dukhovskoi” there is only a single deck. This consists of iron and is scorching hot by day, bitterly cold at night. The poor passengers lie directly on the iron; beneath their heads or backs they have placed their belongings. Above them arches the vault of heaven, nothing else. We cabin passengers, for that matter, have it not a whit better! Our abode by day—and I believe, for my part, better by night as well—is likewise the deck. To be sure, we reside one level higher. Over the midsection of the vessel and at the same time over the two side paddlewheels sits a superstructure containing the galley, the ship officers’ cabins, the pantry, the oil room, and a few other similar compartments. It is above these compartments, then, that we find ourselves. Fortunately, our deck is of wood. Yet despite the blazing rays of the sun, we have no awning or anything of the kind above us either. Only the so-called chart house and the captain’s berth, which stand up in the middle of the deck, provide a little shade. My fellow passengers have already recognized the advantage and carried the wooden bench as well as the few existing chairs into the shade. My trusty long chair also stands up on deck, albeit in the burning sun. I see already that here politeness—as on the train toward the general—is out of place, and it is every man for himself. So let us act accordingly! It is, to be sure, an unchristian thought. But the priest down there on my cushion thinks in exactly the same way, and he, to top it off, is a man of God.
