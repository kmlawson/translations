The Trans-Siberian Railway now lies behind us. Without doubt a monumental engineering feat that belongs among the wonders of the modern world! It is a tremendous civilizing task that Russia has accomplished with the construction of this link to the Far East, and this glory remains undiminished, even if the construction in its particulars gives manifold cause for sharp criticism. The building of the railway was both a political and an economic necessity for Russia, and there can also be no doubt that the railway has influenced the development of the vast territory through which we have traveled over a distance of more than 8,000 kilometers in the most advantageous manner. It is, of course, another question whether one should join in the hymn of praise that the press of all countries—and naturally not least the Russian—is accustomed to singing to the railway's construction. For us Western Europeans, the significance of the railway lies primarily in the fact that through it a relatively [[p318]] [Illustration: On the great Volga bridge (p. 309).]

[[p319]]

rapid connection with the Far East has been created. If, however, it is constantly claimed on the Russian side that travel from Western Europe to East Asia would be possible in nine days upon completion of the Manchurian railway line, this assertion belongs, for the next ten years at any rate, to the realm of pious wishes. In the foreseeable future, it is hardly conceivable that the journey between Berlin and Dalny will be possible in less time than at least 14 days. The Trans-Siberian Railway is single-track, switches are not very numerous, and the traffic that the railway already has to handle—which is of almost exclusively Russian origin—is so considerable that the line can hardly bear any further increase through the participation of foreign trade. Even now there is a shortage of rolling stock. In addition, the operation of the railway leaves an extraordinary amount to be desired, as does its construction. I mentioned that our luxury train travels at a speed of only 25 kilometers per hour. That is a speed roughly corresponding to that of our slow branch lines. But the Trans-Siberian trains can hardly [[p320]] travel any faster. The roadbed was laid rather carelessly in the haste of its construction, and by the Russians' own admission the quality of the rail material leaves much to be desired; above all, the rails are far too light for express train traffic. They are the same rails used among us, for example, for the construction of a light railway. Whether the use of these thin rails is to be attributed to embezzlement, which is after all no rarity in Russia, or to a miscalculation may be left an open question. At all events, it is a fact that these immense blunders have already become very clearly apparent, and that Russia is already forced to tear up the entire track again from Chelyabinsk onward and replace it with heavier rails, while the lighter ones are to be used for laying shunting tracks and the like. Added to this, furthermore, as quite a

substantial defect, which unfavourably affects both operational safety and regular transport on the line, is the fact that all the sleepers are made of wood that has not been impregnated and therefore quickly falls victim to the changeable weather. Moreover, the sleepers lie comparatively far apart, so that when travelling one often has the sensation that the train is gliding across an elastic swamp. For the time being, therefore, one can hardly assume any substantial possibility of traffic increase on the Trans-Siberian Railway. The repairs that will foreseeably be needed very soon will do their part to depress the feasibility and speed of transport to a level that falls far short of the claims put about by the Russian side. It is not the intention here to study the economic changes that the construction of the Trans-Siberian Railway has already brought about for Siberia itself and for world commerce. But one thing is clear: on financial grounds alone, Russia cannot possibly manage in the long run with the cheap passenger and [[p321]] freight rates. If the profitability of the railway is to be ensured, either its capacity must be increased to a level unattainable under present conditions, or an increase in tariffs must be undertaken. In no case, however, would the railway fulfill the purpose so often invoked: it would not be in a position to divert significantly the transit goods traffic with the Far East, which hitherto has chosen exclusively the sea route, because freight traffic on Russian railways proceeds at an incredibly slow pace. Based on Russian commercial practice, through-freight from Germany to East Asia would foreseeably take about three times longer than by sea. Added to this is the fact that the sea route is in the long run cheaper under all circumstances. For us, therefore, the only advantages that remain are in passenger and postal traffic. There is no question that these advantages exist, although a final verdict will only be possible once the railway is completed along its entire length. For people who wish to travel in haste from Europe to the Far East, 14 days is without doubt a considerable shortening of travel time, and the same applies to postal dispatches. But the journey remains, even when the line is completed, quite exhausting, and the low cost of the trip is offset by very considerable hardships that do not arise on a sea voyage. Above all, one will also have to reckon in future with severe overcrowding on the trains, so that, first of all, travel comfort will suffer greatly, and furthermore, the prospects of securing smooth connections at all times are none too favourable.
