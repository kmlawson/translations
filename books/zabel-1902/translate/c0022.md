We are not far from the village of Liangjiatu, a rather large settlement, when the Chinese on my cart suddenly begin shifting restlessly in their seats and looking continually in one direction. I inquire what is the matter and learn that suspicious riders have been spotted back there, probably Honghuzi. I draw my Cossacks' attention to them, and indeed, we see about thirty horsemen halted among burial mounds at a distance of about 1,000 meters. Unconcerned by this, we continue on our way; but once we have passed the village and emerge again on its eastern side, we come under fire and

[Illustration: Banquet at the village headman's in Gouhanzi (p. 29).]

see the horsemen reappear. The fellows hit nothing; evidently they shoot without aiming, as the Chinese usually do, because they themselves are more afraid of their gun than the person they are shooting at. Unconcerned by the banging of shots, during which our Chinese virtually curl up into themselves, we continue on our way; but when the supposed Honghuzi make ready to accompany us—at a respectful distance, to be sure—and keep shooting, the Cossacks leap from their horses, take cover behind them, lay their rifles across the saddles, and return fire. Then, in a flash, six of them are back on their horses and gallop straight at the horsemen. The latter, however, are no less nimble than the Russians, and at a dead gallop they scatter, all in different [[p52]] directions. One of the Cossacks shoots one man's horse dead beneath him. When they reach the spot, the poor beast is lying on the ground; the fellow has vanished into the nearby village.*) As we cannot delay for long, the Cossacks soon rejoin me, and I continue on my way, forewarned by this incident and richer by an experience. In any case, the incident proves to me that it would have been a foolhardy gamble had I tried to continue my journey from Gouhanzi alone.

Soon after this adventure, we cross the railway once again, leaving it to our left, and now take an east-southeasterly direction. We are located directly north of the northernmost inlet of the Gulf of Liaodong. Shortly after midday, we arrive at the Russian staging post of Tujatai, where First Lieutenant Kozmin is the post commander and my Cossacks are relieved. Through the mediation of Mr. Levisohn, I tell the captain where I have come from and where I am bound, and report my skirmish with the Manchus. He is not surprised by this, but explains that such things happen almost every day. The patrols that patrolled the railway line had to deal with bandits or regulars almost daily.—Good prospects for the onward journey!
