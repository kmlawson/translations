Yet Muravyov, too, had not lost sight of his goal in the meantime. The Chinese had declared themselves ready to carry out the border settlement on the Gorbitsa proposed in the aforementioned letter from the Foreign Ministry. Muravyov, however, stalled the negotiations and undertook his second great expedition, with which he transported over 8,000 persons of both sexes to the lower reaches of the Amur and had them settled there by his faithful assistant, Prince Volkonsky, who belonged to a family of exiles. On this second transport he also brought significant reinforcements, by which, among other things, the garrison of De-Kastri Bay was increased to 500 Cossacks.

When Elliot had searched in vain for the vanished Russian ships until the onset of autumn, he reappeared at the beginning of October in the Tatar Strait, where, in his opinion, the Russian fleet had to withdraw before winter. In the meantime, De-Kastri Bay had once again been stripped of troops down to 70 men. Nonetheless, under cover of the dense forest, these seventy engaged the English landing force, which fought against an invisible enemy. Meanwhile, reinforcements were also brought up from Mariinsk; however, they no longer intervened, as the English set sail in mid-October. With this, the military operations of that year came to an end, and the following year brought peace.

In September 1855, Chinese negotiators had appeared before Muravyov in Mariinsk, to whom he presented for the first time the demand that the lands occupied by the Russians up to that point should belong to them, and that the Russians be granted unhindered free navigation on the Amur. The Chinese plenipotentiaries declared that they were not authorized for such negotiations, and produced the well-known letter from the Foreign Ministry concerning the border settlement on the Gorbitsa. Muravyov evaded negotiations [[p198]] [Illustration: Khabarovsk: Monument to Count Muravyov-Amursky.]

on this with the phrase that the Chinese should inform their government of the Russian government's earnest desire to finally arrive at a settlement of its relations with China, and furthermore inform it of his intention to travel down the Amur again the following summer with a supply flotilla.

As a result of the abandonment of Petropavlovsk, whose population was settled along the lower reaches of the Amur, and through Muravyov's two transports down the Amur, a considerable number of settlements had already sprung up. The next transport of troops and settlers was, as mentioned, scheduled for the summer of 1856. In the meantime, however, Muravyov's presence in St. Petersburg had become necessary again, since the Chinese complained about the establishment of Russian settlements not only to the left but also to the right of the Amur (Mariinsk, De-Kastri Bay, etc.), and the Foreign Ministry, in blind incomprehension of Muravyov's plans, was on the verge of demanding from China merely the right of free navigation on the Amur and the construction of storehouses on both banks. On this journey, Muravyov now obtained full powers not only to negotiate the ominous border settlement with China, but to conclude an entirely new treaty, which would no longer settle an open-ended implementation clause of the Treaty of Nerchinsk, but create a completely new foundation.

Muravyov remained in Europe over the summer, while in his absence General Korsakov, in accordance with his instructions, organized and carried out a new—that is, the third—expedition down the Amur. Such expeditions now took place year after year.

In December 1856, the Governor-General departed again for Irkutsk. The government had given expression to the de facto annexation of the territories occupied and partly already settled by Nevelskoy and Muravyov by combining them, along with the entire coastal region up to and including Kamchatka, into a single province, the "Primorskaya." In that winter, work also began on building postal stations along the left bank of the Amur, and a postal service was established using sledges drawn partly by horses and partly by dogs.

In the meantime, events had once again occurred outside Russia that accelerated the realization of Muravyov's plans. It is one of the peculiarities of Russia's East Asian policy that it was able, with little effort, to derive advantages for itself from every major occurrence on the East Asian stage. France and England were endeavoring to conclude commercial treaties with China. For Russia, this was a welcome opportunity to reopen the treaty question with China on its own part as well. Consequently, Putyatin, who had previously concluded a commercial treaty with Japan, was dispatched to Beijing. Muravyov was initially by no means pleased by this, fearing that Putyatin might thwart his plans regarding the Amur question. However, during Putyatin's passage through Irkutsk—he intended to take the route via Mongolia—Muravyov was able to convince himself that, regarding the Amur question, he needed to rein in rather than spur on Putyatin's zeal. Putyatin encountered unforeseen difficulties. First, the Chinese did not permit him to travel through Mongolia, so that he found himself forced to travel down the Amur via Nikolayevsk and from there by sea to Tianjin, where, however, the mandarins again stalled him with useless discussions and barred him [[p200]] from traveling to Beijing, which was permitted only to envoys of foreign rulers who brought tribute. The Tsar's envoy therefore preferred to proceed to Shanghai and attempted to induce the Chinese to negotiate with him there—in vain, of course.
