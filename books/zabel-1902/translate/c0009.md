I open the carriage window and look out. An honest musketeer comes along carrying a large tub containing something steaming, and calls through the open window: [[p25]] “Want some tea?” “How much is it then?” comes the answer. “It doesn’t cost a thing!” “Well then, come right into the parlour!” The good-natured Holsteiner steps into the carriage with his tub, the mess-tin lids are taken off, and the ladling begins. Meanwhile, news is exchanged and politics debated to everyone’s heart’s content. Here along the railway line, the “ninth companies” are stationed on communications duty. The lads were the last to come out, and already they talk of nothing else than “going back home.”

[Illustration: Interior of one of the Beitang forts (p. 12).]

The signal for departure is given; the tea-man jumps with his tub from the train, which is already in motion again. The landscape becomes just as bleak as it was before, and weary of looking, I lean back into my corner. It is already late afternoon when we pull into the station at Tianjin. A great deal has changed there since my last stay. The station building has been completed again; back then the Russians held the railway, but now it is under German administration. And above all else: the coolies have become incredibly insolent. They jostle and shove around the traveler, they pounce on my luggage, which they want to haul over to the train for Tanggu, and one thrashes the other to snatch the earnings away from him. In all, I have six pieces of luggage, of [[p26]] which two weigh about a hundredweight each, while the others weigh only a few pounds. In the blink of an eye I have ten coolies around me; four each want to carry the large trunks, and of the other pieces each coolie takes one. The lightest weighs about a pound, but the fellow heaves at it as though it were a hundredweight load. Finally my boy, a burly, strong Shandong man, succeeds—with the help of liberally distributed slaps and raps on the head—in reducing the importunate crowd to four, who tie the luggage together and carry it off on their bamboo poles without great exertion.

If I do not wish to miss the connection to Shanhaiguan, I must travel on to Tanggu that very same day. A great difference is indeed to be noted between transport then and now. The Russians, who were running the railway at the time the East Asian Expeditionary Corps arrived, regularly took nearly three hours to cover the distance between Tianjin and Tanggu; now it takes barely an hour. In general, only the highest praise can be spoken of the German operation of the line. Our railway troops had tasks to accomplish here that were not only interesting, but also made great demands on the efficiency of the force. Unlike under the Russians, who took no money, the line is operated for fares. I shall carefully preserve the little handwritten and partly poorly printed tickets as interesting mementos.

In Tanggu I find more or less everything as it was. I put up at the so-called “Foreign Hotel,” which had already given me a hospitable welcome once before, after the storming of the Beitang forts. Now the “hotelier”—a new host, by the way, no longer the Armenian, but a Galician Jew—has fitted out a real guest room. This I move into. In the room there is also a bed, a real, proper bed! Since I first set foot off the *Rhein* at Taku, this is once again the first real bed I have slept in. For so long, a few woolen blankets, a Chinese sheepskin, and a well-heated *kang* were my only sleeping accoutrements. And I felt more comfortable with these than later in a real, soft bed.

During the campaign Tanggu achieved a certain fame, or rather notoriety, namely as the place where goods and property ceased to be safe. As is well known, all goods and provisions destined for the interior of Zhili province passed through Tanggu. As long as the Peiho or at least the Taku roadstead was free of ice, Tanggu was the only loading point. Since the roadstead froze over, it has at least been the transshipment point for goods arriving by rail from Shanhaiguan. The same applies to consignments from the interior destined for Europe. As a result, [[p27]] one finds quite considerable stocks of goods accumulated there from time to time. If one now compares the quantity of lost—that is to say, stolen—goods with the sum total of imported goods, a very considerable percentage of loss results. There are probably few military units that could not report stolen shipments, particularly of drinks. The main “hub” for these thefts, however, was Tanggu.
