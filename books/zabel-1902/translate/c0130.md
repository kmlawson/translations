### 4. On the Upper Amur.

Our steamer, the *Kharbin*, is lying right in front of the Hotel Rossiya. Not very many are left of the original traveling party. We divide the available cabin berths among ourselves. Ivan Ivanovich, being the wealthiest, wants to have a cabin all to himself

[Illustration: Settlement on the Shilka (p. 275).]

and his wife, and pays for three first-class tickets each. Mitya and his wife find the fare somewhat expensive. They therefore want to travel second class. In addition, there is the sister and an officer's wife traveling alone, who wants to take her two boys to school in Tomsk. They likewise travel second class because on a private steamer they do not enjoy the fare discount they receive on the mail steamer and on the railway. Consequently, Mitya must part from his little wife, which grieves them both greatly. The sister, the officer's wife with her youngest boy, and Mitya's wife move into “the” second-class cabin. Nikolai Mikhailovich and I occupy the first-class saloon. Mitya, wanting to economize, now even travels as a deck passenger and, through his wife's intercession, gets my long chair to sleep on at night. But since two sofa berths in the first-class saloon are still [[p268]] free, the officer's wife traveling alone decides to take a first-class ticket for her eldest son as well, who as a child pays only half fare, and for the fourth place we gain a captain of the Ussuri Cossacks whose destination is Albazin. So far, everything is quite nicely arranged. We go on board after lunch; the crew is still busily loading. The steamer's cargo consists for the most part of dried salmon, which, tied together in bundles without any further packaging, is stacked directly in the ship's

[Illustration: Rocky bluff with navigation mark on the Shilka (p. 277).]

hold and tickles the nose with an agreeable smell of grease. Little by little, the deck passengers also arrive. I must confess that when we see them advancing in droves, we feel somewhat anxious. How is the little ship to carry so many people, and above all, where are the poor people to find room to sleep, and what if it begins to rain? But these considerations seem matters of indifference to the child of Israel, for the rubles are none the worse if the passengers who pay them are packed onto the deck just like the salmon down in the ship's belly. To be sure, the steamer's certificate hangs in the saloon, stating precisely the number of passengers that must not [[p269]] be exceeded. But for a ruble, the inspecting gendarme overlooks many a thing, and for the rest, the Siberian proverb proves true: “Heaven is high, and the tsar is far away.”

The steamer is scheduled to depart at five o'clock; naturally it does not depart, for Herr Neumann is still having salmon packed into the hold, and this or that deck passenger still turns up. Soon even the little patch of deck intended for the cabin passengers is covered with people, and the only space where one still possesses some freedom of movement is our cabin, although freedom of movement here too is limited to sticking one's legs under the table or stretching out on one's sofa berth. But our saloon has one good feature: it is located right at the bow and has windows on three sides. So we shall have a good view.

Two weeks ago today we departed from Khabarovsk, and we can bear witness that we have not lost a single minute in pressing onward—at least not voluntarily!

August 5.

The *Kharbin* is supposed to depart at ten o'clock in the morning. I therefore make haste to purchase a goodly portion of *zakuska* at Kunst & Albers; I also arm myself, according to local custom, with a travel pillow, since of course on the *Kharbin*, too, any kind of bedding is out of the question. Nikolai Mikhailovich has undertaken to procure the beverages, Crimean wine and vodka. We will regularly take our meals together and share the expenses afterward.

[Illustration: Mail steamer on the Shilka (p. 277).]

Before ten o'clock I am back on board, but Herr Neumann is still not ready. Yet he swears by Mount Sinai that the steamer will leave at twelve o'clock.

At twelve o'clock the steamer naturally does not leave. Herr Neumann now declares he will hang himself from the mast if the steamer does not leave at two o'clock. Even my Russian fellow travelers lose patience and press the ship's owner hard.

At two o'clock the steamer still does not depart, nor do we see Herr Neumann hang himself from the mast, much as we wait in vain. With a resigned smile, we merely hear the assurance [[p271]] that the steamer will now definitely leave at four o'clock. I try a psychologically more effective form of pressure. Namely, I have not yet paid for my luggage freight, and I inform Herr Neumann that I will not pay it to him either if the steamer does not depart at four o'clock as agreed.
