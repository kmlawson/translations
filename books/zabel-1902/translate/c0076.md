He looks for a long time through his glass, I through mine, and truly, far off in the distance, something fish-like keeps leaping out of the water—a whole school, probably whales! But not wishing to make a fool of myself, I prefer to keep quiet about my observation, and in the end I am quite right to do so. For as we draw closer, it turns out to be a school of harmless dolphins. Strange—today I see whales teeming everywhere; every stick floating on the water I take at first for a whale. After all, this unsuccessful watching and peering has already been going on for hours. It is gradually beginning to feel wearisome.

[[p161]]

We head straight for Askold, an island with a lighthouse well known in these parts. The island actually consists of nothing more than a conical mountain upon which the lighthouse sits enthroned. Today a veil of clouds hangs about the peak of the mountain, but down below it is bright. This state of affairs is said to occur frequently. Now, there is many a wag among the captains who likes to tease the lighthouse keeper. As is well known, the lighthouse keeper must fire signal shots and sound the foghorn whenever there is fog and he hears a ship’s whistle. If the weather down below is completely clear while the lighthouse keeper sits in the fog, the jesting captain blows his steam whistle, and before long the foghorn begins to toot and the cannon to thunder. Then the captain toots again, and the reply follows thick and fast. The poor, teased lighthouse keeper toils away up there in faithful performance of his duty, while that rascal of a captain laughs up his sleeve. These are sailors’ pranks that happen often, and which are served up anew to every passenger who comes near Askold.

It is already one o’clock, and we still have not seen a single whale spout. The captain therefore suggests that we go below and have lunch—an appealing proposal in its own right. Only the gunner remains on the bridge. So we partake of our *zakuska* in the cabin and then fall upon the excellent cabbage soup. It tastes delicious, and I am just about to ladle myself a second plateful when... suddenly the engine stops. The captain dashes on deck, I after him—the gunner has sighted a whale! So after all! We can still see the water welling up at the spot where the whale has just dived into the deep. So we wait a while, and sure enough, after about a minute, it surfaces again some fifty meters from us. The tall dorsal fin slowly slices through the surface of the sea; at times the whale glides halfway out of the water in long, leaping motions, and once or twice it spouts a tall jet of water vertically into the air—its whistling audible even to us—the water it had taken into its enormous maw along with the fish. The ship’s engine is running again now; the senior sailor stands at the helm and calls his orders down to the engine room through a speaking tube, while the gunner stands ready to fire by his cannon. Our intention is to stalk as close to the fish as possible. But already, with a sudden upward flick of its fluke, it has plunged back into the deep to fill its mouth with fresh sustenance. The engine stops once more, and we wait for it to surface again. Surface it must after a certain time, for it is a mammal, breathes with lungs, and indeed not through gills, but like every other mammal through its pharynx. After a while it does indeed become visible once more. Again an order echoes down into the engine room, the engine runs, and we approach the whale; but before we are within firing range, down it goes into the deep once more.

Firing range is about five to ten fathoms, no more. Until one is within firing range of the whale, shooting makes no sense. Getting close—that is the art; and to get a sure shot, one must approach the whale from the flank. When whales appear in pods, this is generally not so difficult, because [[p162]] one always gets a shot at one whale or another. Here, however, we are dealing with a “loner” that goes its own way and is therefore naturally far more alert than whales in pods. Getting close to such a solitary whale usually requires endless patience, if it is possible at all.

One sees, then, that whale hunting can still prove a great test of patience and demand much composure and alertness even when one already believes one has the whale safely in one’s pocket—provided, that is, that one has pockets so large. In our case, we chase each other about for nearly two hours. Again and again the colossus surfaces from the depths, announced anew each time by a forceful command down to the engine room, but again and again it comes up at a spot that leaves an even greater distance between itself and the whaler than before. To cap it all off, it begins to rain as well, with the result that despite all our efforts this first hunt comes to naught. Once more the characteristic spout is seen; then the whale vanishes back into the deep and is seen no more.
