I have the choice of whether to travel on further to Yokohama on the *Bayern*, or directly to Vladivostok on a coastal steamer. My ticket is made out for Yokohama. My inquiries reveal, however, that a steamer leaves for Vladivostok as early as the following day, and that the next steamer, belonging to the Nippon Yusen Kaisha, will not depart until the second half of July. To be sure, if I chose this steamer, I would get to know several Korean ports, but then the stay in Japan, which would be of little benefit for my purposes, would cost me more than two weeks, which I would rather not spend on it, because I want to be back home by the beginning of September at the latest.

Thus, at the Lloyd agency—which here, to be sure, goes under the name of the German-Japan firm Ahrend & Co., but is managed by the staff of the branch of the great Vladivostok house of Kunst & Albers—I purchase a ticket to Vladivostok for the German steamer *Daphne*. This belongs to the “Ostasiatische Handelsgesellschaft” [East Asian Trading Company], but has already been chartered for years by the firm of Kunst & Albers. The ticket costs forty yen. The yen is the Japanese circulating coin; it generally stands at par with the Mexican dollar. As is well known, Japan has a gold standard, and the yen is therefore properly speaking a token coin. However, the gold coin dates only from recent times, and since Japan previously had a silver currency, it comes about [[p121]] that the silver yen also represents roughly the true monetary value. Japan has had a peculiar experience with its types of money. When the gold standard was introduced, the gold coins were made with too high a fineness. In the period after the introduction of the gold coin, one received gold everywhere, but soon the banks systematically held back the gold coins and sold them to Europe at a good profit because of their high fineness. Thus it comes about that at present one can get hold of gold coins in Japan just as little as before the introduction of the gold standard. On the other hand, it is quite interesting how Japan manipulates the paper yen in Korea, and in fact an interesting economic struggle is taking place here between the paper yen and the paper ruble. As is well known, Korea is disputed territory between Japan and Russia. Through the skillful exploitation of Korea’s domestic political divisions, both powers have managed, with varying success, to secure their influence in the island empire.

Initially, Japanese influence was predominant, until the day of the assassination of the Korean empress—which to this day has not been fully cleared up—in consequence of which the emperor fled to the Russian legation. It is from that time that an agreement dates between Russia and Japan, according to which both states wish to assert their influence equally *). Naturally, this is only a truce, and it is probably beyond all doubt that the Korean question can be resolved in no other way than by force of arms. At least among the Russians, this view is universal. In the meantime, however, each is preparing the ground as well as possible. In this, money plays a very special role, specifically paper money. It is well known that the paper money issued by a state is nothing other than a promissory note for a specific sum, which under certain conditions must be paid in cash from the state treasury in gold, and in silver-standard countries in silver. The financial standing of a country naturally depends not on the quantity of paper money issued, but on the sum of gold or silver that guarantees the value of the paper. Indeed, the wealth of a country always consists solely in its reserve of monetary metal. A very simple example from India will suffice to make clear how a battle can now also be fought out with paper currency. India, as is well known, consists of a considerable number of independent principalities whose sovereigns administer their country autonomously, albeit under the control of an English resident minister. The danger that India, or at least one or another of these in part quite powerful princes, will one day revolt against English rule has by no means been banished. India is by no means a secure possession; a skillfully led uprising could overthrow the entire rule of England, whose chief weakness consists in the fact that the greater part of its military forces in India is recruited from Indian native tribes. Now, the Indian princes are for the most part very rich, and specifically in cash gold. England’s policy was therefore, and still is today, aimed at persuading the Indian princes either to deposit their money in English banks or at least to exchange it for English treasury notes. The result in both [[p122]] cases is the same. In the event of an uprising, England has the princes in its grip insofar as England is then in possession of what is truly the only valuable money, whereas the princes, with their bundles of English treasury notes, have little more in hand than a packet of paper. The more foreign paper money a country buys, therefore, the more dependent it becomes on the state whose paper it takes. In private commerce, this dependence then manifests itself, in the worst case, in the buyers losing their money—compare Portugal and Greece. In Korea, this principle is now being exploited politically just as in India; Russia as well as Japan takes care to withdraw as much cash money from the country as possible and to place its treasury notes there instead. If war should ever break out between Japan and Russia, the question of which of these two powers has the most paper money circulating in the country will also be of great importance for Korea’s stance in this conflict. Russia is pursuing precisely the same principle in Manchuria through the Russo-Chinese Bank, and its successes in this sphere are enormous. When I traveled through Manchuria, the Chinese—despite the uprising that was then still very much en vogue—were already taking Russian paper money with great preference, from Shanhaiguan on.
