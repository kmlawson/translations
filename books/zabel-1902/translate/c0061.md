## Chapter II. Zwing-Ost.

### 1. Life and Bustle on the Golden Horn.

In Vladivostok I have an esteemed and good acquaintance who occupies a "senior position" at the firm of Kunst & Albers, and of whom I think back only with feelings of the warmest gratitude, as it was his helpful care that not only saw me through the pitfalls of a first stay in a country whose language I do not speak, but whose helping hand I also experienced later on, all the way to Sretensk and beyond, with ever-renewed thanks. We became acquainted last year during our shared voyage on the *Hamburg*, and so my first visit in Vladivostok is to him. I find him in his office [[p131]] just as he is about to go for tea. The first piece of news I learn over the steaming samovar, to be sure, is not particularly encouraging. It ran, short and succinct: "The Amur is currently at its lowest water level, and things look even more bleak on the Shilka. Prepare yourself for the possibility that you may be stuck here or elsewhere for weeks, unable to move on, or else travel home via America." The latter is completely out of the question; I am firmly resolved to make the journey through Siberia under all circumstances, and so nothing remains for me but to fetch my things from aboard the *Daphne*, drive to a hotel, and quietly wait until the rain is kind enough to fall in the headwaters of the Shilka. As soon as that news arrives, I can then travel toward the water. Thus I must try—something I do not usually like to do—to kill time decently in Vladivostok.

Since January 1 of this year, Vladivostok has had an import tariff. To be sure, alcoholic beverages, foreign tobacco, etc., had already been subjected to a fairly substantial duty earlier, but the tariff was generalized according to the principles prevailing in European Russia only from the date mentioned. This innovation represents a considerable disadvantage for the provinces that must obtain their foreign goods via Vladivostok. Presumably, this tariff was introduced in order to favor from the outset the free port of Dalny, soon to be established on the Bay of Talienwan at the southern tip of Manchuria and near Port Arthur, where the actual terminus of the Trans-Siberian Railway will be. That Vladivostok is demoted by these measures to the simple capital of the "Primorskaya" (the province by the sea), by having the transit traffic of the Siberian Railway withdrawn from it, is obvious. This, of course, was not part of the plan from the beginning, and is by no means particularly agreeable to the large import houses in Vladivostok. But the Russian government does not concern itself with that, its measures here being determined exclusively by foreign-policy and military considerations.

Yet customs inspection seems to be handled just as laxly as passport inspection, at least when it comes to travelers' luggage. I have loaded my things onto a sampan manned by a greasy Chefoo Chinese as tall as a tree, and have myself rowed ashore. A building that looks like a custom house is nowhere to be seen upon landing; perhaps that large shed made of corrugated iron over there is the custom house. To be sure, the shed gives more the impression of a warehouse. My Chinese drops me off with my luggage at a meager landing stage, and I now await what is to come. I wait for the customs officials. True, I see several officials who might have belonged to customs loitering about; a few men in white blouses and official caps also approach me, but they do not say a word, merely looking at me. I look back at them. And when we have looked at each other for a while, they turn around and walk away. After a while, other officials arrive who again look like customs officials. To them [[p132]] too I must not be uninteresting, for they, too, look at the waiting traveler for a while. This time, however, I turned my back on them. At that, they go away as well. Finally the business becomes tedious to me, and so I hire a Chinese cart, which loads up my luggage and drives off. I take a seat in a droshky, a vehicle none too clean, but still tolerable. My *izvozchik*—that important Russian word, which I still remember from Port Arthur—wears a long, pleated coat of black velvet, a red sleeved waistcoat over it, and on his head a top hat of the well-known shape of Quaker hats. He speaks fluent Russian, nothing else, and on the instructions of my amiable acquaintance takes me to a hotel that bears the name *Sibirskoye Podvorye*, in German "Siberian Inn." My Chinese carter is directed to the same address as well, and then off we go up the hill. My little carriage has only two horses; it is an incomplete troika; to be a real troika, it must have three horses. The middle horse goes under a high wooden arch, trotting along, while the left horse gallops beside it; the third is missing. And so it goes, uphill, downhill, over potholes and ruts, always at the same wild pace. After a short [[p133]] time I land at the hotel, the *gostinitsa*, and ask the hotelier, a Jew who receives me in the jargon of Łódź ghetto German, for a room—the Russian says a "number." I get my number.
