7 July.

Vladivostok is a little over 40 years old and is today estimated to have 40,000 inhabitants. The city lies on a promontory that juts into what is known as Peter the Great Bay, also called Victoria Bay, and forks in the middle. The basin formed by this fork is the actual harbour of Vladivostok. The main part of the city lies on the western fork of the promontory and is bounded on the west by Amur Bay and on the east by the harbour basin. A chain of islands may be regarded as an extension of this part of the promontory, jutting even farther into the sea, heavily fortified, and bearing the [[p136]] outer forts of Vladivostok. Amur Bay is connected to the harbour basin by a canal navigable by the largest vessels, which cuts through one of the islands—presumably because the channel over the submarine ridge connecting the promontory with the offshore islands is not deep enough. It exists exclusively for the purposes of the navy; as is well known, Vladivostok remains even today, despite Port Arthur, officially Russia’s principal naval station in the Far East.

The harbour of Vladivostok is defended by strong forts that occupy the surrounding heights and make an approach from either the land or the seaward side extraordinarily difficult. In the south of the city, right in its midst, lies the main fort, which is the most heavily armed and whose guns carry clear over the surrounding mountains. The latest artillery practice against an invisible target located out at sea behind one of the mountains and determined solely by telephone is said to have yielded 80 percent hits.

The city of Vladivostok itself consists largely of wooden buildings that still retain the character of the Russian log house. Yet there are already a good many stately stone buildings that enliven the townscape considerably. Particularly striking are the post office, the Admiralty building, the governor’s residence with a pleasant park before it that overlooks the harbour, then the large building complex of the firm Kunst & Albers, and various other edifices that need not all be listed individually. The harbour scene is quite characteristically enlivened by the beautiful cathedral and by the tall triumphal arch standing by the harbour, dating from the time when the present Tsar, then the Tsarevich, made his journey through Siberia and laid the cornerstone of the Trans-Siberian Railway.

The great main street runs from Amur Bay at first in a straight line, but then, following the indentations of the harbour, skirts along it, leaving room between itself and the water for at most a single row of houses. Along this thoroughfare lie Vladivostok’s principal buildings. The other streets more or less all empty into this main artery, so that finding one’s bearings is relatively easy.

The pleasant impression that the city initially makes from the seaward side largely vanishes, to be sure, once one comes ashore. Only the main street is paved, and even then only for a very short stretch. Malicious tongues claim it was better before it was paved than after. I do not care. I only know that the paved streets, just like the unpaved ones, are either caked with clay muck or [[p137]] impassable with dust, depending on whether the heavens bestow rain or sunshine. There are no stone pavements, only wooden boardwalks. These would, after all, be quite passable. But, like Aslaksen, one must be familiar with local conditions; otherwise, it can easily happen that one strolls along the boardwalk in good faith and suddenly plunges through the timber via a rotted-out hole or, what is said to be even more dangerous, tumbles headlong into a ditch. For the boardwalks have often been used to cover makeshift drainage ditches, and the worthy road surveyor has sometimes thought nothing of simply letting the boardwalk come to an abrupt end whenever, in his foolishness, he deemed its continuation no longer necessary, without covering the gaping ditch beneath. One must be especially cautious in the dark.

Even when sitting in one's carriage, if one does not pay attention, one can suffer severe sprains. The streets, for which apparently nothing at all is done, mostly have deep holes, and since the izvozchik invariably tears across this vile surface like a madman, one is tossed about in the carriage as if upon a cannon galloping across ditches and ploughed fields. Down in front, up in back, into a hole on the left, into a hole on the right—that is how it goes through the streets of Vladivostok! I have always admired the skill with which the izvozchik balances on his high box, which has no backrest whatsoever, whereas I can at least brace my back against the seat and my legs against the carriage wall. To the municipal administration of Vladivostok, however, to my deep regret, I can award no other mark than a five, a flat five, without mercy!
