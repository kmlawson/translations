#### 10 July.

He who knows not patience knows what I suffer! Here I have already been sitting in Vladivostok for almost a week, fretting between two hopes that attract me equally. On the one hand, I longingly hope for news that the waters of the Amur are beginning to rise; on the other, I await no less longingly the news that the “Alexander” and Count Keyserling’s whalers have arrived from Nagasaki, to give me the opportunity to take part in a Siberian whale hunt for once. I divide my time between the office of Kunst & Albers, where news of the water level is to be had, and that of Count Keyserling, where shipping news can be obtained; I oscillate back and forth between these two poles, as it were, in swings six hours long. If the news that the water in the Amur is rising arrives first, I will let the whale hunt slide; but if the “Alexander” or one of the whalers arrives earlier, I will let the water level take care of itself and first go along to the whaling station for a few days . . . . Finally! The decision has been made; the [[p143]] “Alexander”, the company’s transport steamer, and one of the whalers arrived this morning, as did the expected schooner; this evening we go aboard the “Alexander”, and at the crack of dawn we put to sea for Gaidamak, the whaling station situated some 50 miles east of Vladivostok on the Siberian coast.

### 2. A Whale Hunt in Siberian Waters.

Aboard the “Daphne”, on which I arrived from Nagasaki, I made, as already mentioned, the acquaintance of Count Heinrich Keyserling, the director-general of a large Siberian fishing company that holds the monopoly on whaling along the Siberian coast. In addition to whaling, it will now also take up freshwater and marine fishing on a large scale, namely salmon fishing in the Amur and herring and sprat fishing along the coast of Sakhalin. The Keyserling family is originally German, that is, Imperial German—for ethnically German it remains to this day, having left its west German homeland over 100 years ago and found a new one in Courland. Besides Count Heinrich Keyserling, who not long ago brought home a charming wife from his Baltic homeland, one who was incidentally also educated in Germany, his brother, Count Maximilian Keyserling, is active as co-director of the company, specifically in charge of the whaling department, while the fishing department is headed by an ethnic Russian director. Count Heinrich, the elder brother, is the founder of systematic Russian whaling on the Norwegian model and of Russian deep-sea fishing in Siberian waters in general. For this reason I commemorate him with particular distinction, quite apart from a sense of gratitude, since he has given me the rare opportunity to attend an interesting hunt.

The fact that Siberia remains to this day practically unexplored and unknown country, despite the travelers who have traversed the vast continent along the well-known Trans-Siberian route, does not quite apply to the sea bordering the Siberian coast to the east—the Sea of Japan, the Strait of Tartary, and the Sea of Okhotsk. The broad back of the ocean naturally allows freer movement for the traffic of nations than does an inland territory; and while the acquisition of Siberia by the Russians all the way to Transbaikalia took place exclusively by land, the Amur region could only be gained through an intimate combination of advances on land and the annexation of the coastal territories from the sea, linked to the illustrious name of Nevelskoy. Even back then, the keels of Japanese vessels furrowed the waves of the northern Siberian seas, but even more so those of American whalers and seal hunters. Their quarry was the valuable bowhead whale living in the waters around Kamchatka, as well as an exceptionally valuable variety of seal occurring on only a few northern islands; the whaler here in [[p144]] these latitudes was the first and best connoisseur of the sea, the rocks, the currents, and the hunting opportunities, which he jealously guarded as a secret, and in which lay his key to success and wealth.
