### 2. Newchwang, the Natural Gateway to Manchuria.

February 16–20.

To assess the whole Manchurian question, it is necessary to examine the significance of Newchwang more closely. As is well known, a number of international treaty ports exist along the Chinese coast where Europeans are permitted to trade. In each of these ports, which are subject to specific regulations and rules established in the commercial treaties between China and the individual powers, there is a Chinese customs office that clears the various steamers in and out and levies the tariff-prescribed duties from exporters and importers. These customs offices constitute the only administration of maritime customs in China established on European models, which was created by and is subordinate to the world-renowned Sir Robert Hart.

The "treaty ports" existing along the Chinese coast and also in the interior along the larger rivers are, in a sense, the joint dominion of the international trading powers. In them, complete equality of rights prevails among the various nations; they are the consular seats of the foreign commercial states. The fact that in some of these ports individual nations have established settlements for themselves on specific territories—so-called settlements, such as we find in Shanghai, Tientsin, Hankow, etc.—does not alter the fact that everywhere and without exception the principle of the "open door" is maintained, and by no means merely with respect to trade, but also with respect to the administration of the settlements. For example, German merchants sit on the municipal councils of both the British and French settlements in Shanghai as voting members with full rights, and it would occur to no one to describe or recognize either of these settlements as British or French colonies. It is inherent in the historical development of these open commercial ports that they are entitled to maintenance and protection on the part of *all* nations whose unimpeded competition in the Chinese market is guaranteed by the commercial treaties with China and among one another.

If this or that port has special concerns or demands to present to the Chinese government, it proceeds independently, usually, of course, in agreement with and supported by or through the mediation of the local consular corps.

Now, as an international treaty port, Newchwang fundamentally stands in no different position than, for example, Shanghai. And yet it was quietly tolerated that since August 6, 1900, Russia has established the described government in Newchwang, to which it indeed appends the adjective "provisional," without, however, indicating what kind of government it intends to establish definitively in Newchwang once it abolishes the provisional one.

In circles that approve of Russia's actions in Manchuria, the example of Tientsin has been cited. It was said that it was wartime, and no one could find anything wrong if the state that assumed the protection of a threatened city also administered it during a turbulent period. But this overlooks the fact that the situation in Tientsin is, after all, fundamentally different. To be sure, a government was established in Tientsin, namely a provisional government, which will be followed again by a Chinese one after the conclusion of peace; but firstly, this administration extends only to the Chinese city, not to the foreign settlement of Tientsin, and secondly, it is composed of representatives of the powers involved. In Newchwang, however, only the Russian rules and governs, and indeed his authority extends not only to the Chinese city of Yingkou, but also to the foreign settlement of [[p68]] Newchwang. The protest of the predominantly British mercantile community of this place has remained unsuccessful.

Yet Newchwang is even more than an open port; it is the actual key to Manchuria. If we take up the map, we see how Manchuria, with the Liaodong Peninsula, thrusts its southernmost tip into the Gulf of Pechili. Now, it is a phenomenon known not only in East Asia that wholesale trade tends to follow the course of the great rivers—in China perhaps with the sole exception of the Yellow River, which, on account of its low water level, places too great obstacles in the way of navigation. In Manchuria we find the following waterways: to the east of the Liaodong Peninsula, the basin of the Yalu River, and to the west, that of the Liao River. The river basin of the Yalu is particularly noteworthy in that it forms the border between Korea and China; it is thus an area that is also of great political interest. In terms of commercial policy, however, the important circumstance is that this border region is virgin agricultural land. Until the second half of the past century, the land north of the Yalu was uncultivated. For political reasons, it lay fallow. Under the name of "No Man's Land" it may have been mentioned here and there, but rarely in connection with trade and commerce. In this so-called "no man's land," which was brought under cultivation only in the last fifty years, predominantly by immigrants from Shandong, there are still large forests with fine stands of timber; the settlements situated at the mouth of the Yalu are therefore important entrepôts and markets for timber, and while the forest wealth of northern Korea has already suffered considerably, No Man's Land still supplies significant quantities of timber. To be sure, with the predatory exploitation being carried on, there is an imminent danger that before long the mountain heights here will be just as bare as in the rest of China.
