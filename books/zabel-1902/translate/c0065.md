If one arrives in Vladivostok as an unknown German, legitimisation by language alone is generally sufficient to receive information and assistance from Kunst & Albers in the most courteous manner. If one comes with recommendations, however, or has personal ties to the firm, one travels through Siberia as if in the chariot of Helios. It [[p140]] is by no means an empty phrase when I frankly confess here that nowhere in the East, or indeed abroad at all, have I found assistance granted so kindly, so generously, and so effectively as on my journey through Siberia from the firm of Kunst & Albers and its branches on the way to Irkutsk.

Apart from the firm of Kunst & Albers, there are several other German houses in Vladivostok, though they by no means possess the universal importance of the former and likely concentrate even more on specialised lines of goods. Thus I might mention the large iron and steel firm Arthur Koppel,

[Illustration: Vladivostok: “The main street, the ‘Svetlanskaya’” (p. 119).]

which does a very substantial business, and the import firm Langelütge, likewise an enterprise that does credit to its fatherland. In general, one can observe that German businessmen stand in high favour with the Russians and certainly outdo their Russian competitors by far—if not in sheer numbers, then in understanding, conduct, and ultimately also in net profit owing to their meticulous bookkeeping. In Russian businesses the craziest things often occur, things one simply would not consider possible: no bookkeeping, fraud on the part of the staff, embezzlement, and much else besides. Nor are the Russian employees in the German firms valued and paid nearly as well as the German ones, [[p141]] precisely because salaries increase with performance and the Russians cannot match the Germans in this respect. A very interesting example of this is provided by the large Vladivostok match factory of Suvorov & Co. Matches are a sought-after commodity in Siberia, and Russian match manufacturing is completely protected against Japanese competition by a high import tariff. At the same time, Russian matches are not cheap. Nevertheless, this large factory had to go into liquidation, reportedly due to slipshod bookkeeping and excessive peculations by individual employees.

As regards the social circumstances of the numerous Germans living in Vladivostok, they associate almost exclusively among themselves. German and Russian notions of comfort and wellbeing are, after all, rather divergent concepts. The employees of Kunst & Albers in particular have their own club, their German choral society, and in addition several private circles. They generally have it quite good; free board and lodging alone cannot be prized too highly. Life in Vladivostok is quite extraordinarily expensive; one pays at least 50 per cent more for all the necessities of life than even in China and Japan, where living is hardly cheap either. The purchasing power of the rouble out here is considerably lower than that of the mark back home. It is therefore bad news for anyone who has marks and needs roubles. On the other hand, every saved rouble is worth two marks fifteen, and in the end it is no harder to save than a mark. For this reason alone, Vladivostok, unlike China or Japan, is still an Eldorado for the “young man.” If he renounces the cult of Aphrodite and Gambrinus, he can save the largest part of his salary, and there are many who arrived here with nothing and went home with quite a handsome sum. If he is capable and works hard, his prospects in the East are still great—greater, at any rate, than at home on 150 marks a month and nothing more. If I were asked, therefore, whether it is advisable for a young man to go to Vladivostok, I would, provided he meets the requirements briefly outlined, answer with a resounding “Yes.”

The pointless waiting is becoming ever more terrible. What is one to do? Merely to pass the time and avoid being reduced to making the rounds of the pubs, this evening I am attending a concert of Russian folk singers being held at the “Naval Club,” the so-called “Morskoe Sobranie.” I love Russian singers in a variety theatre, preferably with dancing and the balalaika, the triangular Russian zither, but listening to an entire concert becomes tedious in the long run. Always the same themes, the half-sentimental, half-wild songs; once, twice—charming! But for an entire evening—as boring as Vladivostok itself! Yet this concert seems to be the main attraction of the evening. The admiral with the two double eagles on his epaulettes is present with his family, as is the governor with his family, along with a great number of military officers and civil officials of colonel and general rank; in short, once again the entire hall is filled with uniforms and ladies’ gowns, once again the same enthusiasm after the songs, once again the same applause and cries of “bis,” [[p142]] just like in the theatre. One has to be Russian to join in with that. Still, I must give the troupe of singers their due: they are the best I have heard so far. A middle-aged lady in old Russian costume serves as conductor; the singers—she is the only lady, soprano and alto being children's voices—appear in boyar costume. The singing has been rehearsed with endless pains and labour into pianissimo and fortissimo and the entire scale in between, yet, to my mind, without really doing justice to the melodiousness of the songs. To be sure, the pianos and pianissimos in particular are of wonderful delicacy, but often a pianissimo follows upon a fortissimo or vice versa so abruptly that I cannot help feeling it is empty effect-seeking. During the intermissions, a warship's band plays.
