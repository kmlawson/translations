For a long time I hesitated over whether I should include a map with the book. As far as Siberia and the Trans-Siberian Railway are concerned, I refrained from doing so because these regions are by no means unknown with respect to the points that could have been marked on a small map, and the reader of the work surely possesses a desk atlas that is entirely sufficient for this purpose. The question proved more difficult when it came to the southern Manchurian regions. My own map sketches cover only a small area, and as a result I could only have provided my itinerary, which, however, would rarely have served the intended purpose for the reader. Consequently, I set out in search of an existing map, though without finding the one I was looking for. Moreover, it would have presented difficulties to reconcile the spelling of the names on the maps with the phonetic transcription of place names that I chose for my book. I am well aware that, with my transcription of proper names, I place myself in opposition to prevailing usage. However, I proceed from the viewpoint that a name on a map should primarily serve to convey to the traveller, as accurately as possible, the sound of the place name that is familiar on the spot to the native from whom the traveller inquires. My method was always this: I had a native on the spot pronounce a name for me until I was myself in a position to repeat it accurately, and indeed in such a way that the native had nothing left to correct in my pronunciation. Then I wrote the name down in our alphabet exactly as I heard it. I consider this method to be the correct one, and I can approve of the efforts—which, particularly with regard to China, aim at implementing as uniform a transcription of place names as possible, with postal considerations playing a major role—only if the chosen transcription corresponds to how the name is pronounced on the spot. In such a case, the disadvantages of schematisation are greater than the advantages brought by simplifying specifically European communication. Admittedly, I must concede that I am myself inconsistent with regard to this theory. A single individual simply cannot fight against entrenched errors, and the post office would probably [[p12]] not know where to send a letter destined for “Peking” or, worse yet, in French “Pékin”, if on the address I were to record the local pronunciation as “Baitsing”. It is very difficult to develop from this dilemma a compromise that does justice both to scholarship and to practical needs. Nevertheless, where special maps are concerned, I maintain first of all the standpoint that the pronunciation of the place name on the spot must be decisive for its transcription. For a special map serves primarily to orient a traveller, and his knowledge of route and region drawn from the map is at an end if, guided by it, he is unable, with the help of natives, to identify the local name and the name found on the map as the same geographical entity. Perhaps a compromise could be reached whereby maps would fundamentally adopt phonetic transcription according to local pronunciation and add in parentheses the erroneous, but unfortunately established, transcription. With regard to China in particular, the chapter of transcribing place names is especially disagreeable, and I have personal reason to raise the issue here. For in my search for a special map of the southern Manchurian regions through which I travelled, I also obtained the recently published “Mukden” sheet of the *Karte von Ost-China* [Map of East China], which is currently in course of publication by the Cartographical Department of the Royal Prussian Land Survey. I was very pleased when I learned of this new publication, because I hoped that order would at last be brought to the appalling chaos of our cartography of China, or at the very least that a uniform method would be attempted. My own judgment in this matter is admittedly based on a relatively small body of empirical experience—given the vast territorial expanse of China. Nevertheless, on the basis of my own travels and surveys, I am able in individual cases and along certain stretches to conduct precise spot checks. Insofar as these have been carried out for the sheets published thus far, “Mukden” and “Tsingtao”, I regret having to say that the disappointment I felt after examining these two sheets was complete. I will speak here only of the “Mukden” sheet, and indeed only of the area to which my personal and direct observations relate: with a few exceptions, I have been unable to reconcile any of the place names I recorded along the Jinzhou–Yingkou stretch with those marked on the map. Well, that is the general defect and grievance in the case of those Chinese maps that do not cover the linguistic area of the Mandarin dialect, or at least of the Peking dialect, which is very similar to it. For mostly—one might almost say regularly—Chinese cartographic material forms the basis of a European map of China. The cartographer usually follows the method—which I myself often employ for my private use—of having a Chinese *lettré* pronounce the Chinese characters appearing on the map, or, if he can read Chinese himself, pronouncing them to himself and then transcribing them phonetically or according to certain other principles. But if a European has mastered Chinese to such an extent that he can read a Chinese map, the rule can be established almost without exception that he has, at best, mastered only the two dialects mentioned above, which can almost be treated as a single dialect. The Chinese *lettré*, of course, likewise speaks the language of the educated, the Mandarin dialect, and thus it happens that on existing Chinese maps one finds almost without exception the [[p13]] transcription of the Chinese characters in the Mandarin dialect, regardless of whether it is understood in the regions in question at all or not. Now, to be sure, the Mandarin dialect is the language of scholars, and since only such a person can become an official, of officials as well; and there are of course people in all provinces who are capable of understanding a place name pronounced to them in the Mandarin dialect—although there are not precisely many of them. Yet however often I have made this experiment, both in northern Shandong and in the most diverse dialect regions of southern China, even the so-called educated Chinese person has only ever understood me once I showed him the character in question on the Chinese map. And the telling thing in this was always the observation that, when reading the character, the man initially never read it in the Mandarin dialect, but in the local dialect—proof that the pronunciation of the place name in the Mandarin dialect was not familiar to him at all—not even to him, the educated Chinese! Yet a map is surely nonsensical if, in order to understand it in the practical pursuit of its primary purpose—that is, on a journey—one must always keep in reserve, as a key, a Chinese scholar or official who converts the transcription in question (presuming it to be correct) into the original Chinese character, and only then pronounces this character once more in the local dialect, so that after this metamorphosis one finally knows where one is, or where one wants to go.*)
