Given the well-known tensions prevailing between Russia and Japan over Korea, it is perhaps not surprising that there has hitherto been a lack of people who have turned their eyes to these tracts of China. One therefore has all the more reason to draw attention to the Yalu; especially for us Germans, this region is gaining in importance the more we gain a foothold in Shandong, for it is, in a sense, a hinterland of Shandong. Not only did it draw and still draws its population from there, but as a producing region it is also otherwise in direct touch with Shandong, for example in terms of silk production. The trade of the Yalu region has hitherto been exclusively junk trade, but I am convinced that it would be well worth opening one of the existing timber markets at the mouth of the Yalu as an international commercial port. In any case, we Germans ought to keep our eyes open regarding these regions and not let ourselves be deterred by unfounded fears of getting caught between the millstones of Japan and Russia.

As long as we do not yet possess an open port on this river, we must also exclude its basin when speaking of the trade routes currently under consideration for Manchuria. The actual trade route through Manchuria passes via Yingkou. I have already mentioned that at Yingkou, the Chinese town where the international settlement of the same name—called "Newchwang" by the English after the city situated a few miles upstream [[p69]]—is located, the summer and winter trade routes from the north converge. "Newchwang" is one of the many ports in North China that are closed to navigation by ice throughout the winter. On the other hand, a characteristic feature of Manchuria is the enormous cart traffic in winter. I have never seen anything in any province of China that could even remotely compare with this traffic. In Tianzhuangtai, for instance, I came to an inn where the stabled mules and ponies numbered several thousand (!), and there were several such inns in that town. The trade of Newchwang is of a different nature in summer than

in winter; in winter, the Newchwang merchant looks more toward the interior of the country, whereas in the fine season he looks out to sea. In winter, the Manchurian farmer brings his produce—beans, bean cake and oil, kaoliang, etc.—on carts from the interior many hundreds of li to Yingkou. When navigation reopens, the foreign shipping companies have their hands full transporting all these staple goods to the south, but mostly to Japan. The realm of the Mikado is the chief customer for the beans and the products derived from them. In addition, there is a brisk trade in castor oil, sesame, silk, and skins or furs, as well as in maize and millet, the rice of the North Chinese.

Yet for imports as well, Newchwang is in a sense the sole mouth of Manchuria, although the trade in European goods is largely in Chinese [[p70]] hands, and for the most part in those of intelligent Cantonese. The import of so-called "piece goods"—that is, manufactured and fancy goods of all kinds—is very considerable, and Germany has a substantial share in it.

To illustrate this, here are a few more figures:

In the fiscal year 1899 (the year 1900 cannot be taken into account as a normal year owing to the unrest), the total value of Newchwang's trade amounted to the respectable sum of 48,357,623 Haikwan taels (approx. 150 million marks). Compared with the previous year, which stood at 32,141,000 taels, this represents an increase of 49 percent. If this latter figure is compared in turn with that of 1888, one finds that the trade of Newchwang grew by 236 percent in ten years—a proportion that stands unique in Chinese customs statistics. In particular, the value of goods imported from foreign countries increased three hundred and fifty-fold during these ten years. From this one can see that Newchwang is a port whose importance relative to the old and well-known commercial ports of China, especially Shanghai and Tientsin, is undergoing a very substantial relative increase. In 1899, the number of ships calling at Newchwang reached 1,161, and the tonnage of these vessels exceeded one million register tons for the first time. Regrettably, it must be stated that the number of German vessels has declined compared to earlier years, whereas Japanese shipping in particular has risen steadily and rapidly thanks to the efforts and endeavors of its government. It is of great interest for the commercial history of East Asia to point out this circumstance, and the brief span of three trade years provides an instructive illustration of the growth of the Japanese Empire's commercial relations.
