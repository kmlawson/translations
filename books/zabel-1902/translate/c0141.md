### 2. Lake Baikal.

21 August.

The high plateau along which the Trans-Baikal Railway runs reaches an elevation of up to 2,000 feet in the Khamar-Daban. The foothills of this mountain range crowd close to Lake Baikal and, together with the so-called Baikal Mountains, enclose the largest freshwater lake of the Old World. After the Great Lakes of North America and Victoria Nyanza in Africa, Lake Baikal is the largest freshwater lake anywhere. It is over 600 kilometres long, its width varies between 30 and 90 kilometres, and the surface area it covers amounts to 34,179 square kilometres. Numerous legends of the surrounding Buryats and Mongols have been spun around it. Its name "bai-kul" means in German "the rich lake", which refers to its abundance of fish. The Mongols call it the "holy lake", the "dalai-nor". In their legends, they place the home of their greatest hero, Genghis Khan, on one of the islands washed by its waves.

As early as three o'clock at night, the splashing roar of the surf, drowning out even the rolling of the wheels, wakes me from my sleep, which in anticipation had been merely a half-slumber. It is approaching four o'clock in the morning; we have left the Selenga Valley and have already been travelling for a considerable distance along the [[p303]] shore of the lake, which gives the impression of a real sea. Before long, we have arrived at the terminus of the Trans-Baikal Railway in Mysovaya. From what was once a wretched Cossack village, the construction of the railway has caused a large settlement to develop, which is only waiting to be elevated to the status of a town. Our train halts, the passengers gradually wake up, and a certain excitement soon becomes noticeable, if only because the customs frontier lies here. It is rather curious that the traveller, who already had to cross a customs frontier in Vladivostok, must undergo another inspection of his luggage here. But the customs frontier in Mysovaya—or, more correctly put, in Irkutsk—is maintained, partly because the old trade route from China through Mongolia joins here, and partly because goods imported overland from Manchuria into Russia without having passed the customs frontier in Vladivostok are also to be subjected to duty. Customs clearance proceeds without any particular difficulties. Because everything went so smoothly, I press a rouble into the porter's hand as a tip. He is not satisfied with it and demands three roubles. The impudent fellow holds out his open hand to me with the rouble coin. Since one rouble is too little for him, I take the coin back out of his hand, put it into my purse, and now he gets no tip at all. He is so taken aback by this somewhat unusual treatment that at first he stands there open-mouthed, and then slinks off completely crestfallen after I thunder a forceful "Pashol!" (march!) at him.

Once customs clearance is completed, we travel a short distance further to the landing place on the lakeshore. The train is pushed by the engine and halts beside the mighty wooden breakwater, which serves at the same time as a quay.

Lake Baikal is the only section where the railway is still interrupted. Originally, the intention had been to avoid the costly construction of a railway around the steep southern shore of the lake and to maintain the connection between the eastern and western shores by train ferry. Therefore, with great difficulty, an icebreaker built by Armstrong was transported in separate parts to Lake Baikal and assembled over on the other side at Listvyannichnaya. The icebreaker was fitted out in such a way that it can accommodate entire railway trains in its massive hull. Train ferry service was to be maintained by this steamer in summer and winter alike. Another smaller icebreaker, the *Angara*, was also built to assist it. Yet considerable difficulties soon stood in the way of the original plans. In winter, it proved extraordinarily difficult to keep a passage clear through the ice of the lake, which is up to eight feet thick; and in summer, the lake is violently churned up by the air currents that occur with the regularity and force of trade winds as a result of the continental location of Central Asia, so that loading the steamer, which rises quite high out of the water, with heavy railway trains at times appears quite hazardous—quite apart from the fact that the *Baikal* alone would not suffice for the traffic, and the entire operation would grind to a halt if it were to suffer any accidents. The icebreaker *Angara*, however, is not equipped to take on entire railway trains. Goods therefore had to be transshipped. Thus, the Committee of the Siberian Railway [[p304]] had to decide after all on the construction of the Circum-Baikal Railway. I read in a number of books that the construction of this railway is said to involve completely abnormal technical difficulties, and the Russian engineers told me the same. To me, however, it seems as though these accounts are greatly exaggerated and base the assessment of the difficulties solely on the views of the Russian engineers. But given the flat topography of Russia, Russian railway engineers are accustomed to building railways mostly only in level terrain, and therefore frequently perceive difficulties that the railway construction of more mountainous countries overcomes with ease. They have a veritable dread of deep cuttings and tunnel construction. Even to avoid small cuttings, they prefer to lay out sweeping curves. The best illustration of this fear of tunnels is provided by the railway across the Urals, which ascends and descends the entire ridge in sweeping turns and serpentines without deeper cuttings or any tunnel construction at all. It is this fear of tunnel building that gives rise to the rumour that the construction of the Circum-Baikal Railway is attended by such enormous difficulties.
