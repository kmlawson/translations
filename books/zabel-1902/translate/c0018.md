Once all the locomotives have been brought across the river, we travel about four kilometers further. The saloon car is left behind, and we board a freight car that has been makeshiftly fitted out as a workshop for the engine drivers and mechanics. Then the train comes to a halt, right on the open track. It is already afternoon, and any thought of moving on is out of the question. So I have to spend the night in the workshop car. Since it is dreadfully filthy, I have a couple of coolies give it a rough cleaning. Inside stands a small stove; coal is fetched from one of the engines, and soon a cheerful little fire is flickering, making the stay bearable. Captain Losev and the engineer travel back to Jinzhou Fu on one of the engines. Before doing so, however, having likewise tried in vain to dissuade me from my plan, the captain yielded to my urging and placed a draisine at my disposal, on which I will continue my journey tomorrow. It is a peculiar sort of draisine, for it lacks the pumping mechanism, so that it can only be pushed or pulled. Nevertheless, I am grateful, even though I can use the draisine for only a single day’s journey, because some thirty kilometers further on the railway line is completely destroyed.

I make myself as comfortable as possible in the workshop car. From the railway soldiers I borrow a mess kit, have a coolie fetch fresh water for me from the locomotive boiler, and start boiling water. Then I buy a loaf of Russian army bread from the soldiers—a thick, black, heavy bread, but one that tastes excellent. I sent the other coolie to the surrounding villages to procure eggs, salt, tea, Chinese pearl barley, and a chicken for me. After an hour he returns, and soon the water for tea is bubbling over the fire, in which, given the preciousness of fuel, the eggs are first soft-boiled. Meanwhile, my boy plucks the chicken that the coolie procured; it is, to be sure, already quite an old chicken, but two hours of boiling will at least yield a good soup. One by one, the engine drivers appear. They have put their boilers in order and extinguished the fires, and now they too want to make themselves comfortable in the car. It serves as their night quarters, and they do not mind in the least that I am to share it with them. One of them speaks German. And so we converse with one another. We share our provisions, as we later will our sleeping quarters. The men are cheerful, good-natured fellows who behave with utmost civility and do full credit to the reputation claimed by the Russian railway troops as an elite corps.
