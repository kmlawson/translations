To contemporaries, the surrender of the Amur region in the Treaty of Nerchinsk meant nothing more than the abandonment of a possession that was of no substantially greater importance to Russia than any other part of Siberia, and which, strictly speaking, had not even really been acquired yet. That changed when the growing importance of the settlements on the Sea of Okhotsk and the Bering Sea increased the need for a viable route connecting them with the west. For the difficult overland route, impassable altogether during the long winter, could no longer satisfy these needs in any way. Resuming the efforts to regain the Amur, at least as a supply route for the Russian possessions on the Pacific Ocean, thus became an *economic* necessity. The last futile attempt to obtain permission from China by amicable means to use the Amur as a waterway dates from 1806. Not until thirty years later had a sufficient number of diverse inducements accumulated to spur Russia to renewed energy. Apart from the purely economic cause already asserted, it was primarily the driving force of an impulsively inclined Governor-General, endowed with extraordinary gifts, that brought about the transformation. At the same time, the successful actions of France and Britain in China exposed the military weakness of the Middle Kingdom, which had been overestimated for centuries. This weakness was further aggravated by the Taiping Rebellion. Russia, moreover, had reason to fear that the European colonial powers, above all Britain, would demand territorial cessions from China that might deprive Russia of the fruits of centuries of labor; and an attack by an Anglo-French fleet on Russian positions on the Pacific coast during the Crimean War opened the eyes of Russian policy to its position in the Far East and impelled it to adopt a faster pace in its measures to secure its possessions there.

The history of the acquisition of this most valuable part of the Russian possessions in East Asia is closely linked with the name of a single man who inaugurated the modern era of Russian East Asian policy: Count Muravyov-Amursky. He had to fight on two fronts: against China and against the opponents of his policy in St. Petersburg. Foremost among the latter was the Minister of Foreign Affairs at the time, Nesselrode, to whom the Near East seemed a far more desirable objective—a multi-phased struggle that played out over the course of about a dozen years, full of intrigues! Its outcome was a brilliant victory for Muravyov and for Russia!

Even as Muravyov came onto the scene, bringing the possessions on the Sea of Okhotsk and the Bering Sea into closer connection with the west via a viable transport route was already recognized as an economic and political necessity, one that even Nesselrode, hostile as he was to all colonial policy, came to appreciate. This had to be done if only because the coast of Okhotsk, Kamchatka, and Alaska were being rendered unsafe by British whalers and American seal hunters. But the privileges granted to the Russian-American Company deserved that name only if Russia also undertook their defense against foreign encroachments. A decision was therefore made to dispatch [[p192]] warships to the Sea of Okhotsk; they were at the same time to attend to the provisioning of the remote settlements, the rotation of troops, and the conveyance of mail. A beginning was made with the newly built brig "Baikal," which departed under the command of Captain Nevelskoy. The crew of this first representative of Russian naval power in East Asian waters consisted of six officers and thirty men.
