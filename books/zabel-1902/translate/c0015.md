Perhaps I can wait there for some time. Any day now the "Apenrade," a German steamer, is supposed to arrive, which can take me to Chefoo, from where Port Arthur can be reached in ten hours by another steamer. With that I console myself. But it is as though everything has conspired against me; at the station I learn that the "Apenrade" has been shipwrecked and cannot make it up here at all. For me, however, that means: either turn back or travel directly overland from Shanhaiguan to Port Arthur! Since I am no friend of turning back, despite the obstacles standing in the way of the undertaking, I quickly decide to choose the overland route through Manchuria, and I immediately make my preparations for this expedition on my own initiative.

When I made the decision to embark on the journey through Manchuria, I was well aware of the difficulties and great dangers associated with such a venture in these warlike times. Nor do I by any means disregard the warnings conveyed to me, but my mind is made up and I stick to my resolution.

At all events, the Russians are also cautious enough not to let anyone easily look into their cards. After all, they have succeeded so far in preventing unwelcome news about their actions in Manchuria from reaching the public. If I therefore undertake to lift somewhat the veil that lies over these events, this likewise requires great caution.

In retrospect, I am glad that I took my route overland. For had I carried out my original plan—that is, had I traveled to Port Arthur in order to obtain permission from the competent authorities there to travel in Manchuria—I would never have reached my goal. Such permission would have been absolutely necessary, firstly, in order to be able to travel on the railway, which served exclusively military purposes [[p36]], secondly, to secure myself against being regarded as a spy anywhere and arrested, but thirdly also because, for the sake of personal safety, I could by no means dispense entirely with the protection of the Russian troops. As an example of Russia's attitude toward the Manchurian question, the following fact may serve: The German Major Borkenhagen was entertaining a plan to travel a bit further into Manchuria on the railway, as far as that was possible. He spoke about this with the Russian station officer on duty in Shanhaiguan, but soon abandoned his plan of his own accord. Immediately after this conversation, the Russian declared to the German station officer that it would have been quite impossible for him to let the major travel. Yet he had not wanted to tell him directly that travel was not permitted for foreign officers. The brief debate that followed did nothing to change the fact that Russia exercises its sovereign rights of occupation over Manchuria and the railway beyond Shanhaiguan—which, moreover, still runs a considerable distance through the province of Zhili—in such a manner that it will not permit any German officer to travel on this railway*). In Port Arthur, the chief of police had a mutual acquaintance suggest to an English officer who spent his free time taking walks whether he might not prefer to depart from Port Arthur. Well, in Port Arthur the Russian does, after all, have a right to get rid of individuals inconvenient to him. But the case just mentioned proves that he is already claiming this right for the whole of Manchuria and part of Zhili as well, even toward German officers, and while the joint operations of the powers in China are still ongoing. Light is also shed on Russia's stance on the Manchurian question by the fact that Russia has withdrawn foreign-service and wartime pay from its troops ever since they have been operating only in Manchuria. Russia thus no longer considers Manchuria to be foreign territory.

Toward me as a civilian, the Russian station officer has fewer objections of principle. This may perhaps be due to the fact that I have already dined and clinked glasses with him. Such things sometimes help overcome such scruples. But in return he is the most zealous warner and alarmist, whereas the German station officer, Lieutenant Heinrich, while drawing my attention to the menacing bands of brigands and other perils, does so less to dissuade me from my plan than to ensure I do not underestimate the dangers. In the end, I succeed in obtaining the Russian's consent for my onward journey. He even gives me a letter of recommendation to another officer who manages the station at Tchentcheusu, probably thinking all the while that he will see me again in a few days.
