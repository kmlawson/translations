We approach the Gaidamak whaling station at about ten o’clock in the morning. The station, which possesses everything necessary for the immediate processing of the whales brought in by the whalers, lies on a wonderful, deep bay. The harbour proper, however, is a very small bay that joins the end of the larger one almost at a right angle, like a wart on a nose. Both the larger and the smaller bay are surrounded by cliffs and hills respectively, so that by their natural situation alone they are sheltered from all storms. The harbour of Gaidamak is one of the finest natural harbours I have ever seen. Indeed, even at the pier, which extends only about six metres out into the sea, the small bay still has such depth that vessels of up to 3,000 tons can berth there. Along these two bays now stand the various buildings of the Gaidamak settlement, which serve exclusively for the harvesting and processing of whales. Naturally, all sorts of tales and talk are exchanged along the way, and it is especially at breakfast that the most important questions of fisheries and whaling are discussed. After all, we have plenty of experts on board who can speak and tell of such matters. First and foremost, there is the director of the whaling department, Count Maximilian Keyserling. He studied agriculture in Halle an der Saale, whereas the memories of my student years gravitate towards Leipzig. Common ground is quickly established, introduced first by the observation—still moving on neutral territory—that Döllnitz Gose tastes no worse in Leipzig than in Halle, though not without an “umbrella”. Then one moves on to more serious questions of science, ultimately to land on fisheries. Furthermore, we have the actual director of fisheries on board, who speaks only Russian, yet is hardly inferior to the Count in courtesy, though naturally this expresses itself only in pantomime. An important personality at this breakfast table—where one begins with zakuska alongside vodka and finishes with a hearty beefsteak—is also the captain of the “Alexander”, a native of Riga, possessing all the unfathomable humour, zest for life, and amiability characteristic of the Baltic German. He formerly sailed his own vessel as his own captain, but sold it and preferred now to bless the “Alexander”, of which he thinks very highly, through the winter in Nagasaki with some new invention of his for the most practical utilization of the cabin’s confined space, and in summer to cruise between Vladivostok, Gaidamak, and Sakhalin. He has now also been in the company’s service for years, has carried many a barrel of train oil to Vladivostok, many a cargo of dried whale meat to Japan, and thus forms, in a manner of speaking, the link between the inhabitants of Gaidamak and the outside world. He is therefore thoroughly versed in the whole business. To this is added myself, who generally remain receptive and perhaps make the impression of a sage, because I maintain constant silence during discussions of fishery matters. For my knowledge of fishing actually rests—aside from the fact that together with my brother I have sometimes pursued trout along the clear streams of the Eifel—solely on being on familiar terms with the secretary-general of the German Fisheries Association, and his name, to top it all off, happens to be Fischer.

The place where the whales stay changes with the season. In spring and early summer they are mostly in higher latitudes. Towards the beginning to the middle of July, however, they move further south, and in winter they even withdraw as far as the southern tip of Korea. In the harbour of Vladivostok, too, years ago, a whale—admittedly one probably separated from the herd, a so-called “straggler”—was shot. The migration of whales depends on definite rules, which basically all come down to where the animals find the most food. One can well imagine that this enormous mammal also

requires enormous quantities of food for its sustenance; its food consists of fish, and only very small fish at that. For the whale possesses only a very narrow gullet, the opening of which stands in quite disproportionate relation to the animal’s other dimensions. Through this gullet, however, no fish can pass that are thicker than, say, a child’s clenched fist. Since the whale also has no chewing apparatus in its enormous maw, but only a kind of grate of long, fin-like tooth formations, “baleen”, hanging down from the upper jaw, it must swallow the fish whole, and can therefore make use only of fish roughly the size of a herring. The whale occurring here is not the so-called bowhead whale, [[p153]] as is also found off the coasts of Alaska and Kamchatka, but a smaller variety. The so-called “whalebone” of the bowhead whale is roughly twice to three times as long as that of the whale caught here, and far more valuable, since, on account of its delicate texture, it is used to strengthen thread in Japan’s silk industry and fetches a high price, whereas the other whalebone can be used exclusively for the manufacture of corset stays and a few other articles.
