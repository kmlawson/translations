My *gostinitsa* is the exact opposite of the Nagasaki Hotel, or the Astor House in Shanghai, or the Hôtel de l’Europe in Singapore, or the Galle Face Hotel in Ceylon. There, after all, one has one’s bathroom, one’s washing facilities, one’s bedding, one’s towel, one’s pillow, one’s light. Here one has none of that. Except for the washbasin, one must bring all of this along oneself if one wants to have it and not pay dearly for it. Fresh water for washing is indeed provided, but so little that one may well assume with good reason that fresh water is an expensive commodity here. There is no bath in the hotel, but there is a bathhouse nearby. A bath there costs one rouble and 80 kopecks, almost four marks—fresh proof of my theory about the dearness of water in Vladivostok. I want a pillow. I get it after a long wait, and find ten kopecks a day charged for it on the bill. I want bed linen. I get it, that is to say a pillowcase and a single bedsheet. On the bill it stands at 20 kopecks a day. Thank God I remember that I have a few towels lying deep in my trunk, otherwise another ten kopecks. For each light I am charged 20 kopecks a day. And yet the room costs two roubles a day. These unaccustomed miseries run, with few exceptions, through the whole of Siberia, and there are only a few hotels that cater directly to foreign traffic and are fitted out accordingly. The Russian, however, always travels with his own bedding, his towels, his soap, his pillows, and his blankets. For he finds nothing more than a bedstead and a crude mattress in his “number”—a fine number, that!

Vladivostok also has a larger hotel, but its prices are correspondingly high. Now, I have no desire to pay 10–15 roubles a day for a room, meals excluded of course, and there is yet another reason why I do not move into the “Pacific Hotel”. Namely, an operetta troupe plays there from 9 to 12, and from midnight to 2 a.m. a tingeltangel. From 2 a.m. until dawn the *chambres séparées* resound with bacchanalia, and there is therefore every likelihood that one will not close an eye all night. On the first evening I spend in Vladivostok, my acquaintance takes me to the theatre in the Pacific Hotel. They are performing *Mam’zelle Nitouche*, the well-known French operetta. In part it is acted very nicely, and sung too; of course, a large part of the enjoyment is lost on me because I do not understand the Russian text. The theatre itself is small, but intimately built, though quite plain. On the left is the governor’s box, on the right the admiral’s; they rarely remain empty. One sees a great deal of interesting women’s faces and elegant gowns, but even more uniforms. In Russia every official wears a uniform corresponding to his rank. Just as there are military ranks, so there are also civil ranks that rank equally with the military ones. The [[p134]] [Illustration: Vladivostok: View over the City (p. 118).]

[[p135]]

gymnasium director, for example, has the rank of major general, the doctor the rank of second lieutenant, etc. In the end, everyone seems to have some bit of rank. During the intermissions, the smoking and drinking male contingent gathers in the hotel’s large dining hall, which serves as a foyer; others let themselves be caressed by the ladies in the hallways; still others stand dully against a wall somewhere with a cigarette butt, sentimentally staring holes into the air. During the performance itself, however, the audience is attentive and, whenever anything pleasant happens on stage, enthused to a degree that must be a real pleasure for the actors. Then the whole fiery Slavic nature of the Russian breaks forth; people shout “Bravo!”, stamp their feet on the ground, clap their hands, and shout “Bis!” (where we say “Da capo!”), and once the passage is repeated again, the very same jubilation starts up all over again. In Port Arthur I attended a farewell performance in which the departing theatre director was fairly showered with silver and gold dedicatory gifts, and credible witnesses assured me that he had not tossed these over the footlights to himself, but that they were truly gifts from theatre enthusiasts. The admission prices, by the way, are not inconsiderable. Our parquet seat costs 1½ roubles, about ten marks.

6 July.

The Courland count with whom I traveled on the *Daphne* has invited me to take part in a whale hunt that is to take place in the next few days. Count Heinrich Keyserling is director of a large Siberian fishing company specifically devoted to whaling. He is expecting three of his ships from Nagasaki. As soon as these have arrived, the hunt is to begin. That would be a very pleasant diversion during the waiting period! I therefore accept with warm thanks, and only with the reservation that I might not participate if, before the ships arrive, word should come that the waters of the Shilka are beginning to rise. I use the day partly to take a closer look at the city, partly to work. Outside it is terribly dusty. I therefore remain in my “number” for almost the entire afternoon and attend to some work that has fallen into arrears. In the evening I drink two bottles of beer in the Pacific Hotel, one bottle of Russian and one “Löwenbräu”. Total price: two roubles! Here, too, people prefer to take money from the living, because there is nothing more to be gotten from the dead.
