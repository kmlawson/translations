## Chapter II. In the Land of the Honghuzi.

### 1. Beyond the Great Wall.

I feel like a smuggler as I find myself on the Russian train on 31 January, heading north. The train departs Shanhaiguan around 5 o'clock in the evening. I am assigned a compartment that has been reserved for me by order of the stationmaster; inside, the temperature is at least 5 degrees below zero. The carriages are unheated, except for a single one that contains a small stove. The Russian railway soldiers traveling on the train huddle around the stove, but it is impossible for an ordinary mortal to stay in that carriage because the stove smokes terribly. I therefore return to my compartment, wrap myself from head to toe in my furs and woolen blankets, and try to picture the night ahead of me in a somewhat rosy light and imagine that I am not freezing. That helps a little.

Just past the station, we pass through an opening. A breach had to be made in the Great Wall, which begins here near Shanhaiguan, in order to allow the railway line to pass through. This place is, in a sense, the gateway to Manchuria, although the political border of the province of Zhili extends even further to the north. Shanhaiguan [[p38]] forms a highly important point altogether. Here, the mountains that stretch from the interior in a north-easterly direction press close to the sea and form the only place where one can reach China proper from the north without having to cross the mountain range. The pass is quite narrow, only a few kilometers wide, and is blocked by the Great Wall as well as the aforementioned forts. In addition, here lie the only two ports, Shanhaiguan and Qinhuangdao, which in wintertime permit traffic between Zhili together with its hinterland, Manchuria, and Mongolia on the one hand, and the sea on the other. A military position, therefore, that can occasionally attain great significance!

We may have been traveling for a good half hour or so—in the meantime it has grown dark—when the train comes to a halt on the open track. The engine has broken down and must be repaired. This takes a full four hours. The day before, a train had derailed on the line.

At the stations we have stops, often an hour long. Strikingly large is the number of Russian-speaking Chinese. The Chinese understands very easily how to adapt to a new master and turn him to his advantage. In the carriage, I converse for some time with an intelligent Cantonese who understands English. He speaks very ill of the Russians; they rob and steal wherever they can find anything. He runs a wholesale business in Jinzhoufu, but since the Russians have been there, he no longer feels comfortable and wants to return to the south.

Regular railway service is maintained only as far as the station of Jinzhoufu; a train runs there daily. The line is not long, yet it took until around 5 o'clock in the morning before we arrived at the said station. With the help of my trunks, I have made myself a makeshift bed in the crowded carriage and remain on the train even after arrival until daybreak.

1 February.

We are now inside Manchuria. One can see already from the construction of the houses that one is dealing with a different tribe here than further south. The pointed, sweeping roof that characterizes houses in China proper has here given way to a flat, barrel-vault-like one. From a distance, the Manchurian houses look like railway cars. Given the great scarcity of wood prevailing here, they are built almost entirely of stone, and indeed not bricks, but hewn fieldstones, which gives Manchurian domestic architecture something very solid. Only the roof is supported by timberwork and consists of a thick layer of reeds, upon which is applied an equally thick layer of greasy loess soil, bound by chopped straw and a little lime. Inside, the house resembles the Chinese type.

When I awake from a brief, restless slumber around 6 o'clock in the morning, I find myself completely alone with my Chinese servant in the cold, dirty railway carriage. I pack up my things and have a couple of coolies summoned, who are to be found everywhere in large numbers in China and are always ready for work. They have to take my luggage out of the carriage and set it down in front of the station building. What to do now? I know that railway service ends here.
