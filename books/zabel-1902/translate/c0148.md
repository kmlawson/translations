### 3. Ural-Volga-Moscow-Petersburg-Berlin.

August 29 to September 1.

An interesting journey still lies ahead of us. Since early morning we have entered the territory of the Ural Mountains. In mighty curves the railway winds up the gentle heights, which are covered with numerous woodlands shading from the lightest to the darkest green and lush meadow slopes. Interesting rock formations alternate

[Illustration: Interior of the Kremlin (p. 311).]

with gorges and gentle valleys through which little brooks flow. The landscape gives an impression somewhat like that of the Thuringian Forest. The Urals are rich in minerals. Gold, precious stones, and above all platinum and iron are extracted here. We reach the crest of the mountain range before noon. At the end, the railway line winds up to the heights in what one might almost call corkscrew turns. At one point one can see the track twice more below on the mountainside. The descent on the other side is somewhat steeper, and at a pace that is positively breakneck for Russian conditions we speed down to Zlatoust, [[p326]] one of the centers of the Ural iron industry, where bayonets and sidearms are manufactured for the Russian army. The town itself lies picturesquely by a mountain lake, whose outflow already belongs to the Volga basin. In Zlatoust decorative cast-iron objects are also manufactured. Russian models depicting troikas, Cossacks on horseback, sled teams, and the like alternate with Western European forms. Behind Zlatoust, the European slope of the Urals merges into the plain almost more gently than on the Asian side. The train now travels considerably faster than before; after all, broader rails are laid here. The locomotives are very powerfully built and are fired no longer with wood, but with naphtha. Towards evening we have taken our leave of the mountain range bordering Asia and Europe; only in shadowy outlines do we still see the low ridges in the distance, and soon we reach the tributary of the Volga, the Belaya, which we cross on an imposing bridge near Ufa. Now we enter the Samara plain. We travel through the cultivated steppe; Russian villages and Tatar ones, recognizable by the domes of their mosques, pass by us.

At 8 o'clock on the morning of August 30 we have reached the city of Samara and with it the broad course of the Volga. The city rises picturesquely on the bank of this mighty river. It is the first major city of European Russia that we reach. Yet we continue to travel along the left bank of the Volga for quite some distance, and only after about two hours do we cross it on an enormous bridge, the longest we pass on the line, indeed one of the longest bridges in the world! As we travel across the river, we see beneath us steamers and barges plying this main waterway of European Russia. From the Volga also comes the magnificent Astrakhan caviar. Yet one hears general complaints that the abundance of sturgeon in the Volga has declined quite alarmingly. The die-off of sturgeon is attributed primarily to the discharge from the naphtha-firing used by the Volga steamers. Back on the Amur, I had purchased a large portion of caviar, for which I paid about one ruble a pound. This caviar was actually intended to be taken home; but, as things go, I ate it on the way with Nikolai Mikhailovich. Now throughout the whole journey I had consoled myself with the prospect of caviar on the Volga. Yet when I ask the price at a stall at the station in Samara, they demand 2½ rubles a pound from me. I still have my prices from the Amur in mind, and consequently the price strikes me as far too high, even though that is not the case at all. I therefore refrain from buying any. In Moscow a pound already costs four, and in Petersburg as much as six rubles.

The journey beyond the Volga continues through steppe country. Here lie the great latifundia in which the wealth of the old Russian families consists. The train speeds past towns and villages from which white church towers gleam. Our pious Russian fellow travelers never fail to cross themselves when passing a church tower. The evening of August 31 brings us to Moscow. Nikolai Mikhailovich travels on to St. Petersburg this very day, and the rest of our traveling party also scatters in all directions. The morning of September 1 shows me Moscow in all its [[p327]] [Illustration: The famous basilica in Moscow (p. 311).]

[[p328]]

splendor. I can imagine no finer conclusion to my Siberian-Russian impressions than standing on the terrace of the Kremlin, the national sanctuary of the Muscovite empire, and letting my gaze sweep from there over the countless blue and gold domes and church towers of the city, over the colorful houses through which the blue water of the Moskva winds its way. Half Oriental, half European, the ancient capital of the Russian Empire lies at my feet, and the impression is so overwhelming and imprints itself so firmly on my memory that I could almost draw the picture from my head. Yet the impression is pushed aside by a longing that now culminates in only one wish—to finally be home again. That very evening, on September 1, I travel onward to St. Petersburg, where I arrive the following morning. Sorting out my passport still causes some difficulties. I had neglected to have my departure from Vladivostok officially certified. But a three-ruble tip, which the hotel commissionaire shares with the police officer, works wonders, and so by noon on September 3 I am already able to travel onward by express train from St. Petersburg to Eydtkuhnen. After a thirty-two-hour journey, I am back in the capital of the German Empire.
