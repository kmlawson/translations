How seriously the two large German shipping companies intend to approach the cultivation of the East Asian field is also demonstrated by the fact that the two lines have jointly brought under their influence the *Ostasiatischer Lloyd* [East Asian Lloyd], which appears weekly in Shanghai and is the only German organ in the Far East—for the *Deutsch-Asiatische Warte* [German-Asian Watch] published in Tsingtao is now merely a subsidiary of the *Ostasiatischer Lloyd*.

Nagasaki, July 1.

The sailing time between Shanghai and Nagasaki is 36 hours; the *Bayern* adheres to it almost to the minute. We departed from Wusong around 10 o'clock on Saturday evening, and today, Monday, a few minutes before 10 o'clock in the morning, the steamer casts anchor in Nagasaki harbor. Punctuality is the greatest virtue of our German mail steamers. When I think of my voyage from Hong Kong to Shanghai on the English mail steamer *Sunda* (P. and O.), I appreciate this virtue all the more. The departure time in Hong Kong was initially set for 4 o'clock in the afternoon; all passengers are on board, awaiting the signal of the steam whistle. Then, a quarter of an hour before 4 o'clock, the chief steward arrives and announces that the steamer will not sail until 4 o'clock in the morning. After three days we arrived at the mouth of the Yangtze. That was at 6 o'clock in the afternoon. Had we not lost twelve hours in [[p116]] Hong Kong, we would have been able to proceed into Wusong and Shanghai that very day. As it was, however, we could not head for Wusong until the following morning. And so we did sail a short way at daybreak, but then the first-class English mail steamer managed to idle about on the Yangtze until 5 o'clock in the afternoon because its agency had not procured a pilot in good time. And this despite the fact that the agency had known for nearly twelve hours, by telegram from the Gützlaff lighthouse, that the steamer had arrived. That was indeed a bit too much even for the Englishmen on board, and they declared that they intended to publish the fact in one of the "Shanghai Newspapers." Later, however, I looked in vain through all the "Shanghai Newspapers" for any notice of the incident. The Englishman is simply too well schooled in patriotism to trumpet abroad things so detrimental to his fatherland.

Nagasaki is new to me. On a trip to Japan that I took a year and a half ago, I did not touch at this important place, the Japanese Hamburg. The harbor itself is wonderfully sheltered; it is a basin neither too large nor too small which, enclosed on all sides by hills, affords secure protection against wind and weather. The harbor is a naval port, and the hills enclosing it are heavily and skillfully fortified.

Nagasaki is, as mentioned, the Japanese Hamburg—at least, so it presents itself to the observer privileged to stay there for only a short time. It is, as it were, the junction of the shipping lines connecting Asia and Europe with Japan, and across the Pacific Ocean with America. To be sure, Russia carries by far the largest transport of goods and passengers from "Russia in Europe" to "Russia in East Asia" by means of the steamers of the Russian so-called Volunteer Fleet. These are indeed private steamers, but they are all constructed in such a way that they can be used as auxiliary cruisers, and they maintain close ties with the government in other respects as well. This is evident if only from the fact that the company is obliged to employ a certain number of Russian naval pensioners as officials. But this traffic hardly enters into consideration for communication between southern and northern East Asia, since the steamers of the Volunteer Fleet, on their way from Odessa to Vladivostok, call almost without exception at no East Asian ports other than Singapore, and then proceed immediately to Port Arthur. The steamers require an average of two months for the entire voyage, and are therefore virtually unused except by Russians.

A large share of the communication within East Asia, and especially with the north, is handled by the great Japanese mail shipping line, the Nippon Yūsen Kaisha—parenthetically, the most heavily state-subsidized line in the world in relative terms. It maintains the principal traffic between Japan on the one hand and Shanghai, Chefoo, Port Arthur, Newchwang, the Korean ports, and Vladivostok on the other. For all these lines, Nagasaki is the true terminal port. Nor must Manila be forgotten in this enumeration, communicating no less with Nagasaki than with Hong Kong.
