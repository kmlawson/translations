In the nature of things, this method of settlement was still easiest to carry out in the more densely populated regions of Western Siberia. The homogeneity of these territories with the mother country, which also shows only a few insignificant differences of degree in soil conditions and climate, contributes to the fact that the provinces of Western and Eastern Siberia form, as it were, merely the extension of European Russia thrust into the Asian continent. The communal settlements with poll tax and repartition encountered difficulties the further east they advanced. In the less settled Eastern Siberia and Transbaikalia, they met with the so-called *zaimka* system. This consists in the settler occupying land in the vicinity of his homestead on a fairly large scale and bringing it under cultivation in rotation, observing fairly long periods of fallow. The effect of the communal settlement that followed later was frequently that *zaimka* owners whose land was incorporated into the newly settled communes abandoned their *zaimkas* and established new settlements somewhere at a measured distance from the commune. In the Amur region, on the other hand, and in part of Transbaikalia, the *volnaya* system is predominant. It consists essentially in each peasant holding firm possession only of the land he has under cultivation, while the grass belongs to whoever mows it. As soon as the population becomes denser, this manifests itself in a shortening of the fallow periods and in a regulation of the use of meadows. While west of the Yablonovy Range, in the cultivable parts of Siberia, identical agricultural conditions and the same communal organization and distribution of property prevail as in European Russia, in the Amur Basin a more liberal conception of ownership predominates. Legally, too, this fact finds expression in that the peasant in the Amur region can acquire land as private property, whereas elsewhere it is absorbed into communal ownership. Added to this is the fact that the different climatic conditions of the Amur region—above all the humidity prevailing in summer and the lack of land that, as in the steppe regions, can be put to the plow almost without preparation—entail a departure from the old familiar working methods of the homeland. In the Amur country, only about five months of the year come into consideration for agriculture. These five months, however, display conditions of an almost subtropical character. Thus, while the severe, snow-poor winter makes the sowing of winter grain an impossibility, of the customary field crops only spring grain remains for Russian agriculture. The cultivation of crops that would adapt to the subtropical climate of the summer months is unknown to the Russian peasant. Furthermore, these crops, just like the moist soil that is harder to work, demand an intensive farming method, almost market gardening, which the Russian, however, does not adopt despite the Chinese example. And so it is that the Amur lands, at any rate today, cannot yet be regarded as a Russian agricultural colony.*) To this a second factor is added. The settlement of the old parts of Siberia was carried out by peasants, the settlement of the Amur region by Cossacks.
