The fleet that appeared off Petropavlovsk on 17 August 1854 consisted of seven warships—four British and three French—with a total armament of 236 guns. The fortification of the harbor by several forts had been carried out in accordance with Muravyov’s instructions during his visit. The seven different batteries comprised 35 guns. It seemed almost a matter of course that these would not be able to withstand the fire of 236 cannons in the long run. Nevertheless, the Allies did not succeed in taking the fortress. The bombardment lasted two days; on both days they attempted to land, and on both days they were repulsed with extraordinary losses. In the final, decisive attempt, they lost no less than a third of their landing force. The operations of the Allies were also adversely affected by the fact that the commander of the squadron, the British Admiral Price, shot himself on the morning before the first attack, and command suddenly devolved upon the senior French officer. Had this incident not occurred, the Allies would hardly have abandoned their attempts to destroy the Russian positions so quickly, given their considerable superiority in numbers.

This victory of Muravyov’s young, newly created army was greeted with understandable jubilation in Russia, and above all in Siberia itself, and strengthened his position immensely. Yet it must be regarded as an act of great prudence on Muravyov’s part that he subsequently did not expose himself to the risk of tarnishing the brilliance of this victory a second time with the shadows of an inevitable defeat. It had become known that the following year, as soon as ice conditions permitted, the Allied fleet was to appear once more before Petropavlovsk in reinforced strength. Russian defensive strength would not have been equal to this. Muravyov therefore sent his personal aide-de-camp from Irkutsk in the dead of the Siberian winter by dog sled to Kamchatka to deliver the order to the commandant of Petropavlovsk to evacuate the place, along with its garrison and civilian population. This order was carried out immediately, and in the spring of 1855 the Russian fleet—consisting of the frigate *Aurora*, the corvette *Olivutsa*, and the transport ships *Dvina*, *Irtysh*, and *Baikal*, as well as a boat—sailed south under the command of the commandant of Petropavlovsk, General Zavoiko. Since the Sea of Okhotsk was still frozen so early in the year (April), the fleet, having slipped past two enemy cruisers under cover of fog, entered the Tatar Sea from the south and anchored in Imperatorskaya Bay.

When the combined Anglo-French fleet arrived off Petropavlovsk in May, it found only a few American traders, who asked to be taken aboard in case the fortifications, their only protection against the natives, were destroyed. The Allied fleet then set out on a fruitless search for the Russian ships in the Bering Sea and along the coast of Alaska. On the very same day, however, that the combined squadron under Admiral Bruce arrived off Petropavlovsk, a British squadron under Commodore Elliot also arrived at the Tatar Sea, before which the Russian ships withdrew northward from Imperatorskaya Bay to De Castries Bay. The Russian fleet was now in a highly precarious situation. For the passage [[p197]] to the north discovered by Nevelskoy was still blocked by ice. One had to be prepared, therefore, for a battle whose outcome would undoubtedly have ended in the capture of the Russian ships. Incomprehensibly, however, Elliot did not attack the Russian squadron, but cruised in the south of the Tatar Sea to bar the Russians' exit until reinforcements should arrive. Outside Russia, Nevelskoy’s discovery was not yet known at that time, and Elliot believed the Russian ships were trapped in the "gulf" as in a mousetrap. Yet while he dispatched the brig *Bittern* to Admiral Stirling to ask him for reinforcements, the ice in the passage broke up (on 14 May), and under cover of fog the Russian fleet slipped out of the harbor of De Castries Bay through the northern passage toward the mouth of the Amur. Just in the nick of time! For on 16 May Elliot returned to De Castries Bay and was astonished in the highest degree to find that the Russian ships were no longer there. For him, no other solution to the puzzle remained than that the Russians had escaped southward under cover of the fog. He therefore steered south, and while the Anglo-French fleet searched in vain for the vanished Russian ships in the Bering Sea, Elliot cruised in the Sea of Japan and along the eastern side of Sakhalin in the hope of intercepting the ships after all—at any rate, one of the most amusing jokes world history has ever played.
