### 3. Blagoveshchensk.

1 August.

Blagoveshchensk is laid out rather spaciously on the flat stretch of riverbank bounded by the Amur and the Zeya. Stone houses are rare, and the streets and sidewalks still display the primitive condition typical of Siberian towns. The town has slightly fewer than 40,000 inhabitants, most of whom make their living from trade, shipping, and the gold industry. It is the only town in the Priamurskaya, the seat of the governor. His residence stands in an elongated park that extends along the bank of the Amur. Many cyclists are to be seen on the streets.

Mr. Kloss calls for me around 11 o'clock with his carriage. We first drive to the landing stage. The agent of the mail steamer line cannot yet give any definite information about continuing our journey in the direction of Sretensk. For the moment, the mail steamers are largely stranded on the upper reaches of the Amur, but the agent hopes that a steamer which has managed to cross the shallows will arrive tomorrow and continue on [[p256]] westward right away. The prospects of moving on are nevertheless rather dismal. Mr. Kloss believes that when we inquire again tomorrow, the agent will simply hope that the steamer will arrive the day after tomorrow. To be on the safe side, I have a berth reserved in any case.

We drive a short distance through the town and then up toward the hills that rise to the west. Up there on one of the heights lies the military cemetery, and today is the anniversary of the relief of Blagoveshchensk. A year ago, Russian troops crossed the Amur, drove the Chinese border army from its positions near the Manchurian village of Sakhalin directly opposite Blagoveshchensk, and then took all the Chinese settlements on the right bank of the Amur down as far as Aigun, which was likewise overtaken by its fate a few days later. The anniversary is being observed today and tomorrow with every pomp. We hope to arrive in time for at least part of the ceremony. Our troika carries us up the hill at a gallop. We pass a place enclosed by palisades nearly five meters high, which serves as a prison. There, the 21st Rifle Regiment, which had been in action at the time, is already marching toward us. The ceremony at the cemetery is therefore over. Nevertheless, we drive on. When we reach the cemetery, we alight from the carriage. We are met by the governor, the Cossack General Gribsky, accompanied by his suite of officers. Mr. Kloss greets him, and I am introduced. The general addresses me in almost dialect-free German, and we converse for a while with him and the police master, who holds a rather important position in Siberian towns. Then the general takes his leave with his suite, and we visit the small wooden chapel in [[p257]] the cemetery, view the monument unveiled today, as well as the graves of the fallen decorated with fresh flowers. The view opening before us from up here is picturesque. The broad silver ribbons of the Amur and the Zeya, glittering in the sun, wind across a gently undulating plain covered with dense green. One might imagine oneself somewhere in Europe, but the awareness that no villages lie beyond the hills, and that across the way the dense forests only rarely echo with the footsteps of a trapper or a nomadic Buryat, causes a feeling of isolation to steal over us despite the flourishing town at our feet, marring the unadulterated enjoyment. Indeed, there is probably no town of Blagoveshchensk's size so confined to the barest minimum of communication with the outside world. One must travel 1,000 kilometers upriver or downriver before reaching the next town, the last station connected by railway to the great global transport network. The difficulties with which communication between these two cultural poles must contend can readily be imagined in light of the obstacles that attended our journey to Blagoveshchensk and our departure from it. This isolated position of Blagoveshchensk naturally creates quite unique economic conditions. Clearly, everything that has already been said above regarding the economic conditions of the Amur region as such manifests itself in an almost typical manner in the vicinity of Blagoveshchensk. Above all, this holds true for the mining industry. Fairly rich coal seams have been discovered nearby. Naturally, deforestation around so large a town yields its unwelcome consequences far more acutely than near the small Cossack settlements. Furthermore, the gold industry on the Zeya and the Bureya is far more advanced than on the lower Amur. This is evident from the fact that factories have been established in Blagoveshchensk to manufacture implements and even machinery for gold mining. Moreover, the primitive method of gold washing is no longer used exclusively; here and there good results have already been achieved with the amalgamation process. Although the gold industry near Blagoveshchensk still suffers enormously for the present from the fact that Russia's actions against the Chinese living along the Amur have driven these laborers—who, given the general shortage of manpower, were exceptionally valuable—almost completely away from the gold operations, it may nevertheless be assumed that the resulting slump is only temporary, and that under peaceful conditions it will very soon be followed by a recovery. Should the gold industry, over the course of decades, gradually overcome the rigors of the climate and transition to the introduction of steam-powered machinery, the need to extract the existing coal reserves will presumably arise first at this very point in the Amur region. Yet here, too, development is likely to proceed at a slow pace.
