## Chapter V. Russia and Manchuria.

Chefoo, March 1–8.

I have positively studied the newspapers in order to reconnect myself with events on the world stage. In Zhili all is quiet for the present, nor have any military events occurred during my Manchurian expedition—at least none of any consequence. I have therefore missed nothing, and in all likelihood will miss nothing if I sit quietly here for a while longer. The passport promised by Vice-Admiral Alexeyev is also taking its time. So I have leisure. From the dispatches that have arrived from home, I see that not too much of novelty has happened there either. The Chinese Question seems to have disappeared from the center of interest; people are only still arguing back and forth over whether a Russian-Chinese agreement regarding Manchuria exists or not. From Russian semi-official quarters the existence of such a treaty is flatly denied. Dr. Morrison, on the other hand, stands firm on it. And that means something. For his political feelers in Peking reach into the European legations just as much as into the Chinese yamens; he can afford to keep a whole staff of Chinese political researchers, and is therefore usually very reliable and credible in Chinese affairs.

During my stay in Newchwang, in-the-know Russian officials spoke quite openly to me about the agreement, which was treated as a done deal. At that time, people still viewed [[p104]] the matter as fairly harmless; they did not yet know that the opposition of the other powers with interests in China would after all become serious. In the meantime, the new German Reich Chancellor, evidently to throw a morsel into the squawking beaks of the fanatical Russia-worshipers back home, has declared that the Anglo-German Agreement does *not* apply to Manchuria. At least, that is the dispatch received by the *Ostasiatischer Lloyd* [East Asian Lloyd]—a paper strongly pro-Russian and, in recent times, ever since it began receiving a substantial imperial subsidy for direct German telegrams from home, turned thoroughly consul-general-esque. In curious contradiction to this, however, stands the report that the British Foreign Secretary is said to have declared in the House of Commons that the Anglo-German Agreement does apply to Manchuria after all, which is also what one would assume from its wording. The situation is thus more blurred today than at the time when Morrison first published the text of the agreement.

It is actually incorrect to speak of an agreement that relates *only* to Manchuria. Article 8 of the treaty, according to Morrison's version, is said to read: “Without the consent of Russia, China may not grant to any other power, or to the subject of such, any mining, railway, or other rights in the Russian-Chinese border provinces, namely in Manchuria, Mongolia, and Turkestan (Kashgar, Yarkand, and Khotan). Nor may China itself build any railways in these provinces without the consent of Russia. Finally, outside Newchwang, no land in these provinces may be leased to a subject of any other nation.”

In the long run it becomes tedious to repeat that our knowledge and conceptions of China are the most inadequate imaginable. Nevertheless, at this point I must once again get to the bottom of an error that I have encountered both in the newspapers back home and out here in the discussion of these questions. Namely, the notion that these borderlands are vassal states of China, and as such are supposedly not part of the fabric of the Chinese Empire at all. They certainly do belong to it, in part even extraordinarily firmly! Manchuria in particular, as the ancestral homeland of the ruling dynasty, is linked with China in the closest manner. According to ancient custom, it is from Manchuria that the Chinese Banner troops are recruited, as well as their Banner generals—some of the most influential figures in the great Chinese Empire! Who on earth coined the foolish notion that Manchuria is a “vassal state”? On the contrary, it is the ancestral land, no less than Brandenburg is the ancestral land of the Kingdom of Prussia. To be sure—today we have a Kingdom of Prussia and no Kingdom of Brandenburg. But does that make the former political Brandenburg suddenly a vassal state of Prussia? Likewise, we have an empire that is no longer called “Manchuria,” but “China.” It happens very often in world history that a conqueror ascends the throne of a culturally superior empire and assumes its title, believing that the ancient name will lend him greater prestige. But did the German lands become vassal states of Rome when Charlemagne and his successors had themselves crowned Roman emperors? If the people who drafted the German-English Agreement had wanted to exclude Manchuria, well, then they could have [[p105]] said so. How untenable the excuse is that Manchuria does not really belong to China is shown by the published draft of the treaty itself, which codifies the *actual* rule of Russia not only in Manchuria, but also in the remaining Russian-Chinese borderlands.
