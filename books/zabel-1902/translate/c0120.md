At the second blast of the steam whistle, everyone is back on board. We cast off and have already been steering upriver for an hour when the expected steamer comes into sight. Everyone hurries to pack up their belongings. Our steamer stops, drops anchor, and gives the other vessel the signal to halt. The latter is towing a large barge behind it, on which the third-class passengers are accommodated; it swings around so that the current does not drive the towed barge onto it, and moors alongside the *Sergei Dukhovskoi*. Our captain conveys the management’s order to the other captain, and the latter announces on his ship that the passengers and cargo are to be transferred.

What followed really defies all description. No sooner has the announcement been made than a storm of protest erupts among the passengers of the *Sergei Witte*, and while down below they are already beginning to take over the third-class passengers, a fierce dispute develops up above, at first between the captain of the other steamer and his passengers. Soon, however, this dispute spreads over to our steamer as well, and now it is passengers against passengers. A most vehement war of words begins: we resent the passengers of the other steamer for not wanting to cross over to our dreadful old crate, and they resent us for expecting such a thing of them. Grand popular harangues are delivered, people are already moving closer across the paddle boxes, and the air is filled with the shrill voices of quarreling people. The captains are powerless. The captain of the *Sergei Witte* is naturally subjected to the worst of it. In the end, he covers his ears, runs to the speaking tube, and shouts down to the engine room, “Steam to the anchor winch!” Five minutes later, the *Sergei Witte* steams off downriver towards Mikhailovo-Semyonovsk. There he goes, and that is the end of that! But now our captain is furious as well. He, too, will now travel back to Mikhailovo-Semyonovsk, report the incident by telegraph to the management from there, and request instructions for himself and the other captain. And so we, too, turn around and travel back to the stanitsa from which we departed at noon today. Then we moor alongside the *Sergei Witte*, paddle box to paddle box; the passengers, among whom good humor gains the upper hand, mingle and spend the evening together in cheerful chatter and mutual teasing. The buffet on the *Sergei Witte* is likewise sold out. We cannot [[p242]] therefore offer one another any drinks. For that matter, the news we receive is hardly comforting. The passengers had taken no less than four weeks for the stretch from Sretensk, and that on the downstream voyage! Up to Blagoveshchensk they had used four different steamers, and from there two more. Over shallow stretches they had been drifted along on a barge. Numerous steamers along the way are stranded together with their passengers, waiting for high water. A small band of itinerant musicians, mostly Germans who were among the deck passengers, brought them some diversion. Grim prospects for us! By midnight, no reply to the captain’s dispatch has arrived.

July 23.
We remain moored until noon before the telegraphic reply arrives from Khabarovsk. Either the management was unable to decide this awkward case immediately, or the telegraph was not functioning properly. That happens frequently; private telegrams, too, must often wait for days when the wire is heavily congested with government dispatches, which is the rule. The dispatch repeats the order: the transfer is to take place. But the passengers of the *Witte* stand firm and say they can only be removed by force from their steamer, which is decidedly more comfortably appointed than ours, though that does not take much. There is nothing left for the captain to do, therefore, but to submit. He accordingly telegrams his management that he has engine trouble and must therefore proceed to Khabarovsk. And what about us? Oh, word has already been wired to Blagoveshchensk to send out a new steamer to meet us and take us aboard when we can go no further. Towards one o’clock in the afternoon we finally get underway again, once more over the same stretch we already know. After thirty-five versts, we reach the confluence area of the Sungari.

The Amur is nearly fifteen kilometers wide here. One can clearly distinguish where the greenish water of the Amur and the yellow water of the Chinese river, flowing with a steeper gradient, unite. At the mouth lies a thriving Korean colony. The Sungari, alongside the Ussuri, is the second great Manchurian river open to Russian navigation. It forms a waterway that reaches deep into the heart of Manchuria, especially when one includes its tributary, the Nonni. On the Sungari and its tributaries lie some of the principal stations of the Chinese Eastern Railway: Ninguta, Harbin, and Tsitsihar. A large portion of the railway construction material was transported along it to these ports, which are also the terminal points of the railway construction divisions. Its course, which carries more water than the Amur and is also regarded by the Chinese as the main stream, is over two thousand versts long. Its mouth area is dotted with islands formed by the alluvial deposits of loess silt carried down by the Sungari.
