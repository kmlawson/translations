Despite this success, which was only partial after all, we have another interesting hunt today that could almost have been a whale hunt. On the homeward run, we notice a number of very large fish in the water. For the most part, they signal their location by their mighty dorsal fin protruding from the water. But whales they are not. They are not large enough for that, even though they are still thirty or more feet long, and they also lack the characteristic spout. The whale-gunner, however, has never seen these fish in his home waters, and they offer a fairly good target to get some shooting practice with the gun, which he has not yet tested. The day is lost for any further whale hunting anyway, because the captain has orders to bring me back safely to the station in the evening, regardless of whether with or without a whale. Consequently, we steer toward one of these fish, which tolerates this quite indifferently. We come within a few fathoms of it, the gun roars, the line runs out, and the steam winch begins to work. But no counter-pull ensues. Finally the harpoon is hauled out of the water; it is covered with shreds of flesh, and the grenade has exploded. So it was a hit. But the fish has vanished. Perhaps it was too small [[p163]] for the heavy projectile, perhaps its flesh was too soft and could not withstand the explosion of the grenade, so that the barbs of the harpoon could not take a firm hold; at any rate, the how and where remain hidden. The gun is loaded a second time. For a second time one of these sea monsters is steered toward, again with good success. Again the gun roars, again the waves hiss, and again the fish disappears, never to be seen again. Unfortunately, a minor accident occurs on this occasion: the grenade bursts before penetrating the body of the fish. But the harpoon must nevertheless have entered the fish's body, for it brings up shreds of flesh once more. Now, however, the gunner swears high and low that he will not waste another grain of powder on this sort of whale. As we later learn, he had probably shot at so-called swordfish, which belong to the dolphin family, that is, the toothed whales. At nightfall we arrive back in Gaidamak. Thus my first whale hunt ends without result. To be sure, the day had been ill-chosen—it was the 13th of the month!

July 11.
In a year or two, the company will have steered the entire whaling operation into different channels. Already in the autumn of this year (1901), the director of the Gaidamak station, Herr Sommermeyer, is traveling to Hamburg to supervise the construction, or rather conversion, of a large ship intended as a sort of floating whaling station, a factory ship on the high seas. It will be the first ship of its kind in existence in the world, combining all the establishments found at the whaling station, perhaps with the sole exception of the cooperage. The machinery is being supplied by the factory of Friedrich Krupp in Essen. The deck of the ship will be fitted out as a flensing deck. Flensing the fish will naturally be done by machine, and hardly a shred of the whale will go overboard, as everything will find a use and immediate processing. This factory ship is intended—as guaranteed by Krupp—to enable the complete processing of a whale in the incredibly short time of two hours. In this time, then, after the whale has been hauled onto the flensing deck, that is, on deck, it will be cut up; the soft parts will be chopped up, conveyed mechanically to the boilers, boiled there, and the train oil will be filled into barrels immediately after boiling. The boiled-out and, no doubt for good measure, pressed residual parts—at any rate completely de-oiled—will be brought to the drying room, ground, and filled into casks to be used later as fertilizer. The same will be done with the bones. These first pass under massive rollers that crush them until nothing is left but a thick pulp. The oil contained in it will be extracted from this pulp by chemical or mechanical means; finally, the meal also enters the drying apparatus and is poured into casks as bone meal. The casks will be sealed and placed in the hold, ready for shipment at once. And all this will take place within a period of only two hours. The whalers thus will have no need at all to keep returning to the station whenever they have shot a whale, nor do they need to moor it first, but rather tow it through the water straight to the factory ship, which will naturally be stationed in the middle of the main hunting zone, and the latter will then take the whale aboard.
