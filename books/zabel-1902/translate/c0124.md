* * *

July 29. The river, which until now exhibited quite a considerable width, is substantially narrowed by the mountains of the Lesser Khingan range closing in tightly from both sides, in places to as little as a single kilometer. Its current is considerably stronger, and the steamer runs quite close to the bank, where the water flows more slowly. The stops to take on wood are curtailed as far as possible; only just as much wood is loaded as is strictly necessary. In recent years, with the increasing deforestation of the banks, wood has risen greatly in price; on average a fathom costs six rubles. The river now runs in numerous tight bends, imparting to the scenery a peculiar charm that had hitherto been missed, especially since the water level was so low that for the most part one could not even see over the flat margins of the banks. Our steamer makes good speed. Today we covered 140 kilometers and are anchoring by the village of Innokentyevskaya.

* * *

July 30. The Lesser Khingan now lies behind us as well. Toward noon we reach the settlement of Poyarkovo. It is a fairly large village, named in honor of the first pioneer in the Amur Valley, Poyarkov. A handsome church done in white and blue towers over the settlement. We are now only 160 versts away from Blagoveshchensk; the city can already be reached from here by carriage, a "tarantass." The weather is splendid; on board, "all well." Nikolai Mikhailovich is playing a Russian card game called "Wind" with Elena Grigoryevna, and I am playing Sixty-Six with the Cossack officer's little wife. We are playing for sweets. We each bought ourselves a large bag of "karamelli," and every lost game costs one sweet. When I lose, I pay honestly; when she loses, she does not want to pay. If one threatens to call her husband, Mitya, to help, she pays immediately. Cheating is permitted. But whoever gets caught at it pays two "karamelli."

### 2. A City in Ruins on the Amur.

July 31.

Around 9 o'clock in the morning we come near Aigun, once the northernmost Chinese bulwark erected against the advance of Russian influence, and most recently famous for the Convention of Aigun, in which the left bank of the Amur was ceded to Russia. Only a year ago it was a flourishing city, estimated at 15,000 inhabitants. A strong Chinese garrison was stationed there. Today the city is a heap of ruins. It was just around the turn of July that the fighting took place in which Aigun was destroyed, and with it China's power definitively broken on the right bank of the Amur as well. Two kilometers from ruined Aigun, a handsome stone building, a Russian barracks, now rises on the right bank of the Amur. To those people who still insist today that Manchuria is still a Chinese province and that Russia entertains the intention of eventually returning its rule over the province to China, this fact may serve as proof to the contrary. The turmoil in Zhili had absorbed the world's interest to such an extent that the important fighting in northern Manchuria completely escaped it. Until last year, Russia was not yet the de facto master of the right bank of the Amur. Only the Chinese turmoil gave it a welcome pretext to complete the work of Muravyov, namely to take full possession of the right bank of the Amur as well, thus cutting China off completely from the "Black Dragon River." It has likewise escaped the outside world that it cost very considerable struggles to drive the Chinese from the banks of the Amur. The cemeteries near Aigun and Blagoveshchensk are an eloquent testimony to these battles. But the work has been thoroughly accomplished. In Aigun not a single roof beam remains; indeed, one can say almost literally: "not one stone is left standing upon another." In the Convention of Aigun, navigation on the Amur is guaranteed to both nations, the Chinese as well as the Russians. During my journey along the Amur I have not seen a single Chinese junk! Russia has hushed up these battles, or at least failed to let their full significance be seen from its reports. Should I not, then, be surprised to find, instead of a Chinese city, only a vast field of debris and a burned-out ruin? A single year had been enough to allow the site on which a flourishing city had stood until then to become overgrown with man-high grass and weeds, so that from the water one would not even have been able to tell that Aigun once lay here, had one not had one's attention [[p252]] drawn to it, and had not a stone tower and the protruding stone walls of a yamen with their fire-blackened remnants of beams caught the eye.

One must grant Russia this: it does a clean job. In two years it builds an entire city on Dalian Bay at government expense, yet one year of Russian civilization is also enough to remove from the banks of the Amur the last tiny vestige of Chinese civilization and to let the ruins of a city that was flourishing only just now become overgrown with weeds and grass. In the very same year, in the extreme north—Aigun, the last and northernmost outpost of Chinese power, razed to the ground; in the extreme south—Dalny, the new Russian San Francisco. The intervening country was taken into de facto possession only in this past year. Until then, the Russian in Manchuria was merely a privileged concessionaire; today he is the master and acts as such—*ut exemplum docet*.
