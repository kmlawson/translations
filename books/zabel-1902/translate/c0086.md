All of this, however, already presupposes a mutual acquaintance, and the European border dwellers of the Ural region certainly possessed fairly comprehensive knowledge of the lands beyond the Urals by the time of Yermak’s invasion, even if this knowledge was not reflected in the Russian chronicles of that era or in the minds of the rulers in Moscow. It may therefore also be assumed that when the Stroganovs commissioned the Cossack ataman Yermak to conquer the land of Yugra, they were by no means taking a leap into the dark, but were well aware of their plans and of what might come out of it for them. It should be added, moreover, that although the aforementioned Ostyak chieftain Yediger sent tribute to the Tsar, he never received any support from him against the Tatars; rather, in the course of a raid, he lost both life and land to them without Russia troubling itself much about the matter—even though this was the era of Ivan IV, to whom humanistic-sentimental historiography has indeed given the epithet “the Terrible,” but who is nonetheless one of the most formidable figures among rulers. Without Peter the Great, no Europeanized Russia; without Ivan, no Russia! He is the founder of the tsardom, which to this day remains the key to the secret of Russia’s strength and political agility. To him can also be traced the close union of the Muscovite realm with the Greek Catholic Church—two of the sturdy foundation pillars upon which the Russian state edifice rests. Ivan’s mission and significance, however, lay primarily in the domestic realm; Muscovite expansion as far as the Urals was in truth merely an imposed struggle over the eastern border, which he had to undertake in order to gain a free hand to establish peace and order at home. This also explains why Ivan himself considered his political task toward the east of the empire to be completed once he had incorporated the territories of Kazan and Astrakhan into the Muscovite state [[p181]] and used them, as it were, as a buffer against any potential Tatar onslaught from the east that might have been directed against the Muscovite heartland. He was content if virtually unknown peoples east of the Urals delivered a meager consignment of animal skins to him each year as tribute. Yet he hardly gave serious thought to ever offering any return for this tribute in the form of protection for these subject tribes against Tatar encroachments; indeed, he did not even undertake the defense of the eastern frontier of the newly acquired territories of Kazan and Astrakhan and the old colony of Vyatka on the part of the state, but instead left it to private individuals, who in turn recouped their costs by exploiting the authorization to defend the national border toward the east through sending more or less regular minor expeditions into the eastern frontier regions with the assignment of collecting “tribute” from the natives. In practice, however, these expeditions were regular raiding parties, which served as aggressive measures for securing the border, but even more so for enriching the wholesale merchants entrusted with the protection of that border. For them, the protection of the eastern border was thus simultaneously, or primarily, a business. This, too, was and remained one of the fundamental principles of the Russian advance into Siberia until the time when the Russian state as such applied a proper colonial policy in Siberia. Authorized and unauthorized persons alike pushed farther and farther to the east, exacting levies in the form of furs and natural produce from the weak native tribes. In many cases, the natives yielded these levies under the pressure of the moral coercion of a stronger race, but more often they gave way to the brutal force of marauders. All indications suggest that such minor expeditions—which by no means ran counter to the morality of the time—already took place during the first part of the sixteenth century from the settlements along the Volga and Kama, without, however, having acquired any greater significance. In any event, it is certain that authority to do so was granted to certain prominent colonists soon after the annexation of the former Novgorod colony of Vyatka. Here, another characteristic trait of Russian colonization also comes into consideration: namely, the tendency and extraordinary capacity of the Russian race to intermingle with alien races, and above all with inferior ones. In this instance, through the annexation of former Tatar territories, there existed a number of Tatar princes toward whom Russia had certain obligations, while at the same time it was desirable to transform them into useful members of the Russian nation. One such family of Tatar origin, descended from a mirza lineage, was the Stroganov family, already Russified by the time of Ivan IV, whose estates on the Kama—the longest eastern tributary of the Volga, having its source in the Urals—dated back to the days of Ivan III. In the course of time, this family had also attained power and prestige under the new state of affairs. Ivan IV concluded a formal treaty with them. He extended the Stroganovs’ concession on the upper Kama, permitted them to establish colonies, fell timber, recruit laborers, and build saltworks, and exempted them from all taxes for twenty years, imposing on them in return, however, the obligation to defend the eastern border of the realm. They also received the authority to establish blockhouse stations along the frontiers and [[p182]] to maintain an adequate armed force. This was the authorization for the aforementioned raids, which now began to assume political significance—specifically from the moment when chance brought the Stroganovs a very considerable number of reckless mercenaries in these otherwise only sparsely populated regions. Robbers, murderers, thieves, adventurers, rabble, escaped convicts—Germans, Russians, Poles, Lithuanians—and all of them summed up under the euphemistic designation of “Cossacks”: these were the pioneers of the Russian advance into Siberia. Their captain was named Vasily Timofeyevich and was known simply by his nickname, “Yermak.” In German, this means “millstone,” and stems from the fact that Vasily had been a boatman in his youth and had usually been required to prepare food for the Volga sailors, which included the preliminary grinding of the grain. Yermak, the river pirate and Cossack ataman, today possesses a magnificent monument created by Antokolsky; he ranks among the national heroes of Russia and has even been admitted among the saints of the Orthodox Church. From robber captain to national saint—a long step! Yermak was more fortunate than Karl Moor, but in return he “stole a kingdom as well,” so as to purchase with it an indulgence from church and state for his long debt of sin.
