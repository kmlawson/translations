Even though the Cossack is likewise a farmer, he still divides his agricultural activities with those of a soldier, boatman, hunter, and post-driver. A thorough division of labor, however, has so far remained largely impossible for political reasons; in the Ussuri region, it is only just emerging. It is also political reasons that induce Russia to keep away the Chinese—who would be equal to the agricultural conditions of the Amur provinces—to exclude them from land ownership, and to tolerate them only reluctantly even as industrial laborers. Furthermore, the Cossack population has always been accustomed to enjoying certain privileges, and allowance has accordingly been made for this circumstance by granting them the river fisheries, as well as the entire left bank of the Amur from Pokrovskaya all the way to Khabarovsk, to a depth of up to 25 versts inland from the course of the river, as landed property. This allocation, however, was an independent act of Governor-General Baron Korff that lacks government sanction to this day. Should the Amur territory truly experience an extraordinary boom as a colonial land in the future, this measure would likely take bitter revenge, for through it new settlers are cut off from the vital artery of these parts, in that any settlement along the banks would always first have to be purchased from the currently legitimate owners, the Cossacks. But should Russia find itself compelled to strip the Cossacks of this property again, that could hardly take place without serious conflict. It was therefore very imprudent of the Russian government to leave these conditions unregulated up to the present, and it would be still more imprudent were the current state of affairs to be maintained for a long time. On the other hand, the authorities have gone to another extreme by barring new settlers from acquiring private ownership of land over a considerable stretch to the right and left of the Ussuri Railway. This measure is intended to prevent land speculation in the vicinity of the railway line, and no doubt many of our land reformers will warm to it. It is, however, an often-observed fact that in regions whose value is first to be developed through the enterprise of new settlers, free [[p293]] competition often accomplishes more within the space of a year than the wisest of government measures within ten. Practice often works differently from theory. If one therefore considers the prospects of the Amur region as a colonial territory in itself, a whole series of favorable conditions stand out, which, if rationally cultivated, justify great hopes. Yet the colonial politician finds only few measures of Russian policy that do justice to these conditions. On the contrary, it seems as though a one-sided policy—one that looks less to the interests of the colonial land itself than to the external political power position of Russia in the Far East—has allied itself with the shortcomings of the human material available for colonization, in order to create here in the Far East the very same most deplorable economic conditions that we lament in European Russia. Here, proof seems once again to be furnished that lands taken into possession by Russia are withdrawn from global economic progress of the kind pursued by distinct trading nations in their colonizing activity; and political daydreams, which already adorn Russian possessions in Siberia with fantastical visions of the future, cannot obscure from anyone who probes to the bottom of things the clear foresight that the same Russia which in Europe cannot prevent peasants from starving in one province while another does not know how to dispose of its surplus harvest is likewise incapable of guiding its colonial land to the calm economic development and prosperity it deserves by its nature. Those with intimate knowledge of Siberian settlement conditions already claim that the peak of the settlement movement has passed, and the thousands of immigrants who return home disappointed and impoverished suggest that there is some truth to this assumption. If it is indeed the case that Siberia cannot support as many people in its narrow zone suitable for cultivation as Russian optimists seem to assume in view of the country's vast size, then the economic prospects for these regions, even under a different administration, are not so brilliant that we need consequently fear an overwhelming influence on [[p294]] existing world market conditions from the completion of the Trans-Siberian Railway. The economic benefits of this enterprise—which is heavily calculated on future growth and has swallowed capital whose servicing would require an extraordinary elevation of the Siberian and Russian level of civilization—then appear in by no means rosy light. What does it profit a modern state to throw away millions upon millions for the sake of its position as a world power, without being able to shape the economic conditions of the country itself so that, out of their own resources, they are capable of replenishing the expended sums within the foreseeable future? A state that engages heavily in foreign policy without its domestic circumstances compelling it to do so is on a slippery slope—and he who is to feed upon his own fat will grow lean, even if he be a giant!
