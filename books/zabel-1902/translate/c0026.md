After sunset on the same day, by order of Vice-Admiral Alexeyev, the Chinese flag flying from the flagpole of the Chinese Maritime Customs was hauled down and the Russian flag hoisted, which flies there to this day and presumably for all eternity will continue to

[Illustration: Russian staging post at an inn in Tudiatai (p. 31).]

fly. From that moment on, Newchwang has in fact been under Russian administration. The Daotai had fled, allegedly with the consent of the Russians, who indeed also permitted him to travel by train with bag and baggage to Tianzhuangtai, which they could easily have prevented. The suspicious charade of the Chinese official, which I mentioned earlier, is cast in a somewhat different light by this. Even if annexation had not yet been formally declared, official documents were not long in coming. The first read, in literal translation, as follows:

"Yingkou, August 6, 1900.

To the Consuls of the Treaty Powers, Yingkou.

Your Excellencies! The hostile conduct of the Chinese authorities, who first stirred up the rebellion and then declared they could not maintain order, [[p60]] ended in an open attack on our forces, which we had stationed in the city in accordance with the wishes of the consular corps. During the said attacks, the Chinese authorities fled, leaving the city to its fate. In order to prevent lawlessness and pillaging on the part of the Chinese mob and to protect the trade of the port and foreign property, the Russian military authorities consider it necessary to place the city under the guard of Imperial Russian troops. It must be added that the perfidious destruction of the Chinese Eastern Railway by insurgents and Chinese troops likewise makes the maintenance of a military force in Yingkou necessary to protect the railway line. The Imperial Government nevertheless recognises as a fundamental principle that Yingkou is a treaty port; that is, the provisional administration shall be established in the interest of the Russians as well as of the foreigners and the Chinese, and does not intend to impair the rights and privileges which they formerly enjoyed in Yingkou. The foreign community is requested to note that the sole task of the provisional Russian administration is to maintain peace and order and to restore trade, and in these efforts I hope to be able to count on the sincere support of the foreign representatives. I have the honour to be Your Excellencies' sincere servant, E. Alexeyev, Vice-Admiral."

The tenor of this document is thoroughly loyal, and one might indeed be inclined to place full confidence in the assurances it contains, had one not already been duped all too often by fine Russian assurances. Alexeyev in particular is one of the craftiest men in the Far East, an exquisite master at operating with fine words and the most soothing assurances, of whom it could never be said that he failed to keep one of his promises, yet who, through it all, gives only such assurances as can be interpreted at will. "Be gentle as doves and wise as serpents," as the Bible says. The application of this scriptural saying can be seen illustrated, among other things, in the consummate manner in which the Muscovite pushed the Japanese to the wall in this entire affair. I recall that in the Sino-Japanese War, Japan had occupied Manchuria, and that Russia, France, and Germany forced the Mikado to withdraw from and abandon Manchuria once more in the Treaty of Shimonoseki. It is not surprising that Japan recalled its old interests in these regions at the very moment when the Russian was about to snatch the tasty morsel away right under its nose. German merchants know only too well what Germany forfeited in Japan by its stance during the conclusion of the Peace of Shimonoseki, without receiving the slightest compensation for it on the other side. They see no outcome of the operation other than that the further advance of Russian power toward the south of East Asia was quite pointlessly aided and abetted. The Tsarist Empire alone derived benefit from the intervention at the Sino-Japanese peace settlement; Germany in particular alienated Japan at that time—a nation that we out here in the East may yet need more than Russia—and Russia's gratitude, unfortunately, in no way corresponds to the sacrifices we made for its policy.
