### 3. Dalny, the East Asian San Francisco.

“Dalny” is a Russian word and means “the distance” in German. The Russian loves to give his city foundations flowery names, e.g. Vladivostok, the “stronghold of the East.” So too Dalny, “the distance.”

Once I have figured that out, I also know what it is all about. Already in Newchwang I had heard that Russia was in the process of building a great trading center in the south of the Liaodong Peninsula, “a completely modern city on the French model,” I was told, and people spoke of it as of something utterly mysterious, almost fairytale-like, that one could hardly quite believe. People spoke of it as of a utopia and delighted in vague notions. No one had seen the place yet, and even later in Chefoo, hardly anyone there knew anything about it either, despite its proximity. And I am certain that at home, too, it will come as a surprise that here we have to do with nothing other than the new Vancouver or San Francisco on the other shore of the Pacific Ocean, with the terminus of the Trans-Siberian Railway, with the great future Russian transit port on whose quay the great central station will rise, where after completion of the railway travelers at the ticket window will ask: “One second-class direct to Berlin, please! ... Please, London, first class! via Vlissingen! ...”

Naturally, I intended to pay a visit to this fairytale new city if that were at all possible; only the name “Dalny” was still new to me, and like so many other people in the East, I had always assumed that it was the port of Talienwan itself. I therefore gladly seize the opportunity offered to me, arrive at the station at the appointed hour by Mr. Timm’s parlor car, and steam off in a northerly direction in the cozily warmed carriage over a glass of tea together with the engineer, the chief physician of the military hospital in Dalny, and a third government official. Again the train rushes through the mountains of southern Liaodong, again it moves forward through high cuttings and over bridges and little bridges, for four hours, to the station of Nansanshilibao, where the last carriages of the train are uncoupled. While the main body of the train continues on toward Mukden and Newchwang respectively, another locomotive is coupled to our four cars, and after a ride of about thirty minutes in an easterly direction, brings us to the Gulf of Talienwan; the train stops at an open spot, in the midst of a half-finished underpass. But before us lies a veritable sea of lights toward which we are now heading. It is Dalny.

One has often read in novels of how in America cities grew out of the ground, one might almost say, overnight. The North American West has much to tell of such foundations. And for anyone accustomed to the slow and steady development of our cities at home, the tales and accounts of such instant cities certainly have something strange and unfathomable about them. While a new city there, to be sure, owes its origin to the entrepreneurial spirit of one or more speculators, or else to some new opening up of treasures that the earth had hitherto kept hidden, here on the Bay of Talienwan it has occurred, probably for the first time in the history of European nations, that a state has carried out such a costly speculation at its own expense and its own risk without these prerequisites. Indeed, it may well be the first time that a state has openly appeared as a city speculator, building a city entirely from the ground up, and then inviting the nations of the earth to come there, to view the homesteads that the bold speculator has built, and to acquire them—the more expensive, the better. Quite apart from everything else, this is at the very least an engineering task of the highest interest and appeal, and the whole undertaking is arguably without precedent in colonial history.

The only place that might possibly be drawn upon for comparison is Tsingtao. Here, too, they started with next to nothing; they even had to remove what little was there—a few wretched Chinese huts—first, in order to create a completely level base for [[p93]] the new city that was to rise upon this ground. But in the end, apart from the few and most indispensable government buildings, the development of the commercial section of the city was left to private enterprise. In Dalny they do things differently.
