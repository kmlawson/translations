For Siberia itself, the railway undoubtedly has immense—local—significance, for freight traffic as well. Already it has brought it about that the Siberian territories are much more closely connected to Russia than was previously the case. The Irbit Fair, which in the past almost single-handedly connected the trade between Russia and Siberia, has completely lost its significance. Siberian merchants now deal directly with Moscow and Petersburg, and often with Western Europe as well, which was previously quite out of the question, as business at that time took its course through the Irbit Fair. The railway has thus already eliminated an expensive intermediary link for Siberia's trade with Russia and abroad. But the local significance that the Trans-Siberian Railway possesses will also, in all likelihood, remain the only one that comes into consideration for freight traffic.

Insofar as the significance of the Trans-Siberian Railway for communication specifically between Germany and our East Asian possessions and spheres of interest comes into consideration, the following points can be asserted. The shortening of postal and passenger traffic benefits our colony of Tsingtao by reducing the travel time by about 14 days. Admittedly, we would only have direct advantages to expect from the Trans-Siberian Railway for our East Asian possessions if the planned connecting line through the Selenga Valley via Kyakhta and Kalgan to Peking were to be constructed, which Russia indeed apparently intends to build.*) To be sure, Russia would then be cutting into its own flesh and again rendering illusory the advantages it wished to create for its new possessions in the Far East through the Trans- [[p322]] [Illustration: General view of Moscow (p. 309).]

But if the Trans-Siberian Railway were to reach the Pacific Ocean by the shortest route at Tsingtao, this locality would likely—lying as it does more centrally within the commercially relevant part of East Asia than Dalny, and being easier to reach than the latter both from South China and from the Philippines and Japan—be able to derive a direct benefit from the Trans-Siberian Railway. For this reason, we Germans have a considerable interest in the construction of the railway across Mongolia, and that is, in my opinion, [[p324]] a viewpoint from which the Trans-Siberian Railway as such and the study of Russian transport policy is also a matter of great importance for us Germans. To be sure, one must not forget in this regard that the construction of the Mongolian line would mean an influence over Peking for Russia that would likely appear more valuable than the future of Manchuria and of easternmost Asia. And given the character of Russian colonial policy, it is by no means out of the question that Russia itself may in the future abandon Port Arthur and Dalny just as lightheartedly as it once abandoned Petropavlovsk and more recently abandoned or disadvantaged the Amur territory and even Vladivostok in favour of a more southerly, more alluring possession. Even these direct advantages, which Germany might hope for from the Trans-Siberian Railway for its East Asian possessions, are thus of such a nature that they could easily turn into their opposite.
