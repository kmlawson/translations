We land. I make careful inquiries as to whether rain has fallen westwards in the headwaters of the Amur or the Shilka, roughly 3,000 kilometres from here as the crow flies. Nothing is known of it yet. Well, no matter then; I am determined to travel.

If the rain will not come to meet me, well then, I shall go to meet it, like Muhammad to the mountain. Under no circumstances, however, will I remain stranded in Vladivostok any longer. For if I must sit down and wait, I can certainly do so just as well somewhere else.

So now it is time to take my leave! I cannot exactly claim that parting from Vladivostok is hard for me. But a pinch of sentimentality is in the German’s

[Illustration: Vladivostok: The Railway Station (p. 155).]

blood, after all, and indeed, I have quite a number of people in Vladivostok whom I shall remember with pleasure, and to whom I must still bid a sincere “thank you” and “farewell”. So farewell visits—a warm word here, there at least a fleeting handshake in passing! Even my Łódź Jew, the innkeeper, gets a handshake, even though he charged me on my bill for an extra day’s lodging and at least five vodkas too many, which he nevertheless smiles and allows to be deducted. In Russia, one shakes hands with everyone anyway, unless one wants to get a reputation for being impolite, and one certainly does not want that. If you enter a restaurant where you have twenty acquaintances sitting at twenty different [[p171]] tables, you have to go up to the twenty different tables and give your hand to each of the twenty different acquaintances. There is no need to press or shake the hand. That would be too strenuous—just offer it; nor is it taken amiss if you extend your hand to one person while already looking around for the next. Having packed my things, however, I look around my “number” with a certain melancholy—a room that was, during my stay, an—oh so dear home to me. Now that my things have been cleared out, it looks desolate inside. Four bare whitewashed walls, an iron bedstead with a straw mattress and a bolster without a pillowcase, on the wall a washstand without water with a tin basin beneath it, then a rough wooden table, a few nails driven into the wall on which to hang clothes, a burnt-down candle stub, an empty glass for tea and for rinsing the mouth, in the corner of the room the obligatory black Byzantine Madonna painted on a gold ground and set in a gilded frame, and on the table a long, indeed an extra-long bill for my stay. This last item of furnishing constitutes the only difference in the appearance of my room between the time I moved in and now, as I leave it. Still, the sight moves me, and I am in a pensive mood as I direct my steps down to the street. With hands behind my back, I stroll up to the heights along which the city stretches, climbing higher and higher until I finally reach a hilltop some five hundred feet above the town, where a magnificent panoramic view of it and of the sea captivates me. A boulder shaped like a bench invites one to take a seat, and with the warm afternoon sun at my back I sit upon my stone and “think, leg crossed over leg . . .”

The scene at my feet is full of atmosphere. Without question! To the left, the gently rippling water of the harbour basin; to the right, the expanse of the already somewhat livelier Amur Bay. Between the two lies the Muravyov-Amursky Peninsula, on which the city of Vladivostok stands. Looking out past the spit of land that encloses the Golden Horn, one gazes across to the dusky steel-blue of the open sea, and directly before me the sun reflects its rays off the pale cliff faces of the treeless islands that form the extension of the peninsula. To the left in the harbour lie warships and merchant vessels; among the latter, several fly foreign flags, some German, some Japanese. Down there to the left, a massive hull can be seen moored at the pier of the “Volunteer Fleet”. The other merchant ships, insofar as they are not discharging cargo by means of lighters and thus lying at anchor in the harbour, have warped alongside the long public quay that adjoins the railway buildings. The warships anchor further inside the harbour; one is currently undergoing repairs in the large dry dock, which has been pumped completely dry. The warships have only just returned from Amur Bay, where they have been manoeuvring; one of the mighty ironclads is still smoking inside the canal cut through one of the offshore islands to connect Amur Bay with the harbour basin proper. Down on the shore, however, it teems with people, carts, and small boats. A merchant steamer that arrived recently is swarmed by numerous passenger boats rowed by Shandong Chinese.
