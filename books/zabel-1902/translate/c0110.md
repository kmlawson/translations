Not until the afternoon of this day do we enter more open country. The forest, which in places was not felled at all but simply burned down to make room for the railway, recedes. We cross a number of tributaries of the Ussuri over iron bridges, and now see extensive fields and settlements dense with houses—a sign [[p221]] that we are approaching the Amur and its capital, Khabarovsk. The roads between the settlements are reasonably well maintained; we see peasants bringing in the new harvest. A cyclist accompanies the train for a while, which is hardly difficult for him at our speed of twenty-five kilometers an hour. At some distance we also catch sight of the waters of the Ussuri glittering in the sunbeams, and before long the first building of Khabarovsk comes into view: the high-perched barracks of the Ussuri Railway Regiment. We round a rise, and suddenly we see the city spread out below us across three hills. Behind it stretches a broad expanse of water. There the waters of the Ussuri unite with those of the Amur. After a few minutes we come to a halt: Khabarovsk Station!

The station lies approximately 80 meters above the level of the Amur and is about a twenty-minute droshky ride from the city. Hasty farewells are exchanged with fellow travelers, and then everyone sees to securing a droshky as quickly as possible. I take my leave of my general's family, after they have extracted a promise from me to visit them once more for tea before my departure. My carriage takes me down a pleasant valley to the city, which lies roughly halfway up between the station and the Amur. I have been advised to take a room at the “Khabarovsk Gostinnitsa,” purportedly the best of the three existing hotels. Affixed to the balcony are also two signs reading “Hôtel de Chabaroffsk” and “Gasthaus Chabarowsk.” So they cater to Germans and Frenchmen as well. My room costs three rubles a day; it is remarkably clean and furnished almost elegantly. Water is also available in sufficient quantity, as are bed linens and towels. Truly a highlight among my Siberian memories!

The firm of Kunst & Albers in Vladivostok kindly gave me letters of introduction to the head of their local branch, a German, Mr. Langschmidt, and has also already telegraphed ahead to reserve a place for me on the next upriver Amur steamer. I therefore deliver my letter this very day, and am received and advised with downright charming kindness. On this occasion I learn that the steamer does not depart until Sunday, July 21. So I have three days to spare for Khabarovsk!
