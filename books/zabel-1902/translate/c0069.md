How was that possible? Watches had been posted all night; on the schooner itself were six soldiers standing guard. Where had they gone? Inquiries were made, in vain; they had vanished along with the schooner. All further searches proved fruitless. They searched for the vessel out on the high seas, though the wind had freshened during the night, to be sure, and pursuit in an uncertain direction was out of the question. Everyone aboard the cruiser was furious; the fine prize [[p148]] [Illustration: Chaidamat Forest Station (p. 134).]

[[p149]]

had vanished. The commander bore the responsibility for the six missing sailors, and both he and his officers had that dull feeling as though they had drunk far too much champagne in the mess yesterday in honor of the good catch, for which the debts now still had to be paid. Fortunately, Russian naval officers are mostly quite well-to-do men.

The mystery was only solved later. Months later, the cruiser, which had in the meantime been relieved, arrived in Nagasaki. And what does one see? The escaped schooner, with its mockingly grinning captain and its sailors, greeting the Russian warship with “three cheers.” Shortly thereafter, the six Russian sailors also reported back aboard their cruiser. They recounted that on that fateful night they had all suddenly and simultaneously been overpowered by the American sailors, and their mouths immediately gagged so that they could not utter another sound. To avoid any noise, the American captain had cut the ropes with knives, let the vessel drift slowly with the wind under cover of the suddenly descending fog, and then quickly set all sails. Since he knew the coast like a true, noble, honest seaman, he maneuvered his vessel that very night into a hidden bay known to him, and while the Russian cruiser searched for him on the open sea and finally steamed away empty-handed, he waited there for two days and then steered for Nagasaki. During this time the Russian sailors had it quite good on board; they received as much whiskey as they cared to drink, and claimed to have been better fed by the Americans than at the Russian consulate, where they had been turned over by the captain in Nagasaki. In the port of Nagasaki, the Russians could of course do nothing to the American, but they intended to capture him again after leaving the coastal zone. The Americans, however, were prudent enough to ask an American warship that happened to be lying in port for protection. The warship put out of Nagasaki harbor with the schooner and escorted it far enough that it was able to reach its American home port unmolested.
