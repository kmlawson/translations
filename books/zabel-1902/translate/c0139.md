## Chapter VI. Through Transbaikalia.

### 1. To Lake Baikal.

August 18.

It is indeed a pleasant feeling to know that the ribbon of rails before us extends, with only a single interruption, all the way to our home soil and beyond. Now only train travel lies ahead of us, apart from the short stretch across Lake Baikal, which we shall cross on a train ferry. Only 11 days of train travel left. A long journey, to be sure! One would almost shrink from it had one not just passed through the school of suffering on the water. To be sure, it does not make a particularly good initial impression that the railway line has been impassable for the past three days. The rain that set in over the last few days—unfortunately too late for us—has partially washed away the railway embankment along the line, and Russians take plenty of time for repairs. Yesterday the first train ran again, but it was unable to cope with all the passengers who had accumulated in Sretensk in the meantime. We must therefore expect a very substantial crush today. Even so, the concern that prompts my Russian fellow travelers to get up as early as 1 o'clock in the morning and head to the station with their luggage at 5 o'clock initially strikes me as somewhat exaggerated. But as it turns out, this precaution is entirely warranted. Originally, I had considered taking the so-called luxury train, which since last week has been running once a week between Sretensk and Mysovaya, the terminus of the Trans-Baikal Railway on the eastern shore of Lake Baikal. From Irkutsk, the luxury train then departs for Moscow twice a week. However, [[p299]] I would have had to wait three days for that train; so I prefer to take the slower mail train and spend the three-day layover on Irkutsk.

Nikolai Mikhailovich's orderly is at the *Kharbin* promptly at 5 o'clock in the morning with two carts. After I finally return the complaint book to Herr Neumann and pay for my excess baggage, even though we took 13 days instead of the promised eleven, we walk alongside our baggage carts to the ferry. It is a so-called reaction ferry. Attached to a pile anchored in the river some distance upstream is a long wire cable from which the ferry hangs. Making use of the river current and the rudder, it swings automatically back and forth between the two banks. After a long wait, we are finally ferried across and pile our luggage in the baggage room. At 7 o'clock sharp the ticket window opens, and Nikolai Mikhailovich manages to be the first to buy tickets. The crush is indeed quite extraordinary, and while Nikolai Mikhailovich checks the baggage, I reserve a first-class compartment with two berths for the two of us. Checking the baggage of two hundred passengers takes an incredibly long time, even though the baggage clerk never tires of clattering on his calculating machine to determine the baggage charges. After having already been busy for three and a half hours, we gather again around 7:30 with our former traveling companions at the tea table in the waiting room and then set out to take our seats. To our immense astonishment, however, we find our compartment locked, and a subordinate railway official hands us our coats, which I had already hung up inside. At the same time, we learn that the compartment has been requisitioned for a Russian colonel and for the stationmaster. Naturally, we protest very vigorously. The stationmaster is fetched, and Nikolai Mikhailovich parleys with him at length to no avail. Meanwhile, however, all the other seats on the train have been taken as well, and they would leave us behind without a second thought if we did not stand up for our rights with utmost resolve. But although Nikolai Mikhailovich is by no means at a loss for words and throws his full weight as a staff captain of the Semyonovsky Guards Regiment into the scale, he achieves no result. Surely we would be polite enough, the stationmaster remarks, to give precedence to a colonel. When the negotiations stall, Nikolai Mikhailovich resorts to a ruse of war. A few days ago, you see, a senior German official in the diplomatic service, who was traveling with special recommendations from the Governor-General and had been conveyed on the government steamer from Blagoveshchensk to Sretensk, passed through, and the "German minister" had frequently figured in our conversations. For anyone with such recommendations at his disposal, everything looks quite different than it does for the unrecommended traveler; in that case, the Russians do everything possible to make a good impression. Although it would have been easy for me to obtain similar recommendations as well, I had deliberately declined to do so, precisely because I wanted to experience Siberia as it presents itself to an unrecommended traveler. Suddenly I notice the stationmaster making one bow after another before me, and my astonishment grows as he opens the door of the compartment, [[p300]] which he had hitherto desperately defended, and invites both of us with a polite wave of the hand to take possession of our seats. The puzzle is solved very soon. Nikolai Mikhailovich, it turns out, had asked him quite out of the blue when the "German minister" had passed through. The German minister? Oh, he left only yesterday.
