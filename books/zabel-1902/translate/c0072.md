Over time and after lengthy study, it has now been possible to determine the places where whales usually stay. The location of these spots can likewise be traced back to natural causes. They are, namely, mostly places where a current breaks and a kind of dead angle is formed in which the water moves very little. One can observe such points on a small scale in rivers. Imagine a stream of water running against a rock jutting out into the river's course and being pushed aside by the rock. The main current will naturally rush past the rock, but a small portion of the water will be deflected from the rock in a direction opposite to the main current, producing a backflow and a slow eddy in the angle formed by the bank and the rock. In such places one will frequently see pieces of wood, foam, and other objects carried along by the river collecting. If one transfers this phenomenon to ocean currents, one will also find here such angles—or rather, in these dimensions, such zones—in which a circular motion, if not a complete standstill, is produced. Most of the fish are also driven to these points, and since the whale follows the fish, this is the whale ground proper. In the vicinity of such zones, which can naturally be square miles in size, the station is then established, from which the whalers set out to hunt and where the brought-in fish are processed.

At first, these hunting grounds were determined empirically, and only later did people realize their connection to current conditions. Men had set out and searched for whales at random. When they found them, they shot them and marked the exact location on the map. In this way, clearly defined areas emerged for particular seasons, shifting with the months. This method was continued for years by entering the individual yearly zones on another map and superimposing each yearly zone in a different color over the preceding one. In this manner, one obtained regions of greatest density and regions of greatest scarcity. The zones of greatest density—or rather, the greatest density of the entire area—then coincided with the zones described above, created by ocean currents. I do not know whether these connections are known, and have therefore recorded them as specifically Siberian observations.

Our arrival in Gaidamak is an event. Flags have been put out to mark the occasion, and everyone is standing on the landing pier to welcome us. Small wonder! The steamers have not been seen in Gaidamak for eight months. Back then, with the coming of winter, they withdrew into Korean waters, and throughout the winter the vessels lay in Nagasaki [[p154]] for general repairs. Communication between Gaidamak and the outside world is not very easy without the sea route. To be sure, one can reach Vladivostok by land by first riding eight versts to the nearest Russian village; roads and paths do not exist, one simply rides across the heath. In the village, one can obtain post-horses, must travel with them for more than a day, I believe, and then reaches a small station on the Ussuri Railway, from which one can take a train to Vladivostok. And yet Gaidamak is still in the “immediate vicinity of Vladivostok.”

Now, however, the season begins anew; everything has been put in order to receive the guests as well as the whales. In the house of the chief engineer’s wife—he being a German—a good dinner awaits us, and in the cooperage, headed by a Berliner, barrels and vats are being built to hold the train oil that is to come.

Gaidamak possesses a complete factory plant. First of all, there is the large boiling house where the oil is boiled, then the cooperage, an establishment of considerable capacity equipped with the best machinery from Flensburg. Then a smithy, a machine repair shop with equipment for manufacturing tin cans and tin carboys for filling with oil; a sawmill buzzes incessantly, large timber yards stand ready there; in short, one can find a bit of everything, even a small office, a large warehouse, and a store of goods for the use of the officials and workers. Except for a few residential houses, all the buildings are constructed of wood—mostly cedarwood, which must grow at least as densely in the neighboring mountains as it once did on Mount Lebanon. The other kind of wood used here is oak, fine heavy oak, such as we are accustomed to in our native forests.

The most important and most interesting building of all is probably the oil-boiling house. Its interior equipment consists mainly of four large, upright boiling caldrons. Each individual one holds the cut-up fat and meat of one whale. No distinction is made here between blubber oil and meat oil. The boiling time is about 24 hours, so that four whales can be processed within a single day. When the whale is brought in, it is first hauled onto the so-called flensing plan in front of the boiling house by means of wire ropes and steam winches. This flensing plan is nothing other than an open-air wooden floor sloping down toward the sea. It is protected against rotting by the oil that has soaked into it over time. On this flensing plan the fish is completely cut up; meat and blubber are stripped from the ribs in long strips, together with the relatively thin skin. For the time being, the bones are stacked beside the flensing plan. As soon as the boiling work is not pressing too hard, they too are boiled out, as they are likewise heavily permeated with oil. Then, owing to the facilities for producing bone meal still lacking for the present, they are shipped to Japan and China, where they are ground up by the farmers and used as fertilizer for the rice fields. The bones are often of enormous girth and almost immense weight. However, they are very porous, and therefore—with the exception of the harder but very brittle jawbones—cannot be used for any bone work.
