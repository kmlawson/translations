As I am interested in viewing the ruins of Aigun close up, I ask the captain to put in to shore. He does me the favor, and I go ashore together with the staff surgeon. The sight that meets my eyes is one of the uttermost devastation. Exactly the same precision in destruction as previously along the Taku–Tientsin line, where the Cossacks had likewise dealt so thoroughly with the Chinese *fanzi* that not one stone remained upon another. We forge a path through the rank weeds of a former vegetable garden. The rapid growth of vegetation has sufficed to overgrow even the former streets with grass and thistles. Here and there a foundation and a scorched, red-burned remnant of a wall jut upward; heaps of shards and charred timbers lie along the street alignments. The main street ran parallel to the Amur. We wander through the ruins. Burrs and grass seeds cling to our clothing. As souvenirs we take along a few shell splinters and some broken potsherds. It is a sorrowful scene of destruction that etches itself into our minds.

From Aigun, a carriage road leads south, soon reaching the valley of the Nonni, the longest tributary of the Sungari. Here it already encounters more densely populated areas, in the center of which lies the town of Mergen. The road then follows the course of the Nonni via Tsitsihar toward southern Manchuria. It is no coincidence that the advance of Chinese-Manchurian culture took place northward in precisely this direction. For, following the river valley of the Nonni to the north and continuing further in the valley of the Zeya, a peninsula, as it were, of good arable soil juts into the surrounding sea of steppes. Indeed, Russian colonization advancing from the north, from Yakutsk, also first reached the Amur valley within the reach of this cultivated zone.

We have hardly lingered ashore twenty minutes when the ship's whistle sounds, urging our return. And so we hurry back to the bank. Meanwhile the whistle has urged us on even more insistently, and as we arrive below, we see the boat that brought us across just pushing off from the steamer again to fetch us. As it draws nearer, we catch sight [[p253]] inside of Russian officers unknown to us. They come closer and land. We greet them briefly with the usual handshake and prepare to board our craft to be ferried across. The officers seem inclined to strike up a conversation. One captain in particular speaks at me insistently. I do not understand him. The staff surgeon translates for me in his flawed German, and from it I gather that the captain is particularly interested in my camera. I show him the camera, turning it to one side and then to the other. He takes hold of it, and to my horror I notice that he handles it very carelessly and is about to let light get to the films. I therefore take the camera back from him with gentle force. Meanwhile, however, the staff surgeon translates for me that the officer wishes to possess my photographic plates; and while I consider myself exceedingly polite for engaging so thoroughly with the stranger officer, it only now dawns on me that he wants nothing other than to confiscate the photographs I took in Aigun. It really is a bit much that one should not be allowed to photograph in a territory that has as yet by no legal or constitutional act been ceded to Russia. Yet indignation is of no use here; only cunning and slyness will do. For whoever has the might has the right. First, I therefore try appealing to the facts of international law, declaring that, firstly, I am a German; secondly, we are on Chinese soil; and thirdly, Russia has no right to curtail the liberties of citizens of a foreign state on foreign soil. But the captain merely declares to me, short and sweet: “*Nichevo!*” What that means, I know full well, and for my part I think “*Nichevo*” as well. Since this attempt has misfired, it now comes down purely to that shrewdness more or less common to all nations, if I am at least to prevent the officer from simply taking my roll of film out of the camera, exposing it to the sun's rays, and thus ruining my fine pictures of the Amur voyage along with those of Aigun. I therefore become very polite again and have it conveyed to him that I give him my assurance that I have taken no more than three shots in Aigun. Would he not be so kind as not to ruin the rest of the films for me, but rather—if he must—take the roll with him and cut off the three Aigun pictures in the dark. In the end, he is no monster after all; he claims that he is a photographer himself and thus understands the matter, and says he will cut off only the three Aigun pictures. So I wind up my roll of film, remove it from the camera, and hand it over to him most ceremoniously with a bow. He promises to have the roll of film delivered to Kunst & Albers in Blagoveshchensk as early as tomorrow. Then we take our leave of one another and shake hands as if nothing had happened. Back on board, I am greeted with shouts and teased thoroughly by my Russian fellow travelers. To anticipate matters, I should add right here that in this instance too the old proverb proved true: “he who laughs last, laughs best”—and the latter I did. When the roll of film is returned to me the next day, I hurry immediately to a Japanese photographer to develop it and see what has actually become of my pictures. I am not a little surprised when developing reveals that all the pictures are still [[p254]] on the film—even my pictures of Aigun! It had been too much trouble for the good captain to develop the entire roll and then cut off the right negatives. So he had simply snipped off the last three exposures, and these had not been exposed at all.
