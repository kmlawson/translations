After five kilometers, the first station, Pervaya Rechka! According to the original plan, the terminus of the Ussuri Railway was to be located here, connected to the landing stage on the Golden Horn only by a spur line. After this was later abandoned, this station serves as an auxiliary station for Vladivostok. It teems with military personnel, as the barracks of the troops stationed near Vladivostok are located here. After a stop of twenty minutes—Russians generally do not manage with anything less—we travel on. The countryside remains consistently pleasant and even becomes idyllic at times, as it does eleven kilometers further on, at the next station, Sedanka, where most of the country villas of the Vladivostok merchants are situated. One large building is particularly striking; it is Suvorov’s match factory, already [[p205]] mentioned earlier, which, despite protective tariffs against Japanese competition, had to go into liquidation on account of Russian slovenliness. At the next station, Khilkovo, we see large dumps of coal, which is mined here and used to fire the railway. The operation was set in motion by the railway administration itself. Vladivostok obtains the bulk of its coal supply from Sakhalin. For that matter, Siberia’s coal deposits have by no means been tackled yet; very little is known as yet about their extent and yield, and, in addition to numerous other obstacles, a lack of workers in particular would impede large-scale extraction, and all the more acutely the deeper the mines lie in the interior of the country. For the present, both the Trans-Siberian Railway, with the exception of the Ussuri Railway, and the entire Siberian steam navigation*) burn wood without exception. So long, then, as Siberia’s timber stocks hold out—with which a sheerly incredible reckless overexploitation is practiced—there is no compelling reason to exploit the coal reserves, and if a private entrepreneur wished to begin mining coal even now, he would hardly find it profitable, since the engines are not equipped for coal-firing, and the transition to it would entail a complete reconstruction of the boilers and fireboxes on both the steamers and the railway, which could not be carried out without disproportionately high costs. For the engines are mostly imported from abroad, built specifically for wood-firing, and there is, above all, a lack of marine engineers and shipyards or machine-repair workshops to carry out such work. But these facts suffice to preserve Siberia’s coal wealth for decades to come, and in them lies also the main explanation for why Russia has not yet approached the exploitation of these treasures, even though they have long since been surveyed and known. But to transport the coal from the interior to the coast by rail or by water and to offer it to the East Asian market, the costs would be far too substantial—even if the waterway alone were to be used—to be able to compete with cheap, if not particularly valuable, Japanese coal. In my opinion, that would be possible only for coal in the immediate vicinity of the coast, especially also for the quite good Sakhalin coal. For the present, however, this is not yet mined in such quantities and so cheaply as to make itself felt on the East Asian coal market in any way other than by removing Russian Manchuria from it as a sales market. —

The region through which we are now travelling is more densely wooded. We turn away from the sea; the railway, which until then had maintained a fairly easterly heading, now turns predominantly to the northwest; at a sharp bend, Amur Bay disappears from view, and the line slowly ascends the gently rolling hill country that confines the left bank of the Suifun. The dense spruce forests over which the eye ranges provided the material for the railway ties over which we glide, and the grass-covered slopes, [[p206]] on which massed sawn-off, but even more so burnt-out tree stumps and here and there a solitary surviving giant of a tree tower up, stretch along the railway line, only to be bordered at some distance by dark-green forests that lie there in the deepest hues of ink. To the left the valley opens up, and soon one distinguishes a narrow silver ribbon winding through a depression. We turn into the Suifun Valley and arrive at Razdolnoye on the Suifun, which is navigable from this point on.
