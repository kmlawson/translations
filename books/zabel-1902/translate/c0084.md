The history of the conquest of Siberia by Russia is rich in features characteristic of Russianness as such. To understand it, it is not enough merely to commit to memory a few dates and the names of a few pioneers to whom, in a purely pragmatic account, the narrative of events is usually tied. These alone do not provide an accurate picture. For what, after all, does it matter what name was borne by this or that Cossack ataman who undertook this or that expedition to determine the course of this or that river, or of what consequence is it whether one knows the place where some insignificant skirmish with natives occurred, or the like? Actual fighting marks essentially only the beginning and the end of Russia's advance into Siberia: in the west, when it was a matter of gaining a firm foothold beyond the Urals, and in the east, when a unified and organized state entity, China, presented an effective “halt” to the Russian advance that had to be overcome. Within these bounds, however, every fur hunter, every Cossack demanding tribute from the natives of a lower cultural level, carried the borders of his fatherland farther out toward the regions of the north and east, where only the waters of the Pacific and the Arctic Ocean finally called a halt to his steps. Siberia was not a land that had to be conquered, but one that needed only to be taken into possession. Proof that the acquisition of Siberia by the Russians proceeded relatively quickly is shown by the very fact that, barely more than fifty years after Yermak's bands had first crossed the Urals (1581), Cossacks had already reached the Sea of Okhotsk and founded on its shores the town of the same name. Thus, as early as the year 1638, Russia possessed a port on the Pacific Ocean. The struggle that unfolded over the centuries that followed, and which, with frequently shifting fortunes, ultimately led to Russia recently reaching the goal of its East Asian expansionist policy, had as its object the push toward more hospitable regions, toward the south—which practically speaking amounted to the struggle for the most direct possible connection with the sea, and indeed with a sea open year-round, an ice-free sea. Yet this task and this struggle entered the consciousness of Russian policy only a little over forty years ago. Until then, the government had been pushed forward more by the impulsive advance of Russian subjects—which it would often enough have preferred to restrain rather than encourage—than it had driven the advance itself. East Asian expansionist policy was consciously pursued politically and economically by the Russian government only after Muravyov was appointed Governor-General of Eastern Siberia, and through him the acquisition of the Amur region was systematically resumed. One can therefore speak of a Russian colonial policy in Siberia only since that time; it was initiated around the year 1860. And there is one more thing to add: for objective reasons, one can essentially speak of a Russian colonial policy only in the [[p177]] Amur region. By its nature, this territory is indeed an economic, political, and geographical colony for Russia, whereas the rest of Siberia, Western as well as Eastern Siberia, lacks the geographical and economic prerequisites to be regarded as anything other than an essential part of Russia. The Ural Mountains are a historical boundary between Europe and Asia, not a climatic or general geographical one; but since it is in any case impossible to place Russia culturally on a level with the European states that stand, on average, at a much higher standard, it ultimately makes no difference if we thereby assign Russia the position that actually belongs to it in geographical and cultural terms, namely that of a European-Asian state. The reasons why the Amur territory is to be distinguished as a colony from the rest of the Siberian possessions in Asia will, it is hoped, become clear from what follows. First, however, I should like to return once more to old Genghis Khan, and with him to the earliest beginnings of Russia's acquisition of Siberia. Before we penetrate into the colossus of Siberia itself, it will nevertheless be necessary to touch briefly upon a few historical questions, since without this much of what we shall see and hear would likely remain incomprehensible.
