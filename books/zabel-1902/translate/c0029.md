The foregoing decrees came into effect on the day of their publication. Since then, everything provided for in them has been put in place. A vast army of officials is stationed in Newchwang, but up to the time I arrived there, the Russians had not yet been able to restore safety and order. It has remained a matter of fine words. Brigandage continues to flourish in the city, and Russian soldiers occasionally join the Chinese bandits. Friction arose between the civil governor and the garrison commander; the two departments therefore do not work sufficiently hand in hand; on the official side the word "provisional" is constantly on everyone's lips, and yet the very first decree already speaks of quarterly reckonings by the tax inspector, and the officials of the civil government are engaged for years to come. Russia is thus contemplating quite a lengthy provisional arrangement. Meanwhile, negotiations are already under way with China. Two versions of a Manchurian agreement have become known. Officially, Russia denies the existence of such an agreement. Yet in fact the annexation of Manchuria has already been carried out; no one [[p65]] can deny it, I have seen it with my own eyes*). But the result of the annexation is not a particularly gratifying one. How, indeed, can anything good come of an administration in which it is bound to happen that the chief of police is dismissed after a short time as incompetent, that the entire police force is driven off as untrustworthy, that the police assistant is banished to Sakhalin because, with the help of his Chinese interpreters, he extorted from a Chinese

wholesale merchant nearly 30,000 taels over time? How can an administration succeed when its entire regime is based on the exclusive mediation of roguish Chinese interpreters, former mafoos and coolies from Vladivostok, and when finally, [[p66]] to cap it all, the civil administrator's own salary is reduced by way of punishment because his revenues are so large that he knows no other way to invest them than in gambling?

15 February.

Today I am sending a long dispatch home, in which I describe conditions as I found them. It is not possible for me to transmit this dispatch directly over the wire here in Newchwang, because instances of postal censorship give reason to fear that the dispatch will never reach Europe in its true wording. The telegraph line to Port Arthur, the so-called Imperial Chinese Telegraph, is in Russian hands. I am therefore forced to send my dispatch in a sealed envelope under a cover address to Chefoo, in order to have it sent from there by wire.
