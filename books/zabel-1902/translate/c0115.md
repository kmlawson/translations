Along the fairly steeply sloping bank of the Amur stretches the city park, in the middle of which stands the bronze statue of Muravyov. Adjoining the city garden are most of the official buildings, such as the Governor-General’s residence, a library building, and the very rich [[p231]] Natural History Museum. This is substantially larger and incomparably richer than the one in Vladivostok, a true treasure trove for the naturalist and ethnographer who has made Siberia and its native tribes his special field of study. Today I spent no less than four hours orienting myself within it and taking photographs. I was even permitted to enter the hall reserved especially for Siberian gold finds and antique trophies from the first battles with the Chinese. This hall is generally inaccessible to the public and is guarded from profane eyes like the Ark of the Covenant, although there is no reason at all for this. The hall contains no particular secrets. Already the museum suffers in several departments from overcrowding. Nevertheless, the arrangement makes a considerably better impression than in Vladivostok, although in terms of preservation a good deal occurs that would make a European conservator tear his hair out. For instance, the museum attendant was instructed to arrange a number of pieces that I wished to photograph and that were not standing in the right light. I intended to take pictures of some very rare ancestral figures of the Ainu. One of them would not stand. Thereupon the good museum attendant simply takes a long nail, uses a second ancestral figure as a hammer, and with it nails the first firmly to the floor. My protest meets with no appreciation. The man smiles, evidently thinking I had not wished to trouble him, and makes a gesture as if to say: “Oh, please, I am very glad to do it for you!”

Beside the museum building stands the considerably smaller building of the Nicholas Library, which contains 40,000 volumes, mostly Russian and French literature. German literature is hardly represented at all. In the reading room a number of newspapers are laid out, among them several East Asian English ones. The most recent bear a date from — May! In certain papers one sees thick black brushstrokes. Beneath these black strokes stand subversive, liberal, and other wicked things that the Russian citizen must not read. When I think of how much subversive material I have absorbed out there in the wicked world, which no censor’s loving brush covered up with printer’s ink for me, a shudder comes over me.

21 July.

Today my Amur journey is to begin at last. Yesterday evening I was invited to Mr. and Mrs. Langschmidt’s and learned that a cabin ticket has been procured for me. Unfortunately, during this persistently and extraordinarily low water level, the large and tolerably comfortably appointed passenger steamers of the “Amur Steamship Company for Passenger Traffic and Trade” cannot run. A so-called tugboat, which does not draw too much water, must therefore make the trip. I am told that it is a far from pleasant steamer. But I still prefer to take the next opportunity. Who can tell what else might intervene to detain me against my will. Unfortunately, the eagerly awaited news has still not arrived that in the headwaters of the Shilka and the Argun [[p232]] rain has fallen, so that the water calamity will probably continue for a long time yet. Moreover, it has been incredibly hot over the past two days, hotter than I have ever experienced, even during summer travels in the tropics. In the tropics one feels the heat less because of its high moisture content than the dry warmth here, which is followed at night by a fairly sharp drop in temperature. Throughout the day, the light-colored clay soil reflects the heat rays quite intensely, and at night readily releases the stored heat again. Hence the contrast is all the more palpable. This morning it is 35 degrees Réaumur in the shade—certainly surprising for someone accustomed to associating the concept of Siberia with the idea of great cold! On the water it will hopefully be cooler!
