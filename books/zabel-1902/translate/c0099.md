Muravyov was meanwhile untiringly active in settling the entire length of the left bank of the Amur. Opposite Aigun, three kilometres upstream at the confluence of the Zeya with the Amur—the site of old Chinese Aigun from the era of Albazin—Ust-Zeysk, the later Blagoveshchensk, had been founded, and the postal line between Ust-Strelka and Nikolayevsk was completed.

Putyatin's insistence ultimately met with success, which manifested itself, however, in a different direction than he had intended, and was considerably accelerated by the outbreak of the grave Taiping Rebellion, which shook the Chinese state structure, and by China's subsequent war with England and France. Under the pressure of these circumstances, China was anxious not to bring about inconvenient complications in the north as well. Yet without harming its position vis-à-vis England and France, it could not negotiate in Shanghai. Therefore, it preferred to shift the venue of the negotiations deep into the interior of the continent, to Aigun. On 16 May 1858, after only six days of negotiations, Muravyov concluded a treaty here with the Chinese plenipotentiaries, which ceded the entire left bank of the Amur to Russia and declared the coastal territory between the Tatar Sea and the Ussuri, as well as down to the Korean border, to be the joint possession of Russia and China. Navigation on the Amur, Ussuri, and Sungari was to be permitted exclusively to vessels of both nations; by contrast, free commercial activity on these rivers was proclaimed. The precise delimitation of the border on the coast was intentionally reserved for a later occasion. It took place just two years later in the Treaty of Beijing. Putyatin had meanwhile—just eight days after the conclusion of the Convention of Aigun—finally signed a commercial treaty for Russia in Tianjin, almost simultaneously with the English and French negotiators, and in 1859 General Ignatyev was sent to Beijing as envoy. While the latter was now negotiating the definitive border delimitation in Beijing, Muravyov toured the newly acquired coastal region and discovered Peter the Great Bay, where he founded Vladivostok, and Posyet Bay. Then he provided Ignatyev with the necessary information, on the basis of which the latter, in concluding the treaty, insisted that the border be drawn even further south than the coastal river Suifen stipulated in the Convention of Aigun, all the way down to the Korean border. China thereby lost its access in the north to the Sea of Japan, its eastern access to the sea. In this way, eastern Manchuria too, the ancestral homeland of the Chinese ruling dynasty, became a Russian province, and it was only a matter of time before the rest of Manchuria met the same fate.
