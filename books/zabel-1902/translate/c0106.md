### 2. In the Ussuri Valley.

18 July.

The region through which we are hurrying occupies, climatically, a peculiar intermediate position between the tropics and the cold zone. I mentioned earlier that, on geographical and climatological grounds, the Amur basin must be described as Russia's true colonial territory. While the general conditions for agriculture and other forms of cultivation in the rest of Siberia do not differ essentially from those of European Russia, and in particular the Governor-Generalships of Western and Eastern Siberia—in those zones that can be considered for cultivation at all—are to be regarded as the direct, broad continuation of European Russia, this holds true only to a very limited degree once one has crossed the watershed between the Yenisei and the Amur, and loses its validity entirely as soon as one considers the southeastern territories of the Primorskaya on their own. We have already seen in the political acquisition of these regions that intervention from the east, from the sea, was necessary for this. The first Russian invasion of the Amur region, which took place entirely by land, had failed. It only succeeded when Russian

[Illustration: Khabarovsk: Cathedral (p. 210).] [[p213]] power reached around Asia and thus met the driving force pressing forward from the west. This process has, in addition to its political significance, a profound economic one as well. We can summarize it briefly by saying that the Amur region gravitates toward the east, toward the sea, while the western parts of Siberia lean toward the mother country. Were we to visualize this on a balance beam, its fulcrum would have to be conceived as resting on the crest of the mountain range that stretches under various names from Central Asia all the way to the Bering Sea. The settlement of the Amur region, too, took place to a considerable extent by sea, and would have had to do so even more had an artificial bridging of the natural divide not taken place via the Great Siberian Tract and, in more recent times, by the railway. Except for a vanishingly small amount of transport passing through Sretensk, the Amur region is supplied with its European necessities from the sea, namely via Vladivostok and to a small extent also via Nikolayevsk, and the disproportionately smaller exports produced by the region likewise take the same route, with few exceptions. As regards first of all the trade of the natives, whose principal livelihood is hunting, it is completely dependent on the immigrant Chinese. Wherever Chinese settle across the globe, it is as characteristic of the equally pronounced commercial superiority of this race as it is of the ineptitude of the Russian people in making the goods of their colonial land permanently serviceable to themselves by any means other than predatory exploitation, that the entire fur trade of this region is in Chinese hands. The slant-eyed merchants have kept their Russian competitors away from the natives by paying the latter advances on their annual fur catch, and doing so directly in kind. In this way it has come about that the natives, although politically subjects of the Russians, are economically dependent exclusively on the Chinese, who thus virtually monopolize the fur trade. This phenomenon explains why by far the greatest part of the annual fur harvest of the Amur region, as well as of the parts lying north of it inhabited by Siberian tribes, does not flow to Russia and Europe, but to Manchuria and North China, where fur is the universal winter garment. Since, however, Khabarovsk is now basically the only fur market in Siberia for the nobler furs—sable, ermine, and blue fox having retreated to the northeast from western Siberia, which is more densely settled and has been exploited by trappers for centuries—the Russian himself is in turn forced to meet his own fur needs from the Chinese. Only the trade in finer seal and otter pelts coming from Kamchatka remains predominantly in Russian hands, but it travels by sea. Anyone, therefore, who believes that one can still buy furs well and, above all, cheaply in Siberia is mistaken. In Moscow, furs are considerably cheaper than in Irkutsk and in Khabarovsk! This even goes so far that Siberia no longer covers even its own needs for fine furs. Later on, in Irkutsk, I bought a ready-made genuine Siberian sable fur at great expense. Later it turned out that the sable had never seen Siberia beforehand, but rather—as the stamp on the back of the pelt also proved—had roamed in its lifetime among the boulders of the great Canadian plain [[p214]]. The fur had then been tanned by a furrier on Leipzig's Brühl and sold to Moscow. From there it had made its way to Irkutsk, and from there was brought back as genuine Siberian sable to Russia, Germany, England, and—should chance have it—perhaps even to Canada.
