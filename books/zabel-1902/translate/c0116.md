Since early morning the bells have been ringing; military personnel in dress uniform can be seen in large numbers on the street, singly and in columns; military bands and color companies march past my windows to resounding music along the main street, the Muravyov-Amurskaya. Today is the Sunday on which the thanksgiving celebration of the Manchurian troops who took part in the recent fighting with the Chinese is to take place. The main part of the jubilee celebration will be a parade in which all the banners captured from the Chinese, nearly two hundred of them, will be carried along. This will be followed by a large open-air service of thanksgiving. For this purpose, a wooden pavilion draped in the Russian colors has been erected in front of the cathedral. Afterwards, the banners are to be hung in the cathedral. I attended the celebration. The parade proceeded according to regulations and with great participation on the part of the rural population. After the parade, with a display of the tremendous pomp of the Greek Catholic Church, a bishop appeared, accompanied by a retinue of numerous acolytes, choirboys, and priests. The ceremony lasted more than two hours. I had intended to hold out until the end. But although I had pulled my thick pith helmet onto my head, I could not endure standing in the enormous heat. Unlike me, the other participants all held out and endured the two-hour sermon. The peasant folk and the soldiers stood bareheaded for most of the ceremonies—for two hours—at +35 degrees Réaumur in the shade—no, they stood at about 50 degrees in the blazing sun! What thick skulls they must have, since of all the participants in this procedure only a single one was struck by sunstroke—in majorem gloriam ecclesiae! The counterpart to this: on January 6 every year, the waters of the Amur are blessed. January 6 is usually one of the coldest days in this region. A hole is hacked into the thick ice of the Amur until the flowing water is exposed. Immediately after the blessing, the fanatical peasants leap into the holy water, only to stand there moments later with their clothes frozen stiff. Holy reason!

At 5 o'clock in the afternoon my trunks are driven to the landing stage, my hotel bill is paid, the tips are distributed, the postcards prudently brought along from Vladivostok are dropped into the mailbox, and Mr. Langschwadt kindly comes to pick me up from the hotel in [[p233]] his carriage to drive me to the landing stage. Down there lies the ship, a side-wheel paddle steamer of little confidence-inspiring appearance. Mr. Langschwadt attends to the shipping of my luggage, the freight clerk clatters on his calculating machine until he knows how much the excess freight to Blagoveshchensk comes to, and I pay. Then my companion recommends me most warmly to the captain. The two young Germans also arrive to bid me farewell, and finally Mr. Lübben, who had received and guided me so kindly, arrives as well, and we exchange many a handshake and heartfelt farewell. After half an hour, our steamer, the *Sergey Dukhovskoy*, is already plying above the mouth of the Ussuri.
