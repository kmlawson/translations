## Chapter III. Russia in Manchuria.

### 1. How Manchuria Became Russian.

7–14 February.

To assess Russian actions in East Asia, it is of interest to examine, in a concrete case, how the Tsarist Empire conducts frontier policy.

Several months elapsed between the outbreak of the Boxer disturbances in the province of Zhili and in Manchuria. This fact has given rise to a variety of conjectures and suspicions regarding Russia. One must grant our kindred neighbours across the Channel that they know exceedingly well how to expose the vulnerabilities revealed by the policies of other powers. From such operations to more or less substantiated suspicions is then but a single step. On the English side, it was first asserted that the Russians had done their part to cause the unrest to break out in Manchuria as well, and that they deliberately refrained from pacifying the country in order to be able to claim that it remained persistently unsafe and that the presence of troops was therefore absolutely necessary. This was then to be continued until people had grown accustomed to Russia being entrenched in Manchuria. And given the short memory with which foreign policy operates nowadays, that is by no means difficult to achieve. Complete clarity will probably never be shed on the Russian manoeuvres.

The first attack by the Boxers on the foreign settlement in Newchwang took place on 4 August 1900; about 150 Boxers appeared on the small “bund” of the foreign settlement [[p58]] at a barricade manned by the Volunteer Corps and attacked the small garrison. The affair must have been comparatively harmless, for the Chinese, who at first merely shouted and performed their dances, were allowed to come right up to the barricade. As before the single combats of Homeric heroes, a battle of words ensued at first; the Chinese leader boasted and jeered that they should go ahead and shoot, as he was invulnerable anyway, etc. He even climbed onto the barricade with theatrical words and brandished his knife, until a volunteer seized him by the arm and pulled him over to the other side, where he was bound. With that, however, a thirst for action seems to have come over Newchwang's volunteer militia as well, for now they began to open fire. Hardly had the shooting begun, however, when the band of Boxers already wheeled about and took to their heels. Ten dead remained on the field. After some time, a detachment of Russian marines appeared. They had nothing left to do, except that they opened rapid fire on one fellow who had jumped into the river out of terror—fire that took effect. After this cheap victory, they took the captured Boxer leader, marched in triumph with him to the large square where the government yamen stands, tied him firmly to a stone, and a “squad” of Cossacks and volunteers, about fifty men, fired a volley at the prisoner from a distance of about 100 (!) metres. When that had been done, a mandarin came walking along, positioned himself beside the executed man, and wanted to say something. They silenced him by forcing him to take the corpse of the Boxer leader onto his back and throw it into the river a few hundred metres away.

Towards 9 o'clock the Boxers had regrouped and joined forces with Chinese soldiers. They prowled around in the reeds and sedge grass behind the foreign settlement. Four hundred Russian marines, five hundred Cossacks, and several companies of infantry engaged them and drove the Chinese back into the city. Throughout the afternoon, two Russian gunboats then shelled the Chinese city, a bombardment answered by the Chinese with machine-gun and infantry fire. But this entire bombardment was not much more than a farce. No damage was done to the city; rather, they shot over it on principle—ostensibly to hit the fleeing enemy, but in fact to spare the Chinese city, because the Russo-Chinese Bank, which evidently has strong interests in the city, declared that a regular bombardment would cause it the greatest damage.

Peculiar was the behaviour of the Daotai*) in this matter. During the Boxer attack in the morning, he sent word to the Russian commander requesting protection for his own person, specifically against Chinese soldiers and Boxers. When a detachment of Russian soldiers marched up to provide him with the requested guard for his yamen, he had, however, long since vanished from Yingkou with all his valuables and departed for Tianzhuangtai.

As a result of the mediation of several European merchants residing in Newchwang, the bombardment was halted in the late afternoon. Several intrepid men, under the leadership [[p59]] of the head of the firm Bush Bros., Mr H. A. Bush, went into the Chinese city and sought to get hold of the leaders of the Chinese guilds, as well as a Chinese government official. They succeeded in this, and so that very evening an agreement was concluded between the Russians and the Chinese according to which the bombardment was to cease, whereas the guild leaders, on the other hand, undertook to ensure that the weapons located in the Chinese city were handed over by 10 o'clock the following morning.
