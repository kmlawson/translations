The isolated location of the city, however, also led politically to a catastrophe that stands fairly unique in world history in its kind and scope. It will be remembered that at the beginning of the Chinese campaign, the news passed through the press of all countries, with the exception of Russia, that near Blagoveshchensk the Russians had driven no fewer than 5,000 Chinese into the Amur. At the time, this news aroused a storm [[p258]] [Illustration: Landing place near Blagoveshchensk (p. 238).]

[[p259]]

of indignation among all humanely minded people.

It was that same General Gripsky to whom I have just been introduced who gave the order for that deed. Upon his head the entire wrath of the world was discharged. I took pains to see clearly in this undoubtedly terrible affair, and discussed it both in Khabarovsk and in Blagoveshchensk with a wide variety of figures and eyewitnesses. The facts that could be established are briefly as follows. When the news arrived of the outbreak of the Boxer disturbances in Zhili, which also threatened the young Russian possessions in southern Manchuria, Governor-General Grodekov in Khabarovsk issued the order that all available troops stationed in northern Manchuria and along the Chinese border were to be moved immediately to southern Manchuria and to the Trans-Manchurian railway line. The entire garrison of Blagoveshchensk was also imprudently withdrawn, leaving in the city only about 150 men of so-called police troops, who, moreover, were not even armed with modern rifles. This measure was undoubtedly the cause from which what followed arose essentially as a mere consequence. Opposite Blagoveshchensk [[p260]] China assembled a fairly considerable military force. The entire right bank of the Amur from the village of Sakhalin down to Aigun was occupied by troops. The Chinese fired upon the Russian steamers maintaining traffic to Stretensk and Khabarovsk, thus cutting off Blagoveshchensk’s supplies along the only possible route. The Chinese troops, however, did not remain in an observational posture, but began already from the right bank of the Amur to shell the city in earnest. It is equally clear that with 150 police soldiers it was impossible to suppress a potential uprising of the Chinese in Blagoveshchensk itself, much less, therefore, to repel a possible attack across the river. It is hard to say how he could have done this otherwise in the best manner—one that took into account both the security of the Russians and the humane sentiments of contemporaries—than by imprisoning all the Chinese who were in the city. On the other hand, it would also have been impossible to intern and support the roughly 5,000 Chinese inside or outside the city, because guarding them would have deprived the city of still more defenders. Consequently, the only remaining possibility was to dispatch the Chinese to the other side of the river. But that side, as noted, was occupied by Chinese troops. Had the Russians now placed boats at the disposal of the 5,000 Chinese to cross over to the other bank of the river, nothing would have provided a guarantee that the Chinese military,

[Illustration: A floating department store on the upper Amur (p. 261).]

which had very few craft at its disposal, would not have seized the opportunity to reach the other bank. Consequently, the Chinese were led to a spot situated upriver where a ford was supposedly located, and were told to see that they got across to the other side. The soldiers drove those who hesitated into the river with fixed bayonets. Of the 5,000 people, according to unanimous estimates, only about 40 reached the other bank alive; the rest drifted down the river as corpses toward the Sea of Okhotsk. So much for the account from Russian sources.

Whether this measure can now be justified on human and political grounds is difficult to say. On the Russian side, the lives and property of 35,000 subjects—indeed the entire position on the Amur, won with such great effort—were at stake. A drastic measure also seems to have been unavoidably necessary under the conditions of the time, and despite [[p263]] the monstrous cruelty involved in sacrificing the lives of 5,000 people, no other way out seems to have remained. Indeed, the resolve to do so, if dictated not by fear but by cool deliberation, could lay claim to tragic greatness.
