Men of the kind described were also the companions of Yermak, whom he had induced to cross with him from the Don over to the Volga, the river that, since the conquest [[p184]] of Astrakhan and Kazan, had developed into the most important trade route across the Russian continent. Yermak’s bands soon became a general scourge here. Navigation ultimately suffered so severely from rampant banditry that the merchants lodged complaints with the tsar. Ivan therefore outlawed the daring Cossack captain along with his Cossacks—among whom various fugitives and rabble whose pasts were not questioned had in the meantime gathered—and dispatched troops to rid the Volga of the troublesome pirates. Under the pressure of these measures, Yermak preferred to shift the scene of his activity further upriver, and thus he gradually arrived at the mouth of the Kama. He also moved up this river and reached his closer homeland, the estates of the Stroganovs. There can scarcely be any doubt that the newcomers continued their trade here as well, and would soon have become a severe burden to the Stroganov estates. The latter therefore killed two birds with one stone by taking Yermak’s bands and Yermak himself, some 800*) men in all, into their service. They thereby freed their own estates from a scourge, but at the same time extended their power beyond the Urals, having obtained authorization to do so a short time earlier through a special rescript from Ivan. To be sure, he had had in mind only the defense of Russian territories, not an offensive. The Stroganovs were thus playing a game of their own; when Ivan heard that they had taken the outlawed and notorious brigands into their service and intended to attack tribes that were allegedly willing to pay tribute, he was greatly incensed and ordered Yermak’s immediate recall. Yet news from the Urals to Moscow and messages back to the Urals took months in those days, and by the time the tsar’s order reached the Stroganovs, it was too late. The ataman was already beyond the Urals. The Stroganovs played va banque and won. Yermak sailed with his men in boats as far up the Chusovaya as possible, then had the boats dragged overland across the gentle heights of the Ural Mountains until he reached the Tura, and with it the river basin of the Ob. This occurred in 1580. In the following two years, he conquered the Ostyak realm, the former Yugra, which had already acknowledged Russian suzerainty in the time of Yadigar. It had in the meantime been conquered by the Tatar khan Kuchum and was ruled from his capital, Isker or Sibir, situated on the Tobol. The city of Sibir, in the vicinity of which Tobolsk later arose, gave the entire land all the way to the Pacific Ocean the name “Siberia.” Yermak sent word of his victories not only to the Stroganovs but also to the tsar, requested amnesty for himself and his comrades, and offered the tsar in return a new kingdom that only awaited his voivodes. The amnesty was granted; as a special token of tsarist favor, the former pirate captain received from Ivan a cloak that had adorned the shoulders of the ruler of all the Russias himself, along with a substantial gift of money. Soon the voivode arrived as well, and Yermak meanwhile conquered with his Cossacks the land extending to the Irtysh. On one of his expeditions he fell [[p185]] into the hands of his old blind enemy Kuchum, and drowned in the Irtysh while trying to save himself by swimming. This occurred in August 1581.

His death was a great loss for the Russian cause. But the spell had been broken, the stone set rolling. The fame of Yermak’s deeds had attracted a significant influx of people who had nothing to lose, but all the more to hope to gain. In the meantime, the Tatars fell into fighting among themselves, and it was relatively easy for the regular Russian troops, who soon marched in, to deal with them one by one. The Cossacks, meanwhile, ranged ever further; soon they were on the Yenisei, soon on the Lena; nowhere did they encounter any further substantial resistance on the part of the natives. Isker or Sibir was the key to a land of many thousands of square miles in whose forests the sable dwelt, and from which news of other riches, including gold and ivory, soon reached the West. Yet the fur hunter, the “tribute-collecting” Cossack, and the gold prospector were followed by the merchant, and the in part quite dismal social conditions in Russia entailed by serfdom made Siberia appear as the land of freedom, to which peasants and serfs who had fled bondage soon escaped as well. Thus the first agricultural colonies arose in Siberia, established partly in the immediate vicinity of the numerous fortified military stations, the “ostrogs,” or the winter quarters of tax collectors, the “zimovyes,” but partly also somewhere out of all contact in a quiet, fertile spot in the forest. Supplies were soon transported regularly and in varying volume along a road that slowly pushed eastward from the west via Tobolsk, Tomsk, and Yeniseysk, initially reaching the Sea of Okhotsk via Yakutsk, but later branching off toward Irkutsk and pushed further eastward from there until it terminated at the Shilka. Here the waters of this river, and later of the Amur, then took over the role of Siberia’s main commercial artery, which until then the northern highway had had to fulfill. In recent times, this latter route has also been followed, with a few deviations, by the railway.
