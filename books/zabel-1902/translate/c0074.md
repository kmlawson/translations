The workers employed in Gaidamak are Russians, Chinese, and Koreans. This composition is typical of the Primorskaya province, that is, the province by the sea, east of the Ussuri. The relatively most valuable among these workers is the Chinese; next comes the Russian, insofar as he is not a skilled worker, which very few are; last comes the Korean. The latter is the frailest and least robust. The number of Koreans living in Southern Siberia is quite significant. They are mostly people who were conscripted in one of the numerous civil wars, crossed the border, found asylum among the Russians who were in need of laborers, and are now unable to return without facing the death penalty. Socially they rank lower than the Chinese, at least if one goes by the work they perform. The Chinese, however, I rate higher as a worker than the Russian because he is considerably more adroit and industrious, and in any case delivers relatively far more work for his lower wages, even if the objective work output of the Russian can be somewhat higher—which, to be sure, is by no means certain either. In any case, the Chinese possesses the great advantage over the Russian that he does not drink, and is therefore a very steady worker. He already perceives it as quite an extraordinary luxury that he is not required to work one day out of seven each week.

Of skilled Russian workers, there are currently only three fishermen from Odessa in Gaidamak, who are on the point of going to Sakhalin to establish herring fisheries there. The only thing in which ultimately every Russian has some experience is woodwork. Yet that is also a necessity in a land where one must cobble everything together for oneself: house, spade, and fur coat. In this, necessity is often the best teacher, though as pupils [[p158]] the Chinese certainly lag nothing behind the Russians. In the cooperage, for instance, only Chinese are employed. European workers could not be more industrious or more painstaking than they are. To be sure, one must keep an eye on them to ensure they do their work properly, for they naturally cannot compete with trained coopers.

In log-cabin building, on the other hand, the Russians are masters. Thus, the fishermen had framed a completely finished, handsome, large wooden house, which is being dismantled, stowed into the belly of the *Alexander*, and shipped to Sakhalin, where it will be re-erected in about two days and will serve as living quarters for the fishermen and the fishery director, along with his bookkeeper and the latter’s wife.

July 12.

Tomorrow morning at four o’clock, the first whale hunt is indeed supposed to take place. Whether it will be successful remains to be seen, since the whaler must first discover the whales’ whereabouts within their cruising range. I am therefore moving my bag and baggage over to the whaler *Nikolai* this very evening, where the captain has placed his bunk at my disposal. Despite its small capacity of only about eighty tons, this little steamer is already one of the largest whale catchers in existence; other whale catchers are mostly smaller, including those in use on the Norwegian coast. Formerly, whaling was even undertaken only in small boats. One tried to get close up to the fish in order to thrust the harpoon into its body by hand. Today, this method of whaling is obsolete and is resorted to at most if the whale refuses to die and thrashes about for too long. Then a boat will usually be lowered, from which the coup de grâce is administered. Nowadays, a cannon mounted forward at the bow of the ship is used to launch the harpoon.
