Here, too, the post road to Possiet Bay joins in. It is already growing dark, but the dusk detracts little from the charm of the scenery. For a long time now no station follows, and we already pass in the moonlight the picturesque Suifun cliffs, by which the railway is pressed close against the riverbank. I am alone in my compartment; the train seems to be used almost exclusively for local traffic. . . . It is ten minutes past nine; we have penetrated the first 100 kilometres into the Asian continent: Nikolsk, the first stage on the way to Prussian- [[p207]] Berlin, is reached. The town lies more than a kilometre from the railway station. And so: “Isvoshchik! . . . Gostinitsa!” “Khorosho!” The horses fly at a gallop across the dusty country road.

My driver duly drops me off in front of what is supposedly the premier hotel in Nikolsk. He receives one rouble as his fare. That is of course far too much. For he curses dreadfully and demands more, until the innkeeper comes out and has the case laid before him. Hearing that the driver received a rouble, he leaps through the front door in a single bound, seizes a stick, and calls out a few flattering remarks in Russian to the isvoshchik, which the latter apparently returns until he catches sight of the brandished club. At that, he brings his whip down across his horses’ backs so that they break straight into a gallop, and—was seen no more.

I am assigned my “number”. It costs one rouble for the night. The barman, for a change, speaks *daitsch* once again.

The geographical location of Nikolsk is extraordinarily favourable. It is also primarily to this that the prosperity and rapid rise are due, which have made the place—a town for the past three years—with its 13,000 inhabitants the most populous and important station on the Ussuri Railway. Here is the gateway that opens onto the rich hinterland of cultivated southern Manchuria. To be sure, in the event of a Chinese insurrection in Manchuria, the town would be the most exposed to danger. The 100 kilometres that separate it from the Chinese border offer no great security. But on the other hand, it is also the most important garrison town in the Primorskaya. Before the outbreak of the Chinese unrest, it garrisoned the 1st East Siberian Rifle Brigade, the 3rd, 4th, and 5th Rifle Battalions, two batteries of field artillery, a mortar battery of the East Siberian Artillery Brigade, the Ussuri Cavalry Brigade, and the 1st Transbaikal Cossack Regiment. Since the Chinese troubles, however, the garrison has been significantly reinforced. Through the construction of the railway, Nikolsk did not lose, like most of the settlements situated directly on the Ussuri, but only gained, and will gain still more once the connecting railway across Manchuria, which terminates here, is finally completed. It is the junction where the transport routes of the easternmost part of the Russian possessions converge: from the north (the Ussuri and Amur regions), from the east (Vladivostok), from the south (Possiet Bay and Korea), and from the west (Manchuria as well as the other tracts of land traversed by the Trans-Siberian Railway). The soil conditions, too, are extremely favourable. Added to this is the fact that Nikolsk is one of the southernmost Russian settlements in Siberia, so that the climatic conditions also accord the place a promising position. In the immediate vicinity of the railway station, which for the time being still lies more than a kilometre from the first houses of the settlement, a separate settlement has already grown up that specifically serves the railway. There we find pleasant officials’ quarters with little gardens in front, alongside machine shops, carriage-building workshops, large warehouses for railway material, a two-class school for the railway officials’ children, a hospital, etc. It will [[p208]] in all probability hardly require a period of more than ten years before the gap between the settlements at the station and the houses of the town is completely filled.

How gladly would I have altered my travel itinerary even now and taken the route from Nikolsk via Harbin straight across Manchuria to Kaidalovo station between Sretensk and Chita, where the so-called Chinese Eastern Railway joins the Transbaikal line of the Trans-Siberian Railway.
