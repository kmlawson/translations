### 6. On the Shilka to Sretensk.

11–17 August.

The journey on the Shilka is much more picturesque than on the Amur. The rather swift-flowing water has carved out a deep bed. Numerous bends wind around the rock formations pressing against the bank, and the stands of timber here seem to be better preserved than along the course of the Amur. Navigation markers—lanterns—stand along the banks in such a way that from one lantern the next one upstream and downstream is always visible. The boatman takes his bearings from these markers. They are found along the entire course of the Amur and its navigable tributaries, and the lamplighter in his small boat is often the only human soul for fifty kilometers or more. The settlements we pass make much more the impression of agricultural colonies, although the type of village is exactly the same as along the Amur proper. Whenever the steamer replenishes its firewood, we gladly leave our boat for an hour and stretch our legs a little in the forest or scramble up the steep riverbanks. On one such occasion, we discover a Buryat family that has pitched its summer camp in a small clearing. They live in a tent made of birch bark. A number of their utensils—buckets, cups, and so on—are made of the same material. Their domestic animals are dogs and reindeer, whose cows are also milked. Nowadays it is not easy, on a journey through Siberia, to still encounter unspoiled natives in their primitive state. I was told of a young French scholar who had traveled into the tundra to seek out natives and study them in the wilderness. He traveled for months and finally tracked down a single Tungus family, about whom he then wrote an entire book.

We all long for the steamer trip to come to an end very soon. By noon on Saturday, 17 August, we have already reached Ust-Kara, the principal center of the Transbaikal gold industry. The place is only 65 kilometers from Sretensk. For the last time, we buy milk and fruit from a peasant woman; now we will not put ashore again until Sretensk.
