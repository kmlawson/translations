If, however, Germany even today still maintains the just standpoint it originally adopted when it committed itself to the principle of preserving the economic and political status quo in China, then it is its duty and its right to lead the way with a resolute veto on this question as well, now that it has, after all, been appointed by the Powers as the executive agent out here. It is perhaps not widely known that before the [[p107]] proposal was made to entrust the supreme command to a German, Russia likewise already had a popular general in reserve in Kiev in the event that the Powers should agree upon a Russian. Yet the German Emperor’s proposal to appoint Count Waldersee commander-in-chief in China was by no means unwelcome to Russia. Now it no longer needed to concern itself with so-called general international interests and could pursue its own special interests. Yet now that Germany has indeed assumed political hegemony out here, it is its duty to assert it in the general interest. From the German standpoint, too, this is logical and necessary if one is serious about the catchphrase of “*Weltpolitik*.” The only state pursuing *Weltpolitik* out here has hitherto been Russia alone. All the other nations have done little serious work for themselves in Zhili, but Russia has made good use of its time. And even if it has had incomparably harder work in Manchuria than the others elsewhere, even if it is still not in a position today to pacify the province permanently (the Powers have not managed that in Zhili either), it is nevertheless working toward its goal—the very real goal of a great Russian-Asiatic world empire. East Asia for Russia! In a tiny span of time, the Tsarist Empire has covered a tremendous distance toward this goal; in this past year, it has pushed its giant bulk a good stretch further south. Where is this to lead? And will the European Powers not perhaps miss the right moment to call out an effective “Thus far and no further!” to this further advance? We are, it seems to me, well on our way to “missing” it.

Anyone, however, who might believe that Russia now has land enough and would of its own accord halt its steps on the path southward at natural obstacles and political boundaries would fall into a serious error. The importance of railways for the commercial opening of China is well known, but Russia has amply demonstrated to us that it translates the word “railway” as “possession of the land through which the line runs,” and that to it a railway is at the very least a political border that it can close whenever it chooses. In § 12 of the draft treaty, Russia demands from China a concession to build a trunk or branch line of the Manchurian railway to Peking or the Great Wall. As I have learned from a good source, the plan is this: from Shanhaiguan, where the Mukden or Newchwang–Shanhaiguan line—annexed by Russia and not yet returned—terminates, the railway is to run in a directly southwesterly direction toward Peking. Thereby, not only is the British Shanhaiguan–Tianjin–Peking line to be killed off, but competition is also to be created for the Kaiping coal mines situated along this route, namely by opening up coal deposits said to exist in the north of Zhili Province, northeast of Peking. A second line would then seek to connect with the Trans-Siberian Railway along the great tea route Kalgan–Kyakhta, etc., so that Russian railways would lead out from Peking in two directions. Russia goes still further, however, and specifically in a southerly direction. There exists an important railway concession from Peking to Hankou that was acquired by Belgians. It is said to be a fact that the Belgians obtained this concession through the aid of the French government, [[p108]] which in turn is said to have been put forward by Russia. This is talked about out here in informed circles as something quite well known. The result will be that in a few years Russia will have its locomotives whistling in Hankou, in which it takes an extraordinary interest on account of the tea trade. Just think: on the Yangtze, a sphere of interest claimed exclusively by Britain for many years, and recently half-conquered by Germany! Sincere admiration for Russia’s East Asian policy, and above all for its *spiritus rector*, Finance Minister Witte!

Yet in political dealings with Russia, one should not forget the lessons it has taught us in this eventful year. To anyone who has occupied himself somewhat more thoroughly with these matters, it must gradually have become clear that no power on earth possesses anything approaching the political influence and political prospects in China that Russia does. Russia knew this before the political turmoil began; it did for itself what it deemed necessary to do, and did so with its characteristic thoroughness and brutality. On the other hand, it knew just as well how to free itself at the right moment from the international cant about humanity and other empty platitudes, and thus no longer let itself be deceived about its true interests—just at the right moment to prevent the breach with China from becoming unbridgeable. Despite the catastrophes of Blagoveshchensk and Tanggu, despite the plundering by its soldiers, despite the looting of the holiest Chinese sanctuaries, the Russian has not antagonized China so deeply that he cannot turn back; on the contrary, he knows his adversary and knows that he commands immense prestige among the Chinese. Russia acted shrewdly from *its* standpoint when it abandoned the allies at a favorable hour and looked only to buttering its own bread. The perfidy of which it has thereby made itself guilty remains, of course, and cannot be undone by anything, but no more can its successes for Russian policy.
