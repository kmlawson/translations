The foreign settlement in Nagasaki is small, and Nagasaki's overseas trade corresponds to it. The relatively largest business probably consists in coal. This is found nearby and brought right up to the harbor basin by rail. As a result, the price can be set fairly low. To be sure, Japanese coal cannot be compared in quality to Cardiff coal; it is said to be about 30 percent lower in value than the latter, which means that for the same work performance, approximately 30 percent more Japanese coal is consumed. In spite of all this, it still turns out to be cheaper than English coal, and it therefore dominates the East Asian market. In any case, it is qualitatively to this day still the best East Asian coal. Hankow coal was indeed praised to me from various quarters as being better, but for the time being it is not yet a factor in the East Asian market, since the mines near Hankow, which are in Chinese hands, have not hitherto been in a position to produce enough. Then there is a large French coal mine in Hongay (Tonkin), belonging to the "Société des charbonnages du Tonkin." However, the coal extracted here first requires special processing before it is usable for general shipping needs. It is now pressed into briquettes. And even then, it is said to require special grates and a special manner of handling during combustion. In spite of substantial support from the French government, the company has operated at a deficit until now, and has only turned the corner somewhat through the energetic efforts of a new director, a Swiss. As to the value of our Shandong coal, absolute certainty has not yet been reached. Rumor has it that the coal is not exactly the best in quality, but compensates for this disadvantage through its quantity.

As soon as our foot has touched Japanese soil, a friendly man steps up to us, his legs clad in long, dark-blue, tight-fitting, jersey-like trousers, to which the sock with the separated big toe is directly joined, his chest covered by a loose blue jacket. He politely takes off his cap, which turned out a bit tall for his small head and is modeled in cut after our soldiers' caps, makes his bow, and politely invites us to take a seat in the jinrikisha he pulls. There one sees at once: order and culture prevail here! In China, ten fellows with their vehicles would immediately have formed a veritable wagon fort around us, and with laughing faces would have invited us to take a seat by uttering a terribly coarse-sounding, but as inarticulate as possible "Hey!", until we would have had to bring our little walking stick into play, which in such cases very often renders good service as a little interpreter. But here everything proceeds orderly and politely. The jinrikishas are drawn up in rows, like the cabs back home; no more rickshaws pull up than are needed; the coolie moves deliberately, not too fast and not too slow, and then he trots through the streets of Nagasaki. The first impression is an extremely civilized one—that is, if one comes from China. This impression is moderated just a little when one rides through the Japanese streets and encounters half a dozen strapping young Japanese women, one of whom, with great nonchalance, straightens her disarranged [[p118]] dress. This mixture of a superficially adopted culture with Malayo-Mongolian essence, called Japanese, meets us at every turn. Eminent experts on Japan have expressed themselves quite extensively on this question and therefore spare me doing the same here. A traveler passing through should accept conditions with less criticism than one who intends to concern himself especially with a country. But writing a book about Japan is not my intention. Hence, only a few notes here!

If one has no guidebook, Baedeker, or similar books, then one does best to entrust oneself to one's rickshaw coolie. He has his particular route along which he takes travelers. So "go on!" First we ride through the foreign settlement. One half of it consists of the offices of commercial houses and banks, the other half of sailors' pubs, which often, by their very appearance, constitute the grossest violation of good taste. First of all, there is usually a beauty standing in front of the door, whose greatest beauty is mostly her well-fed figure. She belongs to the general category of international rabble, speaks a bit of just about every language, and possesses such knowledge of human nature that she will never address a German other than in German, a Frenchman always in French, an Englishman or American always in English. Where her cradle stood is mostly obscure, usually no doubt in Galicia or Romania, to judge at least by the German she speaks, and Patriarch Abraham certainly did his part in his day to make possible the existence of this painted creature in a dressing gown, who calls out to you: "Please, gentlemen, come on in!" One of my fellow travelers from the steamer seems to subscribe to the maxim: "if evil boys entice thee, follow them not, but go before them." At any rate, he is the first to follow this enticement, even if the boy here is a maiden—an imperishable one, to be sure, at least forty-five years old already! In for a penny, in for a pound! So we have to go inside with him into this noble hotel. I do not know whether I shall still recall all the names written outside on the signs: "Deutsches Gasthaus," "Oriental Hotel," "American Bar," "Hotel de France," "Taverna italiana," "Rossija Gostinniza"—I believe those are all of them. So we, too, order a whisky and soda. Just as we have taken the first sip, the beauty appears with the sweetest face in the world and asks whether we would not like to drink wine... Pay the bill—post-haste! and faster than we came in, we are already outside again, not without having left behind an outrageous sum for our whisky and soda.
