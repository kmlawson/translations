So in the evening we celebrate a cheerful reunion in the staging post hotel: the major, the captain, the ordnance sergeant, the Russian Cossack staff surgeon—who, incidentally, is treated as a guest of the German Empire and does not have to pay anything—and I. In the pleasant company of the staging officers, the dinner tastes splendid to us after having fed "out of the crate" for the two preceding days, and it is late before we seek out our beds.

During the night, the station is fired upon several times from a cemetery situated higher up. The night before, a sentry shot and killed a Chinese man who attacked him, and the Chinese are evidently out for revenge.

Moreover, the joy with the fine staging house is soon to come to an end. The rightful owners have already reported back and are demanding their house return. Negotiations for a compromise are indeed underway, but the missionaries insist [[p33]] on their bond. Thus the German staging post will likely soon have to move out of the beautiful property that it took so much trouble to fit out.

30 January.

This morning at 10 o'clock the train continues in the direction of Shanhaiguan. We enter a mountainous region, the same formation as throughout North China: bare, treeless hills and behind them blue, partly rugged peaks; the individual ridges are separated by fairly flat valleys through which the railway wends its way. It certainly strikes me that the region here is more prosperous than in the south. One can see beautifully built houses, well-tended fields, and above all very many more trees. The villages are consistently the most beautiful feature of the landscape.

A few kilometres from Qinhuangdao, the rival port to Shanhaiguan, which the British in particular have expanded and put in order, we cross a fairly wide river on a bridge nearly five hundred metres long, rising about ten metres above the water level. When we arrive at Qinhuangdao station, where a fairly strong detachment of German troops is also stationed, it is already dark. The port proper lies about an hour away from the station. The British have built a connecting line there, which, however, has no direct link to the tracks of the main line. Why this connection is not being built, I do not know. In general, the rule is that Shanhaiguan and Qinhuangdao complement each other. That is to say, if the wind comes from the south-east, Shanhaiguan is usually iced over; if the wind comes from the north-east, one cannot land at Qinhuangdao. The British probably attach such importance to this port at present because at Shanhaiguan they have the uncomfortable neighbourhood of the Russians. On the other hand, Russia has refused to contribute to the port expansion works undertaken in Qinhuangdao by the allied powers, simply because it is banking on Shanhaiguan and therefore does not want to help improve a landing place that serves essentially foreign interests.
