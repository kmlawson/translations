Besides the Cossack staff surgeon, who is bundled up in his thick fur coat and does not take it off despite the glowing stove, there are three German officers in the compartment: Major Borkenhagen, commander of the heavy field howitzer detachment in Tanggu; Captain von Koxzing of the field artillery regiment in Tientsin; and Ordnance Lieutenant Deutsch. These three gentlemen form a commission whose task is to pass judgment on the artillery pieces captured by Germany in China and to select those that are to go to the Zeughaus. The gentlemen have already been to the arsenals in Peking and Tientsin, as well as to the Taku [[p31]] and Peitang Forts, and have issued their orders there. Now they are travelling to Shanhaiguan to inspect the guns found in the forts there. At the railway station in Tanggu, one could already see an entire train loaded exclusively with Chinese cannons of all kinds, including a number of splendidly crafted old bronze guns that will be on display to be admired in the Zeughaus.

I have often heard it said that we had really done China considerable harm through the seizure and destruction of so many guns and weapons. In response, I can only point out again and again that Zhili is only *one* province of China, and that our troops—that is, the international troops—have by no means combed through even the whole of Zhili. To be sure, the arsenals in Tientsin, Peking, and several other cities have been destroyed, and the Peitang and Taku Forts will be razed, perhaps also the Lutai and Shanhaiguan Forts. But there are still many forts in China, and we have found arms and ammunition supplies in almost every larger city—or rather, failed to find them. So let us not be too boastful of our successes in China. We have barely scratched China's skin, and it has far too much resilience to be taken so lightly in the future as has been done in the past.

Early in the afternoon we approach the town of Lutai. It is probably best known for the army that withdrew from there upon the arrival of the allied forces and was never seen again. A series of military operations was directed against it, without anyone being able to lay hands on it. This army was stationed in no fewer than seventeen forts that encircle the town proper like a belt. Yet these are not forts in our sense, but fortified military camps mounted with artillery. Since the departure of the Chinese, the Russians have maintained a guard in the forts, but since the Russians handed the railway over to the Germans, the largest of them has been garrisoned by—six men.

The station buildings along this line are not as distinguished and elegant as those on the Peking–Tanggu line; for the most part, they were built only recently by German soldiers using timber and corrugated iron underpinned with masonry. In Lutai, I greet the officer of the railway regiment stationed there, who has created a very cosy home for himself out of these limited means—small, but charming.

The next station is Kaiping, known for its coal mines. They belong to the Chinese Engineering and Mining Co. and supply an excellent coal with which not only Tientsin but also a large portion of the steamers operating in North Chinese waters are provided. It is a good, rich coal, far more valuable than Japanese coal, which burns away too quickly.

The countryside now begins to become somewhat more pleasant. Here and there, ridges of hills approach quite close to the railway; the fields are well cultivated and show good care; at the stations, the Chinese offer fruit, chestnuts, nuts, cakes, and the like for sale. But beggars and the sick, among them unfortunate lepers, also crowd around the carriages and plead for alms. Thus the journey continues without variation until, already in the dark, at around seven in the evening, we arrive in Tangshan, which lies roughly halfway between Tanggu and Shanhaiguan. Today the train goes only as far as this station, so we disembark and are warmly received by the German staging post stationed there.

The Tangshan railway station is relatively elegant and large. It has a well-concreted platform and a proper, large station building. The town is quite significant, especially because of the nearby mines and the large factory buildings that have been established here along the railway. Above all, there are railway workshops, iron foundries, and engineering works of considerable size. At present, to be sure, they are still at a standstill—less because there would be nothing to do or because this area had suffered particularly from the Boxers or troops, but because the Chinese workers, who mostly came from Hong Kong or Shanghai, fled to their homes at the beginning of the Boxer unrest and have not yet returned. Several large mission houses also stand in Tangshan; the missionaries, to be sure, likewise preferred to reach safety in good time. The German staging post has quartered itself in one of these buildings, where a number of guest rooms have been fitted out. Everything is, of course, wartime simple, but neat and clean—like in a hotel—and is also run as such, because every evening guests are expected from the trains who cannot be accommodated anywhere else. Under these circumstances, it is self-evident that one does not stay for free. But the prices are at cost, and no charge is even made for the room. In any case, the officer responsible for household management has made all the arrangements with positively touching care and great circumspection—proof that the German officer, when required, can play the hotelier himself.
