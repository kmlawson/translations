Further along we come to a spot where sand is being poured into the sea and a basin is being built for smaller cargo steamers and for boats. A little further still, we see the preliminary work for a small dock. Where a peninsula juts out into the sea, thereby forming a natural small basin, work is underway staking out a large dock—a dock in which the largest ships in the world can be docked, even if they were to grow larger still. On the spit of land itself, we already find completed a good number of workshops, foundries, turning shops, steam hammers—in short, everything needed for machine building and shipbuilding. Mr. Timm's saloon carriage, in which we traveled together to Dalny, was built in these workshops. All along the harbor basin, however, run tracks on which the massive cranes and working machinery move, and behind them stand sheds upon sheds filled with building materials, tools, etc. Just how immense the dimensions are on which work is conducted here can be seen from the following charming episode, which possesses the virtue of truth. When work began on carrying out the plans envisioned for Dalny, cement emerged as one of the largest expenditure factors. It was an enormous quantity that was needed. A competitive tender was then opened for the supply. One factory, in cooperation with others, was in a position to undertake the deliveries. But the price demanded per ton seemed too high; the factory, however, declared that it could not supply it any cheaper. Thereupon, the following course of action was taken. The rumor was spread that excellent limestone had been discovered near Dalny and that they now intended to set up their own cement factory. But the factory remained unyielding. The game thus had to be played out to the end. In fact, limestone was present, but it was of little use for this purpose and even less sufficient in quantity. Nevertheless, the Russians ordered the machinery necessary for setting up a cement factory, and since the large cement factory naturally had its spies as well, they were forced actually to erect the machines and begin producing cement. All of this together required an expenditure of several hundred thousand rubles. Only when the large cement syndicate saw that work had actually commenced did it yield and quote a lower price. Thereupon, their own cement manufacturing was shut down again, and a contract was concluded with the large factory. If, then, several hundred thousand rubles need not be spared merely to force a factory into a small price reduction, and if, despite the loss of those several hundred thousand rubles, a substantial gain still results for the client on this contract, what colossal quantities must be needed!

At midday I am invited to lunch at the home of Chief Engineer Sakharov, who has built himself a house that is less imposing on the outside, but appointed with all the greater luxury within. The entire conversation revolves around nothing other than Dalny and its future. High-flying plans, presented with enthusiasm, are what I get to hear, and my doubts must strike their sails before the triumphant confidence conjured up by the optimism with which the artist believes in the realization of his plans.

Then the chief engineer leads me through the completed part of Dalny; we inspect the new hospital, then he shows me houses for lower and higher officials, workers' houses, [[p99]] which are intended to meet the demands of modern hygiene and social policy; each family occupies a little house of three rooms, a kitchen, and a cellar; each group of houses situated around a large courtyard has an ice cellar; every dwelling has its own small yard and garden; in the corner apartments of the side wings of the horseshoe-shaped housing complexes reside subordinate officials charged with supervising the entire complex.

In the government quarter there is also a small hotel, furnished by the state right down to the napkins and leased out at a moderate price; furthermore, there is a small clubhouse for the officials, a large public garden with a bowling alley, then a barrack-like building for unmarried officials, which on the inside, however, is furnished quite comfortably, and a large number of Chinese houses built with more than superfluous luxury. The Swiss chalet style predominates.

Dalny is to become a free port, Sakharov tells me, and the railway freight rates are to be set so cheaply that they can defy any competition from maritime shipping. Both in land sales and in the leasing or selling [[p100]] of buildings, the most extensive accommodations and support will be granted to all interested parties without distinction of nationality, and everything possible will be done to interest the world, and especially the East, in Dalny. Fast steamship lines, connecting with the through trains, are to link traffic with the cities of China and Japan; in short, they want to make Dalny, as it were, the center of the Far East. And that is why Russia needs a secure railway link through Manchuria to Siberia. Hence Russia's efforts in Manchuria, hence the struggle for an open port, and hence already now the sealing off of Vladivostok through the new import tariff! Dalny is merely the crowning of a pet plan long cherished by Russia. Around Dalny there will perhaps entwine, within the next ten years, a wreath of fears and hopes of all those whose pocketbooks are invested in East Asian affairs. And one can harbor no doubt that, given the energy with which Russia pursues its East Asian plans, it will also succeed in making Dalny the focal point of *its* East Asian interests, perhaps even the focal point of East Asian interests in general. But because matters may turn out this way, it is necessary that we draw little, unknown Dalny somewhat out of the obscurity of its provisional existence.
