That the considerations that led to this are of a purely political nature, and that their consequences signify a completely unjustified preference for political viewpoints over economic necessities, is just as clear as the fact that the favoring of the ports in the south occurs at the expense of the ports in the north. What colonial-political immaturity comes to light in these measures! What schematism! Russia willfully conjures up through them a crisis in regions that are still far too little consolidated internally to endure such notched knives at their most sensitive points. The consequences of this policy are already showing [[p219]] themselves. Agriculture, which most needs nurturing, is severely depressed throughout the Amur region as a result of the increased cost of agricultural implements, etc.; food has become considerably more expensive, particularly flour, especially since the territory still has to import a substantial amount of flour. Added to this is the fact that, since the introduction of the tariff, brisk smuggling has developed across the long, uncontrolled, and indeed entirely uncontrollable Chinese border, which now threatens seriously to damage, if not ruin, legitimate business as well. Only from a repeal of the tariff ukase can salvation be expected for the easternmost Russian colonial territories, whose cultural and economic conditions are already highly unhealthy in themselves. Moreover, this tariff is already the second disappointment for the Amur country. The first consisted in the fact that the originally planned stretch of railway along the northern bank of the Amur, from Sretensk to Khabarovsk, was not carried out, since the line was routed through Manchuria. Instead of the hoped-for connection of this hitherto most flourishing region of eastern Siberia, it was furthermore completely isolated, since the greater part of the transit traffic that previously went along the Amur is now also withdrawn from it by the railway diverted to the south. This policy of Russia is cruel and pursues anything rather than the economically so beneficial principle of state compensation for harsh transitions. The Amur country is additionally burdened with the heavy load of a tariff that serves to promote the southern regions even more and to artificially increase the natural diversion of transit traffic to the south as well. With every kopeck of tariff the Amur country must pay, it therefore enhances the ability of its keenest economic competitor, South Manchuria, to turn the hoped-for benefits of railway construction for the Amur ports into their exact opposite. It is incomprehensible that Russia should willfully tear apart again in this manner the fruits of sixty years of industrious labor. But this incomprehension in the face of real colonial-political problems—where the schematism that is twenty times more at home in Russia than among us fails completely—is encountered only too often out here. There is a lack of solid official personnel; complaints about the unreliability and ignorance of the existing staff are universal. The governors-general and governors are recruited from among the higher officers; fortunately for it, Russia does not yet seem to know any assessorism in the German sense in its colonial policy, otherwise the second fundamental evil would surely join the first out here. In any case, Russia's economic policy in easternmost Siberia reveals dilettantism of the most disastrous kind; the weal and woe of the colonial territory depends on the arbitrariness of whichever one-sided foreign-policy, mostly even purely military considerations happen to govern at the time. Under these circumstances, given the lack of suitable colonizers and the dubious quality of the available human material, a cold skepticism toward the economic enterprises of the Russians in the Far East seems to me, upon mature consideration, entirely appropriate.

For almost the entire morning we traveled through forest. Near the small stations at which we stop, almost all of which are built on the same model, the [[p220]] forest is cleared, and one sees fields and grazing cattle. Frequently the stations consist only of an individual station building and the characteristic water tower for the engines; the settlements around these beginnings are yet to come. There is no doubt: they will come.

Alongside the railway line runs an old road that apparently served during the railway's construction for transporting materials, and perhaps previously also as a tract for overland traffic and communication between the settlements lying away from the river. Today the road is completely overgrown; the numerous corduroy bridges are rotten and for the most part collapsed from decrepitude—proof that the road is no longer used at all. The railway has replaced it completely.
