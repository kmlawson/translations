Now this part of the sea is undisputed Russian possession, and Russia is by no means idle in diligently asserting its rule, namely by protecting the fishing concessions granted to its subjects against predatory American fishermen. Every year, schooners of American fishermen who unlawfully fished or hunted seals in the coastal waters of [[p145]] Siberia are brought in, and the government in Vladivostok has at its disposal quite a number of such vessels that were seized from Americans and are now chartered out to private individuals. One of these formerly American schooners is the very one that, laden with coal, has now been brought in tow by the whaler *Nikolai* from Nagasaki to Vladivostok. Its story is perhaps not entirely uninteresting.

In San Francisco there lived—or still lives, if he has not died—a wealthy shipbuilder who, an Anglo-American, had inherited from his mother country a penchant for sport and from his new home one for high stakes. One day, a well-known racing yacht wins a regatta off San Francisco. As is possible only in countries with a predominantly English population, the racing yacht becomes the talk of the town across half of America, is depicted a thousand times in every possible position, with and without owner, with and without the finery of her sails, the owner himself in sailing, riding, cycling, and possibly even swimming costume in all the sports papers and magazines of America and England—in short, all that fuss develops which we Germans cannot comprehend to such a degree in sporting matters. The trophies of this victory, however, gave the aforementioned shipbuilder no rest, and at last he announced that he would build a yacht that would surpass the much-lauded victor in speed. Everyone is astonished and intrigued in the highest degree, but nobody believes him; a debate arises, and finally the shipbuilder further backs up his assertion by offering to wager 80,000 dollars on the victory of his new yacht. Then the matter begins to get truly interesting; the bet is taken, further wagers are placed, millions are staked on the old yacht and the new, in short, the whole affair becomes more exciting by the day, and the day of the “race” draws nearer and nearer. At last the yacht is completely finished; amid an almost colossal turnout, the race begins. And lo and behold, the new yacht indeed runs like the wind and already has a lead over the former winner. During the sailing, and even beforehand, the shipbuilder had been drinking heavily in his cabin with several friends to the hoped-for victory, and finally, during the second part of the race, it occurs to him to take the helm out of the hands of his proven helmsman, despite the warnings of his friends. Ambition, however, tickles him to cross the finish line as helmsman himself in the face of what seems an undeniable victory. Whether it was the champagne or his lack of skill, in short, he steers so clumsily in the second part of the race that the lead is lost again, and the old yacht ultimately crosses the line ahead of the new, even if only by a few lengths. The loss of his high stake and the mockery over his defeat angered the shipbuilder so greatly that he had the beautiful yacht completely rebuilt, stripped out all the cabin fittings, converted the promising vessel into a cargo ship, altered the rigging, and subsequently sold the whole thing to a seal hunter, who took the costly craft to the Sea of Okhotsk, engaged there with her in [[p146]] illicit seal hunting, and then lost her to a Russian cruiser. This cruiser brought the vessel along with her crew to Vladivostok; the crew was given the opportunity to inspect Siberia a little more closely, not merely from the sea, but also from the inside for two years, and the ship was chartered to Count Keyserling’s fishing company for the ridiculous price of 80 rubles a month. To this day, the yacht still sails splendidly under the command of a Japanese captain, though she is usually towed by one of the whalers, which can make up to eleven miles an hour; and when the wind happens to come directly from astern and the now-schooner spreads her sails, it is not uncommon for the schooner to sail right past her towing steamer. At all events, one may once again apply to this costly ship the saying: “Sic transit gloria mundi!”
