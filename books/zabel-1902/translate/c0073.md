The trimmed meat of the whale is then carried into the boiling house, where it is thrown into a cutting machine that slices it into small cubes. These cubes all fall into a pit and from there are lifted into the boiling vats by means of a scooping apparatus that works exactly like a dredger. Once the boiling process is completed, the train oil is pumped into large vats, from which it is then drawn off into barrels and shipped. The residual waste is not yet utilised at present, but simply thrown into the sea.

In chemical factories, all kinds of finer fats, stearin, glycerine, and many another fat, as well as substitutes for foodstuffs, etc., are prepared from the train oil, though these would generally retain little memory of their origin in Siberian waters. Whalebone, likewise exported as a raw product, later enjoys the particular affection of the ladies, who are even accustomed to wearing it over their hearts, provided they have not joined the ranks of dress reform—which is decidedly the more sensible course.

The utilisation of the whale in wintertime is quite different—a method of utilisation that is possible only in East Asia. For then the whale is cut into strips, root and branch, right down to the bones; these are dried in the open air, then simply packed into the ship's hold and shipped to Japan, where they form the daily sustenance of the Japanese, who eat them with their rice. Dried whale meat also finds no small market in China. Over time the meat hardens considerably, but dissolves again upon cooking to an agreeable tenderness.

Incidentally, the prejudice of civilised humanity against whale meat is just like the prejudice against snails, oysters, and horsemeat. It is simply a prejudice. Fresh whale meat in particular can be prepared exactly like veal and tastes quite similar, so that there is no need at all to be horrified upon hearing that there are people capable of eating whale meat. One does not, after all, have to go so far as to drink train oil like the Greenlanders, though here too the climate may well have been father to the thought, and perhaps we too would drink train oil without disgust if we were placed in the living conditions of the Eskimos.

We have an excellent lunch at the home of the German engineer, the director of the Gaidamak station, Mr Sommermeyer. The main dish is roast venison, and the mistress of the house apologises that, in the rush, she was unfortunately able to obtain absolutely nothing else, only roast venison—just think of it, "only" roast venison! As though the roe deer were simply running about the poultry yard! It is almost like that; at least they are said to occur in rich numbers in the neighbouring forest—notabene a genuine primeval forest—as are red deer. The abundance of game in this region is said to border on the fabulous in general. The surrounding country is a sheer park for both roe deer and red deer. To be sure, I personally did not see a single tail on an evening stalk, but I must observe in this connection that just as I set out, I happened to encounter an old Chinese woman; hence my bad luck! It is nevertheless said to be a fact that the forest teems [[p156]] with game. This leads to the following anomaly. As is well known, the Russian government is labouring to colonise these regions. Now the Russian peasant is anything rather than a coloniser. If he has his piece of bread today, his tea, his lump of sugar to nibble on, and his dried fish, he does not think of tomorrow; he lives, in truth, a Christian, contemplative life, taking no thought for the morrow, but acting like the fowls of the air. This applies even to Siberia, to which, after all, the better and more energetic elements still migrate, since the decision to emigrate in itself already presupposes a

[Illustration: Cooperage in Gaidamak (p. 141).]

certain initiative. The settler is assigned a fairly large parcel of land by the government. Upon it he first builds his log cabin from the trees he fells, then he eats up what he has brought with him, and when that is gone, he finally begins, little by little, to turn his land to account. Here the friendly Chinaman comes to his aid. In the eastern parts of Siberia, he is the true husbandman and market gardener in our sense. The Russian peasant knows nothing of vegetable cultivation; his understanding does not extend beyond the few crops of his native agriculture, and he is very slow to learn. The Chinese is the disinherited party here at the hands of the Russian, and yet he defeats his oppressor with peaceful weapons. He leases a piece of land from the Russian's property, grows vegetables on it [[p157]] and takes them to town, where he sells them at a good price. But the lazy Siberian peasant, instead of tilling his land himself, prefers to parcel it out on lease to the Chinese, collects his rent, and folds his hands in his lap or takes to hunting. Here again the Chinaman comes to his aid. For to the latter, the deer's antlers, still covered in velvet, are considered a valuable remedy. Such antlers command very high prices; Chinese merchants pay 50 to 500 (!) roubles for them, depending on the quality. Therefore, unless he is too lazy even for this, the Russian settler takes a gun, goes into the forest, and shoots deer to take their antlers. The meat is then sold cheaply, very much cheaper than roe venison. Once he has shot a few deer, he has enough money to live for a year, lies down on the stove bench, drinks his "chai", and nibbles sugar with it. That is the average peasant in this region. Of course, there are also intelligent Russians with initiative who make something of themselves. Thus one man hit upon the shrewd idea of capturing deer and penning them in a large enclosure. Here the animals live without any care, that is to say, as if in the wild. The peasant now does nothing more than snap off the deer's antlers at the appointed time. He repeats this every year, and thus derives a right handsome income from his stock of deer, which can amount to several thousand roubles a year.
