## Chapter IV. Through the Primorskaya.

### 1. “There, where once Genghis ruled.”*)

July 16.

If I were superstitious, I would board the next steamer heading south and return to Europe by some other route, anything but right across Siberia. I have not even begun my journey on the Trans-Siberian Railway, and I have already missed a train! It happened like this. There are no porters in Vladivostok. In their stead, Chinese carters handle travelers' luggage to and from the pier or the railway station. Since the only express train departs daily at 9:25 in the morning, a Chinese carter had been ordered for me to be outside the hotel at 8:15. He was indeed there punctually. Loading took ten minutes. The trunks were tied onto the clumsy two-wheeled cart so that they would not go sailing off when the cart rocked across the streets of Vladivostok. The droshky I was using and the cart with my luggage departed from the hotel at the same time. In less than ten minutes I was at the station; the cart, which had to travel more slowly, was supposed to follow within five minutes at the latest. It had no intention of doing so, however. At long, long last, after much waiting and a few suppressed [[p202]] curses, I see it swing around the corner. It is already ten minutes before the train's departure. Now, quick: unload and check the luggage! In passing, I also learn that the honest Chinese had overturned the cart on the way while trying to take a sharp corner in haste, and that as a result he had to untie all the trunks, right the cart, reload it, and lash it down anew—all of which was rather beside the point to me at that moment, because I focused all my attention solely on settling matters with the clerk who was to check my things. I, with my foolish notions of European speed, was firmly convinced that a span of ten minutes would suffice, especially as the other passengers already seemed to have been attended to. But calculating the excess luggage charge evidently caused the clerk great difficulties. Apparently, not a soul in Russia learns mental arithmetic. At any rate, every shopkeeper, every Russian banker, and every counter clerk works out all four basic operations exclusively on the abacus. Indeed, when a Russian wants to know how much two and two make, he first reaches for the abacus, and if right after that you ask him how much two times two is, he clatters away with the beads for a while again and is afterwards extraordinarily surprised when he discovers that two times two is also four, and that you knew it in your head. Just as the prevalence of reading and writing has gradually degraded memory among us, so the abacus has it on its conscience that people in Russia generally cannot perform the simplest addition in their heads. Yet when a Russian is clattering away on his abacus, you must leave him in peace, otherwise he will miscalculate and have to start the whole operation all over again from the beginning. In my non-Russian subject’s intellect, I now thought I could lend the good clerk a helping hand by repeatedly presenting to him—both orally and in writing—the result of the calculation of what the excess luggage charge for my bags as far as Khabarovsk on the Amur would cost, which I had long since worked out in my head. But to no avail! Incredulously he stared at the figures I had written down for him, nor did he seem to feel any fondness for my money, which I had already counted out and placed before him; and since I had unfortunately disturbed him in his clattering, he started anew to calculate the problem of how much six and a half poods—of which one pood is free luggage—would cost if one pood costs such and such an amount. He miscalculated, arrived at too high a sum, I protested, and when he finally had the result right, the train was just pulling out of the station! “If you slip a ruble note under his abacus beforehand, he calculates much faster,” someone explained to me afterwards, to whom I poured out my woes. Well, that is just the sort of thing one needs to know beforehand!

Instead of the 9:25 train, I am now traveling on the train that leaves Vladivostok at 3:25 in the afternoon. That is “the” passenger train on this line, whereas the morning train is “the” express train. The latter, to be sure, does not deserve its name because it travels particularly fast; by no means fast for an express train, a mere 21 kilometers per hour! Rather, it is because the passenger train runs even much slower! To cover the 721-verst distance to Khabarovsk, it takes from 3:25 this afternoon until 6:40 in the afternoon the day after tomorrow; that is, on average, [[p203]] 14 versts per hour. For the sake of fairness, however, it must be mentioned that the verst is still somewhat longer than the kilometer—a full 67 meters longer, in fact.
