At 4 o'clock in the afternoon, anchor is dropped again near a woodcutter's hut. The steamer is packed from top to bottom with wood; wood is stacked on deck as well. After all, we have plenty of time. The steamer that is supposed to meet us will take a couple of days to reach the bar that we cannot cross. It is not until 6 o'clock that we continue on [[p243]] our way. The wheels have barely made some twenty turns when we gently run aground. The wheels now only churn up a mighty spray; nothing helps, our steamer has run onto a sandbank. How long will we be stuck here now? Also until the high water is kind enough to make its way to us? Great excitement on board! Boats are lowered; sailors row around the steamer and probe the perimeter of the vessel with their sounding poles. Fortunately, the ship is not grounded along its entire bottom, but only at the bow. Sailors and deck passengers therefore join forces in hauling the wood supplies stacked forward to the stern. In addition, massive spars are lowered into the water, a chain is laid across them, and the steam winch uses them to heave the ship up at the bow. At the same time, the wheels run in reverse. After several futile attempts, during which the passengers too must withdraw aft to lighten the ship forward, we happily break free again. We travel for a few more hours between the flat banks, and then drop anchor somewhere. It is raining outside, and the cold, wet weather prompts us to pour a little more cognac than usual into our tea for dinner. In this way, it turned into grog.

July 24.

We have been under way since daybreak, but since 8 o'clock in the morning we have been at anchor again, namely before the rather large Cossack stanitsa of Yekaterino-Nikolsk. The settlement, like most larger settlements in this part of the Amur region, dates from 1858. It is the headquarters of the staff of the Amur Cossack Division. Here a number of our Cossacks leave the ship. The place is considered one of the most flourishing Cossack settlements on the Amur. The inhabitants, to be sure, live under the same conditions and engage in the same occupations as the other Cossacks on the Amur: fishing, hunting, navigation, logging, providing post horses in winter, etc. Little farming is done, no more than is necessary for their own needs. It could hardly be otherwise, since transport conditions almost preclude working for export. The Amur fisheries belong to the Cossacks, one of the many privileges granted to them. In other respects, however, the land on which they live has only been given to them in fief; it is not their property. Land tenure conditions will likely yet prove a source of considerable vexation for Russia in its colonial territory.

This morning we reached the southernmost sweep of the Amur; the river now runs due north for a long distance.

From here to the bar that we cannot cross because our steamer draws too much water, it is only a few hours. This morning around 9 o'clock the captain wired Blagoveshchensk once again. If our experiences of yesterday and the day before are anything to go by, we will not get away from here before tomorrow morning. Soon we will know the place by heart; we have already visited every single shop and bought up whatever zakuska we could get hold of. We hear that three large [[p244]] steamers are said to be stuck upstream. On deck we walk around the chart house with the sun and wait anxiously for the telegram. Noon comes, afternoon, evening, midnight. We are still stuck fast. As yet, no telegram has arrived.

July 23.

Nikolsk no longer has any interest in us at all. Today we once again walked around the chart house with the sun. Despite its 1,200 inhabitants, the stanitsa does not possess anything like a village tavern, or we would certainly have stopped in there. And so it grows dark again, but the agent in Blagoveshchensk makes no sign of life. Splendid conditions indeed on this state-subsidized Amur mail-steamer line! And to top it off, virtually the entire time we have been on this tub, we have received no other meat dish than—fish! The prospect that these conditions will continue for days to come makes me nervous. My Russian fellow passengers, seemingly, do not get nervous. They pass the time in their own way; already lively sympathies and antipathies have developed [[p245]] among the passengers, people play cards and—wait. One simply waits! For me, this waiting with no end in sight is, I believe, a greater torment than—if I were to have a ring put through my nose!
