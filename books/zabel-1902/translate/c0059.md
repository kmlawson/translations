I have absolutely no inclination toward seasickness—that is to say, I am by no means inclined to get it. Even so, I get it. I have not yet calculated how many thousands of kilometres the sum of my sea voyages amounts to; it is a goodly amount. By now I ought gradually to have become seaworthy. The captain, to be sure, says there are even old sea captains who have spent their whole lives at sea and yet still get seasick in foul weather. That comforts me. Incidentally, at dinner I was the only one who held out until the last course. That I owe, [[p126]] to be sure, more to my willpower than to the good food. For the food already seems calculated to ensure that most of the passengers become seasick. After dinner, I therefore seek a little recreation on deck. The cabin is so stuffy. So I put on my waterproof raincoat and climb up to the upper deck, despite the rain, wind, and tossing. The wind now strikes me as quite pleasantly refreshing. I station myself at the railing, facing in such a way that the wind drives the rain straight into my eyes. Ah, how restorative! I gaze over the railing; it seems to me as though a shark is eyeing me from below, looking so lurking, so expectant. But no, surely that is only a phantom of the sea, perhaps a sea serpent, which are said to occur here as well—July—it is just the right season for it. So I continue gazing over the railing, and from sea phantoms my mind turns to figments of the brain; the association of ideas carries me along, one thought chasing the next. I recall a conversation with a ship’s doctor friend of mine who maintained that seasickness was an obsessive idea and originated in the brain. The thought makes me shudder; no, on the contrary, I have the distinctly unpleasant sensation that seasickness comes from the stomach. I consider how this theory might be supported with evidence... Damned shark, don’t gawk so stupidly... “Hullo, what are you doing back there then?” suddenly rings out behind me. The captain really must have eyes everywhere! “Captain, I was feeding the shark a bit.”

On the Sea of Japan, 3 July.
We are approaching the Korean coast. The captain points to the left with his hand and declares: “There it is, can’t you see it?” No, I cannot see it. Here we are not far from Masanp'o, which the Russians have occupied. According to other reports, they have not occupied it. Where does the truth lie? A state treaty between Russia and Korea, according to which Korea cedes this place to Russia as a naval station and outpost between Vladivostok and Port Arthur, has not been made known. It seems to come down to the fact that Russian private individuals, while in agreement with the government and probably at its instigation, did acquire land there to establish a coaling station, but purely as private individuals. The Japanese naturally took their countermeasures at once, and on the very same basis: namely, they have now for their part acquired almost all the other land that the Russians did not buy. If Russia now comes along and wishes to take the island politically as well, Japan has an evident right to deny it this. In terms of cunning, the Japanese is quite a match for the Muscovite. Masanp'o may after all one day prove a very pretty pretext for a Russo-Japanese war over Korea. When it will come is questionable, but I have received the impression that it will come soon, unless a modus for an amicable settlement is quickly found—an outcome to which the Japanese are inclined despite the English alliance.

The company of passengers on the *Daphne* is a rather motley assortment. A Courland count with his young wife, an amiable and pleasant couple; then a Russian captain with his wife, who speaks German, English, and French just as fluently as Russian. The ship’s *chronique scandaleuse* whispers that she had eloped with a young naval officer and has now been retrieved in Japan by her husband. Such things happen even in the best of families in Russia and cause no particular stir. Russian society ladies, for that matter, usually command several languages. Then there is a young German-Russian from Libau, who relates with immense pride that he is making a journey “round the world” and flirts with his English, but who otherwise belongs to that class of sporting travellers who set off on their journey, if possible, with merely a single suit, one shirt, and a kopeck in their pocket, and upon their return pay the usual visits to newspaper editorial offices, where the junior local reporter is given the momentous assignment of writing five lines about the man for the local section. Five lines, and by no means any more! Another variety of megalomania brought on by compounded vanity. Furthermore, we have an American miss on board, who comes from Port Arthur and is bound for Vladivostok, very plainly dressed, as an American miss always is in such circumstances. Then an English itinerant preacher, who does not speak his English but quacks it, and whom one usually sees on deck as the faithful cavalier of the American Lady of the Camellias. Finally, travelling in the cabin (there is, *nota bene*, only one class on the *Daphne*) are another Russian couple, who are constantly reading verse (evidently newlyweds), then the maidservant of one of the couples, and an old Russian babushka who has a remarkable eye for the best piece lying on the serving platter and often skewers it from far away with deadly accuracy. When it comes to helping themselves, the Russians are not at all timid, and, at least when the soup tureen or the roast platter is set on the table, they subscribe to the principle, deeply rooted in human nature: “Modesty is an ornament, but you get further without it.” Ordinarily, I have always made it a rule, when dining at a table with several others, to offer the dish to the others first. As is well known, politeness generally obliges politeness in return, and so it was to be hoped that the others, out of courtesy and inborn modesty, would always take the smaller portion and leave me the large one. With the Russians, this is the first time that this speculation upon the nobler impulses in man, which has otherwise always served me quite well, has failed.
