It is, however, certain that certain regions of Siberia are very rich in placer gold, which is extracted partly as gold dust, partly in the form of so-called “erratics”—compact, mostly heavily abraded little nuggets, at times even lumps of native gold. But of the thousands who set out to search for it, even in places lying right next to discovery sites, a very few found—something, and one or two really found enough that they could carry it home. The calculation of probabilities turns out hardly more favorable than in a lottery. In Australia things went, and still go today, in exactly the same way. The situation is quite different as soon as quartz gold comes into question. In [[p225]] general, wherever placer gold is found, this is not far off, and it has already been discovered in many parts of Siberia, for example in the immediate vicinity of the Amur. But the exploitation of quartz requires a thoroughly professional mining operation, to which the above-mentioned difficulties apply on an even greater scale than to the placer gold industry. The Russian out here is not equal to a regularized industrial enterprise, which presupposes a substantial store of comprehensive commercial and technical knowledge—and, for that matter, neither is he in European Russia. The directors of the vast majority of large Russian enterprises are not native-born Russians; at the very least, they hail from the German Baltic provinces or from immigrant parents—a fact, unfortunately, undeniable in the light of the Russian national character, which is otherwise thoroughly congenial and dear to me! Still, the gold industry of Siberia is also still in its infancy. How could it be otherwise, when the development of the entire country is still so immensely backward! But development will soon reach a limit, namely in the lack of transport routes, in the lack of knowledgeable managers of enterprises, of reliable officials, of laborers, and—in the climate! The climate that preserved ivory across millennia thus also ensures that the exploitation of Siberia’s mineral wealth proceeds so slowly that our descendants will hardly be able to complain about us, as Alexander once did of his father, that we have left them nothing more to do. — —

Yesterday evening at the hotel, I made the acquaintance of two young German merchants, one of whom kindly served as my cicerone today. Among other things, we paid a visit to the German brewer, a Mecklenburg man, Mr. Lübbens. Yes indeed, Khabarovsk has a brewery! But Mr. Lübbens, or rather the two Messrs. Lübbens—the brother also relocated here from Mecklenburg at an already very advanced age—brew exclusively bottled beer. Only a mineral water factory run by a Russian competes with them locally in manufactured beverages, producing seltzer water and notably the Russian national drink, “kvass,” drawn as a fruit lemonade under carbonic acid pressure. Genuine kvass, of course, is something quite different. Crumbled and roasted black bread, as black as our army bread, is mixed with sugar and boiled down in a measured quantity of water. The cooled brew is strained and bottled for fermentation. Then the fermentation process is interrupted, the bottles are corked, and after some time it can be drunk. It is thus a kind of non-alcoholic beer and tastes somewhat like it too. The beer that Mr. Lübben brews, however, is good German pale lager. Oh, what a delight, deep in the interior of Siberia, to drink German beer at the source once again! To be sure, it does not taste quite as good as in Germany. That is due to the wretched water conditions. For in Khabarovsk they do not yet have water pipes, but use the water of the Amur, even though all the sewage flows into the Amur as well. And your true, noble, honest Russian—with a haircut that seems to have been produced by turning a flowerpot over his microcephalic head and cutting off all around the [[p226]] hair protruding beneath it, which radiates from the crown in its natural lay—drinks the often brownish-yellow water just as it comes from the river. The better sort of people at least filter it. Yet filtered river water is not well suited for brewing. Since there simply is no Pilsen water in Khabarovsk, the Lübben brothers just have to use Amur water as it is, and the men of Khabarovsk cannot demand beer as good as what we have in Germany.
