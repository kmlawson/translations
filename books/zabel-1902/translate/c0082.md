When the passengers come ashore, it is again Chinese who take their luggage from them and load it onto their small two-wheeled carts. They are joined by Korean porters offering their services, but they cannot accomplish as much as the Chinese. Koreans are therefore, on average, employed in the lowest positions—as domestic servants and dishwashers, street sweepers, and farmhands—while the Chinese are at least waiters, coachmen, servants, and boatmen, thus performing work that is socially more highly regarded. And then, it is the rule among the Chinese that in a relatively short time they achieve independence. In any case, the retail trade, insofar as it serves the needs of the colored races, is largely in their hands; even the *izvozchik* occasionally buys something from the Chinese, and remarkably even prefers to do so rather than from his own countryman, who competes with the Chinese as a booth-keeper and petty shopkeeper, as a seller of boots and matches, Japanese seltzer water, and ribbons, cord, buttons, needles, and so forth. So it teems and surges and crawls down there around the harbor, and up to me drifts nothing more than a faint humming and buzzing, from which only now and then the shouts of Chinese coolies heaving a bale of goods, or a broken chord of a harmonica played by a Russian drayman before the serving counter of his regular booth-keeper, steals quietly up. Along the main street, however, droshkies rush by; now and then a horseman is seen; and now, along the railway track from the Amur Bay, a train slowly approaches, momentarily hidden from view in a cutting as it draws near, then crosses the main street at grade and pulls into the station complex situated by the harbor basin. From up here the city itself looks rather bare; between the individual houses, insofar as they have not been crowded into the main street, the Svetlanskaya, making full use of the available space, there is a striking amount of room. Broad areas, mostly wasteland, a smaller part built upon, separate the houses from one another, especially in the higher parts of the city. From up here one also sees all the more clearly how few stone buildings the city actually possesses. Still, this perception does not detract from the overall impression one gains of the whole picture at our feet. On the contrary, the mostly light colors of the houses—on which only the main timbers, the window frames and mullions, and the shutters are painted green—enliven the scene, which suffers at most from a lack of natural greenery. In return, however, the mainland heights and individual islands bear forest at some distance, and the green grassy slopes shimmering darkly in the distance contrast with the bare heights on whose slopes Vladivostok is built and by which it is surrounded. An ingenious Frenchman takes advantage of the abundance of grass on one of the islands near the harbor by having established and tending a herd of cattle on the island, which sustains itself and multiplies there almost free of cost. In this way Vladivostok always has fresh milk and good butter close at hand. This undertaking also has the advantage that cattle plagues frequently rage among the Russian peasants' cattle, which, given the lack of any comprehensive inspection, compels caution in the consumption of milk and dairy products brought to market by the Russian peasants. Through this isolation, the herd is said to have actually remained healthy.

An important part of the landscape would still be missing were one not to mention the numerous forts, one of which rises right above my head on a hill that overtops mine by a good deal. But directly in front of me, too, I look into one of the forts; I distinctly make out the sentry on the rampart. On the heights around the harbor basin as well, and further out on the islands—everywhere one sees the sharp lines of earthworks and glacis behind which batteries lie concealed.

On the flagship down in the harbor a signal sounds; immediately afterwards the other warships repeat it, and the pennants and flags slowly come down. It is six o'clock in the evening. The merchant steamers haul down their flags as well. The sun is still shining brightly; it is being quite generous. It is, after all, midsummer, and Vladivostok lies on the same latitude as Venice and Nice. It will still be a little while before the sun sinks down back there in the Suifun Valley, behind the mountains over yonder whose contours and hues blur when viewed against the light, to shine for the people far to the west—in Venice and in Nice, in Berlin and in St. Petersburg. Indeed, what time is it over there now, over there in Europe, with our loved ones at home? Six o'clock in the evening in Vladivostok—that is about nine o'clock in the morning by Central European Time! In Petersburg it is an hour later, so the time difference between Petersburg and Vladivostok is still roughly eight hours. A third of the day, then! Only in eight hours will it be six in the evening in Petersburg. Just imagine what it means when, in a single empire that moreover forms a contiguous landmass, it is six o'clock in the evening for eight consecutive hours every day.
