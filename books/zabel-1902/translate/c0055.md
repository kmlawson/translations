Such taverns offer, one might say, the reverse side of European Nagasaki. A good and distinguished hotel, by the way, can be recommended: the Nagasaki Hotel, where I found food exceptionally good by East Asian standards.

Now it is the turn of Japanese pleasures. Our gentlemen rickshaw coolies drive us through the city, always between the dainty Japanese shops where one can buy the most charming Japanese wares, so pretty and so cheap. Naturally, they drive us to a temple. This craze must have been taught to them by the Americans. The Americans run to every temple they see or that is listed in their “Guide Book”. And they are [[p119]] all in there! But once you have seen a few Buddhist temples, you know exactly what the others look like. To be sure, the temple we visited is very beautifully situated high above the city, and one has a lovely view of the harbour and the roofs of the town, especially the latter.

We climb back down the hill that we had had to climb up to the temple. From this physical exertion one of our party has grown thirsty, and so we follow once more the enticements of smiling sirens, Japanese ones this time. Right where the long flight of stairs leads up to the temple hill, a Japanese teahouse sign beckons above, and a Japanese tea girl below. We step inside through the doorway, which is lightly closed off by long hanging Japanese beaded curtains made of little pieces of bamboo, into the room. Here everything is furnished in the Japanese style. We are led up the stairs; up here stand a European table and European chairs. On these we sit down. Very little is said; we look at each other and await the things to come. The milieu of the “geisha”! First, several maids appear with fans. Each of them fans everyone, and since we cannot answer their Japanese questions, we fan the little Japanese women right back in reply. Then we order. We ask for “Tansan”, a Japanese mineral water. That is understood, but it is also the only Japanese word that we understand. Yet no, one more: “Geisha”! This word has now been uttered as well; eyes light up—we are going to see a real geisha! My companions do not yet know the sight of such an enchanting creature and are full of suspense. Evidently they are expecting a beauty who will sing “The Goldfish Pond” to them in Japanese in melting tones, or “I am the little geisha of Japan”, and ask them for lessons in kissing. So they want to hear a geisha. She is sent for; every respectable teahouse has its house geisha, as it were, who is always available. And appear she does, but faces grow longer and longer. This geisha has already been past thirty for several years! To be sure, she must once have been beautiful, at least so one of my companions consoles himself—the one who would rather go ahead of the wicked boys. Then she begins to play on a mandolin-like instrument. That already sounds a little nicer. Then, however, she begins to sing, and scores a resounding success—of the most negative kind, to be sure. Her forte is screeching, her piano squeaking; only her falsetto is nature. For she sings only in falsetto. At this the guests turn away in horror; only that one companion of mine, who simply lives deeply in illusions, remarks that while she had not sung beautifully, she was nevertheless interesting in herself, and presses a yen into her hand as a token of his appreciation—something he will not do again later! When, however, he is then asked to fork out another yen for the single bottle of Tansan, which is otherwise to be had everywhere for twenty cents at most, even he begins to grumble. In the end he remarks that he had imagined a real geisha rather differently after all.

Now, however, the rickshaw coolies have caught on. Seeing our predilection for such establishments, whose chief characteristic is that one is fleeced beyond all measure, they know quite a number more of them. For they get a percentage there. And so it is not long before they stop again before an establishment said to house geishas. We [[p120]] wave them off. After a little while they stop again and turn round with the meaningful word: “Geisha”! Again we wave them off; they trot on, but after a little while they stop once more and whisper with a wink: “Geisha”! It truly seemed as though in all Nagasaki there were no sights to be seen other than geishas. In the face of such persistence others too would eventually have relented, and so the companion who always likes to lead the way declares that the geisha we had seen had been an old screw, and perhaps the young geishas would be nicer. I have no further inclination to chase after these geisha adventures—which are, after all, interesting enough for newcomers—and take my leave, as I also have some more serious business to attend to. In the evening I meet the jolly company again; they are sorely disappointed by the first impression Japan has made on them. Indeed, much foolish stuff has been produced in the way of infatuation with Japan, and it is no wonder that this takes its revenge. I, too, must confess that the first impression I gained a year earlier during my first stay in Japan likewise fell, in every respect, far short of what I had imagined of Japan and its inhabitants, and it was not exactly softened on subsequent visits. One expects to find a civilized people and finds a typical Asian people, who so far have copied little more of what we call civilization than throat-clearing and spitting. This applies, after all, to all aspects of life. For the thoroughbred European, however, it stands out as downright disagreeable in what the Englishman refers to simply as “Japanese life”. One must have reached a high degree of resignation to find pleasure in it in the long run.
