### 3. Khabarovsk.

July 19.

The city, which bears the name of the Yakutsk merchant Khabarov, who came to these regions some 250 years ago as one of the first, was elevated to the status of capital of the Priamur Governor-Generalship because of its central location and because the main communication routes of the region converge there. When Muravyov sailed down the Amur for the first time and reached the mouth of the Ussuri, he exclaimed with delight: “Here a city shall stand for me!” At first, to be sure, he and his successors resided in Nikolayevsk, which lies about 1,000 kilometres downstream on the Amur. The new city received fresh impetus from its elevation to capital in 1880, and today it counts roughly 15,000 inhabitants, of whom about two-thirds are Russian. Yet the place makes by no means as imposing an impression as Vladivostok. It bears a distinctly inland character. The [[p222]] larger firms that maintain their stores there are mostly branch establishments of Vladivostok houses. The population is anything but a peasant population. They are mostly petty craftsmen, traders, officials, soldiers, and petty bourgeois, like in a small town back home, who are simultaneously one another’s customers and suppliers, competing with each other and otherwise practicing economic inbreeding. The wholesale business is in the hands of a few. Khabarovsk owes a not inconsiderable influx to the gold riches of its surrounding region. One must interpret the word “surroundings” here as broadly as possible. A district lying 500 kilometres away is still considered part of the surroundings. Frequently, however, the gold deposits lie even further from Khabarovsk. Two large tracts in the Amur Province are distinguished where gold prospecting is said to be worthwhile. One area lies along the upper reaches of the Amgun, the other along the upper reaches of the Zeya. For the former, Nikolayevsk and Khabarovsk come into consideration as the nearest large towns; for the other area, Blagoveshchensk. Acquiring a concession costs nothing. If a gold prospector finds auriferous earth at any spot, he erects a marker, writes [[p223]] his name and the date upon it, and has the seizure of possession registered at the nearest official office. Then the land around the marker post belongs to him out to a certain distance, provided that third-party rights are not infringed thereby and that the person concerned is Russian. In Khabarovsk lives a family that has become very wealthy through working the goldfields. The story told about them is that the father of the family first acquired an auriferous parcel of land in his own name, then a second adjoining one in the name of his wife, and then six more in the names of his six children. Now he is said to lack only one more piece of land to have fully consolidated his holdings. The seventh child is said to be already on the way. Attached to this manner of taking possession is the further obligation that work on the occupied land must actually be commenced within a certain period of time. However, this regulation is frequently circumvented by having a few workers trek out to the area in question before the deadline expires and dig there for a few days, regardless of whether gold is found or not. But that anyone else might forestall the owner in the meantime is hardly conceivable. For the difficulties under which the Siberian gold industry operates are very substantial. First to be considered are the tremendous distances and the lack of transport routes. Machinery that is to be installed, say, this year had to be transported to the goldfields already in the previous year, and ordered in Europe and shipped to Nikolayevsk, Khabarovsk, or Blagoveshchensk a year before that. The same applies to provisions. Since nothing, not the slightest bit of food, is to be had on site, all provisions must be brought to the work sites in the spring. Halfway along the route, warehouses even frequently have to be built, because it is impossible to manage the transports in a single season. These warehouses are exposed to the depredations of adventurers, wildcat prospectors, or escaped convicts. Furthermore, washing can be carried out for at most five months of the year, because in ice it is simply impossible to wash gold. To these grave obstacles is added the greatest of all: the lack of labourers. True, many hundreds are lured every year by gold fever and hire themselves out to the mine owners. But these are adventurers, often a vile rabble, who outnumber and override the few loyal workers and the officials, cheating and stealing from their master wherever they possibly can. Often enough the officials are in league with them. Attempts have been made to try Chinese labourers, but the government resists this. For anyone who has the governor or someone else of influence as a cousin, a blind eye is turned, sometimes both. For a long time the regulation existed that the gold extracted had to be sold to the Russian State Bank or to the Russo-Chinese Bank, which had specially employed chemists determine the alleged fineness and paid only for that. Lastly, one must not forget that the “washing” of gold is fundamentally always a game of chance, upon which a sound business should really never be built. The solid foundation of gold extraction is formed by the processing of quartz. This, however, is not practiced in Siberia, even though gold quartz is also said to be present in abundant quantities, with the exception of the previously mentioned gold mine of Mr. Lindholm on Askold. To be sure, formal [[p224]] mining and washing plants do exist at individual, particularly productive locations, but by the admission of persons who are themselves involved in such large-scale operations, an exact commercial calculation can scarcely be made in washing operations. At times enormous yields, at times tiny ones; the stability, ton for ton, as in quartz processing, is lacking.
