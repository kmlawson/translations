A grubby, dyed-in-the-wool Russian who does not speak a single syllable of any civilized language receives me, and finally I manage to find out that not a room is left. Assuming that the business about the “first-rate” hotel was a misunderstanding, I have myself directed to another. Alas, that dive [[p82]] is much, much worse, and once again no room is to be had—thank God! I still think in my youthful recklessness. I shall therefore try my luck with a third. My standards, however, have already dropped quite extraordinarily. They set me down at a third hotel. There a room is available. The Russian waiter boy first leads me through a foul-smelling courtyard, then up a narrow flight of stairs, a so-called chicken ladder, to a low upper floor. He stops in front of a crude wooden door opening onto a flimsy gallery facing the courtyard, opens it, and—I recoil from the dreadful odor that wafts toward me from the “room” intended for me. A tiny little chamber with a slanting roof, inside a narrow iron bed with dirty covers, a bedside mat, and a chair upon which stands the washbasin, the windows pasted shut with paper strips in the Russian fashion and impossible to open. It is simply appalling, and the thought of having to spend even a single night in it fills me with dread. In despair, I initially leave my trunks downstairs in the hallway under the care of my boy, consume some of the zakuska on the bar counter, and drink a vodka, thoroughly Russian. That is my morning coffee. Then, however, I immediately seek out a gentleman who is an official of the Russo-Chinese Bank and to whom I have letters of recommendation. Fortune favors me: in that very same institution I also run into a good acquaintance, a fellow traveler from the *Hamburg*. “Tell me, the hotel situation here seems to be in a pretty bad state.” “Yes, no decent person can stay in a hotel here. But if you are willing to make do with a sofa at my place—you know, one of those old-fashioned ones with broad armrests——.” “Oh, warmest thanks, with the greatest of pleasure!”

### 2. Port Arthur.

February 25.  
Port Arthur is the Russian Kiaochow. Only, after the occupation the Germans had to start completely from scratch and, so to speak, conjure a new city out of the ground with Tsingtao, whereas in Port Arthur the Russians found an already built, European-style modern naval port. Accordingly, they have more or less left everything as they found it. This goes so far that they have not permitted a single private individual to build thus far. If certain firms did not wait for a license, they must reckon with the risk that one fine day the authorities will simply have the building torn down again or confiscate it for themselves. Indeed, up to now not even sales of government land have taken place; it is said to be doubtful whether it will ever come to that at all, since there is in fact an intention to reserve Port Arthur exclusively for naval and military purposes. The buildings that have been erected so far are military or government structures; private individuals almost all live in restored Chinese houses that have been makeshift-adapted for their residential and business purposes. Nowhere in the East have I seen such wretched offices as in the Russian Tsingtao.

Port Arthur possesses a truly magnificent harbor. It opens out toward the sea through a fairly narrow exit, bounded to the right and left by mountains [[p83]] rising steeply from the water. The harbor basin proper forms, so to speak, a small inland lake, enclosed on all sides by sheltering heights and connected to the sea only by a narrow, short channel. At all events, from the town of Port Arthur one always sees only the small inland lake, and can catch a view of the open sea from only a few spots. The location is thus extraordinarily favored by nature. All around on the heights stand forts, and nowhere else in the world, in all likelihood, is the ban on photography enforced as rigorously as here. If, despite this, I manage to snap a picture here and there, I am constantly aware that this is actually a crime that could easily make me acquainted with Russian fortress walls.

Nature has been lent a helping hand—and indeed, it was the Chinese who did so. For under the direction of French and German instructors, they had a large, deep basin dug below the mountain that borders the harbor entrance on the east, completely sheltered from any inclemency of the weather. The Russians now use it as a naval harbor. Only warships and vessels of the so-called Volunteer Fleet are permitted to anchor in this basin. Along its shore stand warehouses and cranes, and a dock capable of accommodating the largest vessels is built directly alongside it; in short, a model nautical facility has been created here of which China could be proud, and which it surely yielded only with great anguish to its lease-eager neighbor in the north.
