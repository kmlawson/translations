Then, however, one would have to travel onward over the mountains (the Lesser and the Greater Khingan) by post-horses, and it was very questionable whether these had not all been requisitioned for the railway works. In any case, the journey would certainly be extraordinarily arduous, and even if that alone would not deter me from embarking upon it, the consideration that the duration of my trip would likely become entirely unpredictable certainly does. Besides, I am also very keen to see the regions that have been the object of a centuries-long struggle between Russians and Chinese. While conversing with the buffet attendant, I stand at the bar and, in the Russian manner, take my *zakuska* before supper in the form of a caviar roll. I praise the caviar and learn that it is fresh caviar from Amur sturgeon. If I was so fond of caviar, the buffet attendant remarked, then I really ought to travel up the Amur. “There you eat the ikra right with spoons!” So along the Amur we shall go, where caviar is eaten right by the spoonful!

July 17.
Seen by daylight, the town indeed gives the impression of rapid growth. The streets consist of two rows of houses fronted sporadically by wooden boardwalks, with a morass between them. When driving through by carriage, the mud spatters high, and if another carriage passes by, one does well to open an umbrella, as it is after all more convenient to have it cleaned later by the hotel servant than to have to wash one's own face, especially given the general dearness of water. The great Vladivostok trading houses Kunst & Albers, Langelütje, Churin, etc., possess branch establishments here, and the marketplace is full of wooden stalls in which Russians, Jews, and Chinese offer all kinds of bric-a-brac for sale. The houses are for the most part of wood; of stone houses there are at most twenty, among them a beautiful white cathedral towering over everything, which, however, does not suffice for the religious needs of the inhabitants. Besides it, there are three more wooden churches in the town, joined by a fourth in the settlement by the railway station. The troops are quartered in extensive barracks and cantonments in the south of the town. Ancient remnants of walls recall the legendary kingdom of Bohai, whose residence is said to have been situated here. It was followed by the kingdom of the Manchus. This was crushed by Genghis Khan, and upon its ruins arose the new Manchu empire of the Qing, who now sit upon the throne of China and have in the meantime themselves been driven from their ancestral homeland once more by the Russians. That is the fable of the big beast that devours the smaller one, only to be eaten itself by an even bigger one.

Numerous passengers await the arrival of the Vladivostok express toward 2 o'clock in the afternoon. In the morning the train from Manchuria arrived from Pogranichnaya, bringing several high-ranking officers who are traveling to Khabarovsk to attend a great jubilee celebration, taking place next Sunday, of the regiments that took part in the Chinese campaign. The Russians think so too; in an instant, the doors of the carriages, arranged like our corridor express trains, are crowded, and I succeed in making my way directly behind a general traveling with his wife into a still vacant compartment, where I immediately make myself at home. The officer addresses me; I keep hearing only “madame, madame,” and shake my head regretfully: do not understand! In the meantime, the orderly brings his master's luggage into the compartment, some ten different pieces, including an enormous valise that will not fit into the luggage racks above the seats at all and has to be placed in front of the window—the large bedding sack without which the Russian does not travel out here. We take our seats; besides the three of us, there is also an elderly white-bearded gentleman in the compartment, who is incessantly smoking cigarettes from a holder some twenty centimeters long. From time to time a reproachful glance from the beautiful eyes of the general's wife falls upon me, which, however, I cannot explain and connect only dimly with the “madame, madame” from earlier. Meanwhile, cruder instincts prevail in me for the moment, and so I stroll through the corridor of the train toward the dining car, where I initially seek to soothe my growling stomach with [[p211]] a hearty cabbage soup with beef, a Russian national dish called “shchi,” which I subsequently succeed in doing with the assistance of a “côtelette” à la maréchal. Gradually, one passenger after another arrives—merchants, engineers, officers; I count no fewer than five generals on the train. Among them my neighbor from my compartment, who presently also appears at the dining car table with his wife, accompanied by the white-bearded gentleman with the long cigarette holder.
