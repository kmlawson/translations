#### 3 February.

I use today’s day of rest to look around the station a bit, although there is really not much to see. I take a few photographs, watch an officer lungeing his horse, and get my bearings regarding the branch line to Mukden. The building in which the officers live is quite clean and well furnished inside. It evidently served previously as a residence for the European station official, was destroyed, and later rebuilt by the Russians. The fireplaces built directly into the walls, in which a cheerful fire flickers, are very cozy. It is incredible what heat the Russians, who are fairly immune to the cold, can endure. For the most part, the rooms are overheated. Every officer has his comfortable camp bed with him, which folds up into a small box and is very handy. The field bakery and the water-filtration apparatus are not lacking at any station. Everywhere there is excellent black bread. The samovar stands on the table all day long, and people indulge in it diligently. The Russian food we receive is very hearty, but not particularly tasty; usually the dishes are too greasy. The best part of the meal is undoubtedly the so-called “zakuska,” the “appetizer,” which is eaten before the hot courses. They consist entirely of good little delicacies: caviar, piquant canned fish, ham, salad, and so forth. Usually one is already so sated by the zakuska, to which belong—at least—three vodkas, that one can hardly enjoy any of the hot dishes.

By chance, the officers are invited to lunch with the Manchurian village headman, and I too receive a direct invitation when they learn of my presence. Toward midday, a guard of eight men stands ready to accompany [[p47]] us to the headman. It is remarkable what respect the Russians have, after all, for the men with queues. They never go anywhere alone, always accompanied by an escort with fixed bayonets. I am also urgently advised not to go into the village alone;—in Zhili we were not so fearful.

Outwardly, the village headman differs in no way from his subordinates. Nor does he seem to care much about his appearance, for his coat is torn and positively glistens with grease, especially at the back where the queue hangs, and in front where drops are wont to fall while he eats. Nonetheless, the Russian officers fraternize with him in the most friendly manner, shake his hands, occasionally put their arms around him as well, and through it all the Manchu knows how to maintain his dignity, which the officers do not always manage to do. Later they plan to make the headman drunk on vodka, but he, accustomed to his potent samshu, in the end proves able to hold his liquor better than the Russians. Meanwhile, the soldiers stand in the courtyard and keep watch so that no harm comes to their masters, perhaps also to escort them safely home should alcohol, if not the Chinese, play a trick on them. On the return from the Chinese meal, which proceeds like all the others but produced quite good food, one makes one's way back home by stages [[p48]]; on the way, stops are made here and there, one looks at this and that, and lingers for some time first in front of, and later inside the house of a Catholic Christian family whose members are said to have suffered greatly at the hands of the Honghuzi *). Here too, the Russians fraternize most vigorously with the Chinese; people embrace, and it falls little short of kissing. And yet we are always surprised when Russia emerges from negotiations with the Chinese with greater success than we do. The Russian simply stands closer to the Asiatic race of the Chinese than the other Europeans do, and while we work with European matter-of-factness and reprisals, he embraces the Chinese and wrings the finest concessions from him in the guise of the most intimate friendship, while also letting reprisals peek out of his pocket—reprisals which the Chinese, for his part, fears more from him than from anyone else. For he knows full well that in such matters the Russian brooks no nonsense and actually strikes once he has raised the stick. Velvet paws and an occasional bloody scratching—that is how the Russian makes the Chinese compliant.

When we have returned to our quarters, we find Colonel Modl’s telegraphic permission to continue the journey, along with the order to provide me with an escort of ten Cossacks as far as Yingkou (Newchwang). Thus this important question is settled, and I accordingly make my arrangements for continuing the journey. Foremost among these is procuring a cart and draft animals. It is too cold for riding, for it is no pleasure to sit on a horse in at least 15 degrees of frost. Moreover, I want to record as accurate an itinerary as possible, and therefore prefer to sit, well wrapped up, in a Chinese cart. I send my servant to the same village headman with whom we had dined, and after long negotiations and efforts, and after the staging post commandant has also spoken a stern word, we finally succeed in hiring an open farm cart for the price of ten dollars to Yingkou, to which three animals are to be harnessed. The cart is to be ready at eight o’clock the next morning and equipped to travel at a continuous trot.
