Thus I have unreservedly given expression to the view that forced itself upon me during my stay in Manchuria. Whether the message will be very welcome everywhere, I do not know, but I hope at least that it will not be dismissed out of hand without examination, even if one does not agree with it.

## Chapter I. By Ship to Vladivostok.

### 1. A Side Trip to South China.

(From a letter.)

Hong Kong, June 21, 1901.

. . . But now hear above all what has happened in the meantime. For since my last letter from Chefoo at the beginning of March, you have remained without news. Meanwhile, a great deal has occurred. So attend devoutly to my report, which runs as follows.

As you know, after my expedition through Manchuria I took an extended rest in Chefoo. I wanted to wait and see whether any world-shaking events were brewing on the theater of war. But since these were too long in coming, I used the time to join a new friend, the engineer Schauer, who was just about to set out on a minor expedition into the gold districts of northern Shandong—the first since the outbreak of the unrest. Schauer is an engineer of the German Society for Mining and Industry Abroad, and specifically "deals exclusively in gold." You will receive the detailed report\*) on this expedition by the next mail from Shanghai [[p111]]. I had returned to Chefoo only a few days before when, like a bolt out of the blue, a wire reached me reporting the death of my friend Ho Amei in Canton. You know that Ho Amei was a respected South Chinese merchant. He had prospected the provinces of Guangdong and Guangxi in all directions for precious ores and minerals, and himself held several mining concessions. Through his good offices, dating back to my visit in December 1899, I already owed one of the best South Chinese concessions, which we intended to exploit jointly. His son Ho Ke Yong, who was educated in my parents' house some fifteen years ago, informed me of his father's death with deep sorrow, and this news was important enough for me to arrange my travel plans accordingly at once. I chose the next steamer bound for the south and traveled on it, without touching Shanghai, directly via Amoy and Swatow to Hong Kong, arrived there at the end of March, and then proceeded to Canton to express my condolences to Ho Ke Yong and at the same time to look after my own interests. From then on, I was now in Hong Kong, now in Canton. In Hong Kong I met quite a number of dear old friends from Shanghai again, and in the small, but all the more convivial European colony in Canton I spent delightful hours. The memory of it brings before me above all the images of the amiable German consul Kallen; the interpreter of the German consulate, Lange, one of the foremost connoisseurs of China, who is soon to come to Chefoo as vice-consul; as well as the head of the firm Melchers & Co., Mr. Südhans. Above all, I like to think back on the happy hours in the home of Mr. Lange, whose wife is also to be thanked for the genuinely German spirit and cheerfulness that brought such an invigorating element into the thoroughly international character of Canton's social life.

But I could not bear sitting still in one place for long. Nor did I resign myself to doing so any longer than was strictly necessary to come to an arrangement with Ho Amei's legal successors and conclude some new contracts with Chinese parties. If you had ever been in the position of having to negotiate with Chinese or conclude contracts with them, and moreover with people who are generally not accustomed to doing business with Europeans, you might perhaps be able to appreciate what an achievement it represents that I was able, within the very first fortnight, to—bring a contract to completion. The deliberations always took place in a large conference, in which all members of the family down to the third and fourth degree in descending and ascending lines participated; and then, in the end, the contract still had to be submitted for appraisal to ever so many old friends of the family and ever so many wise men and old greybeards, each one of whom brought forward a new objection and yet another piece of foolishness that had to be refuted with a considerable display of glibness. Fortunately, Ho Ke Yong still speaks German reasonably well, so that communication posed few difficulties. The worst part was when the oral agreements were now to be codified, and that in both the Chinese and German languages. This was done by the Chinese first drafting a text in Chinese on their side in long private conferences, and I a text in German on mine. Both texts were then [[p112]] translated and compared with one another, merged, and ultimately a joint contract emerged, which received its final official seal of attestation at the German consulate. The superintendent of the Berlin Mission kindly assisted me with the final editing of my agreements with the Chinese.
