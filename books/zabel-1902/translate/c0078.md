As far as I know and was able to ascertain, the whales that come into question for the region along the east coast of Siberia belong to the group of rorquals. The Russians themselves asserted that they were the same whales as were caught in Norway. With this would also accord the shortness and thickness of the baleen, which on average does not reach one metre, as well as the fact, observed by myself, that when surfacing the whale blows no more than twice, but usually only once. The Gaidamak whaling station is likely in essentials a copy of the Norwegian whaling stations, as described in exact detail in volume 6 of the *Abhandlungen des deutschen Seefischereivereins* [Transactions of the German Sea Fisheries Association]*). Gaidamak is the best-equipped station; however, as mentioned, it comes into question for whaling only in midsummer and autumn. In winter, when the whales head south, whaling pursues them to the Korean coast, where a whaling station likewise exists, which however is of a more provisional character and is equipped solely for flensing the whales. This station has in a sense the character of a mobile station, as it is customary to relocate it according to the location of the whales. There are no facilities of any kind for extracting train oil there; instead, the whales taken in winter are merely brought ashore, cut up, and the meat and blubber salted down in barrels and shipped to Japan, where they serve as food. The bones of the whale are likewise shipped directly to Japan and marketed there. Traffic between the whalers and the Japanese ports, where Japanese merchants are once again the buyers, is handled by the transport ship *Alexander*. In Japanese ports, Count Keyserling's company maintains no special stations; the vessels call there only now and then to take on cargo or to be docked. The Gaidamak station, on the other hand, possesses the complete facilities of a well-managed Norwegian station, for the present, however, still with the exception of the fertilizer works. The transport of the unground bones, however, is treated only very much as a secondary matter. At the station itself, enormous piles of whalebones lie stacked up, still awaiting subsequent processing and grinding. Presumably no separate guano works will be established at Gaidamak at all, since this will be replaced by the future large factory ship, which in all probability will also process the stocks of bones still stored at Gaidamak as occasion offers. In addition to the apparatus for cutting up the whales and extracting oil, and apart from the cooperage and the living quarters for officials and workers, the Gaidamak station also possesses a kiln for drying the timber used in barrel-making, then a forge that serves mainly for the production of explosive shells and harpoons, and further a small workshop for making large tin vessels that are used in place of barrels. In this workshop all kinds of mechanical work that arise are carried out; this is necessary at a station so completely cut off from communication with the outside world as Gaidamak. Furthermore, there is a sawmill there that is constantly in operation to carry out the rough woodwork. Power is supplied by the steam boiler of the machine shop. The personnel [[p165]] of Gaidamak consists of the chief engineer (a German), a bookkeeper, a warehouse manager, a cooper (likewise a German), two machinists, a mechanic, and about a dozen Russian workers. The salaries of the Europeans range between 300 and about 70 roubles. In addition, about thirty Chinese whose wages range between 15 and 30 roubles, and about ten Koreans who receive 10–20 roubles, are employed and resident at the station as labourers. These salaries are monthly. The train oil obtained at the station is first transported by the company's cargo steamer to Vladivostok, and from there is sent almost without exception to Odessa on vessels of the Russian Volunteer Fleet. For the present, as far as I am aware, the business works exclusively for Russia, and the same is to happen after the inception of fishing operations, specifically herring, sardine, and salmon fishing. The latter in particular will be able to count on a very strong local market, and the surplus is to be shipped to European Russia. Meanwhile, if its enterprises succeed on a large scale, the company intends to expand the market for herring, salmon, and sardines to the rest of Europe as well, specifically to Germany and England. The number of whales taken has hitherto been rather variable; nevertheless, the annual catch at the Gaidamak station is likely to be about 50 to 80 whales. Concerning the catch along the Korean coast, I unfortunately possess no particulars. It must be taken into account here, however, that the company can look back on relatively recent experience, that for the time being a great deal of experimenting is still going on, and above all that closer studies are still being made concerning the haunts and frequency of the whales. In my opinion the operations themselves are not conducted with particular intensity; people have a relatively generous amount of time, and various measures are adopted that subsequently prove superfluous. Yet there is no doubt that whaling in Siberia, once this first period has been overcome, still has a significant future. A chapter entirely of its own is likely to be formed later on by the hunting of the true Greenland whale, which occurs in the Sea of Okhotsk. For the present, this is pursued almost exclusively by Americans, and indeed unlawfully in the manner described. Until it is taken up and systematically exploited by the Russian company, which holds the concession for this as well, much water is likely to flow down the Amur.
