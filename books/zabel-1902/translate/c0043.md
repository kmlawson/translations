The father of this bold speculation is the Minister of Finance, Witte. Out here he is regarded as the most capable Russian minister, and especially for East Asia as the "man who has arrived." At any rate, [[p94]] Russia owes a large part of its East Asian influence to him; Pokotilov, the director of the Russo-Chinese Bank in Beijing, is his political agent, and it is an open secret that he works and accomplishes more for Russia than the envoy himself. As for Dalny, it is, so to speak, the clasp on the long chain of Russian successes in East Asia, to which is attached the anchor with which Russia secures its commercial interests in the ice-free ocean. The great Asian landmass bounded by the colors of the Russian Empire is surrounded by the ice of the ocean for half the year. Consequently, it is a vital question for Russia to create for itself, in one way or another, a permanent connection to the open sea, if the Trans-Siberian Railway is not to lie fallow for transit traffic for half a year. And that is why Russia does not run its Trans-Siberian Railway to Vladivostok, as had originally been planned, but instead seeks a place further south, on the permanently open sea. Originally, Talienwan was chosen for this. But it proved too exposed to winds and swells. Consequently, they moved to the opposite shore and established a city in the open field, which they named Dalny. Once the city is finished, the merchants and non-military personnel from Port Arthur will be settled in Dalny. In Russia such things are done *par ordre de mufti*. Instead of Port Arthur, Dalny will then be pushed all the more vigorously as a commercial center. And when one knows this and considers the efforts Russia is making with Dalny, one must admit that Alekseyev spoke the absolute truth when he assured the merchants of Newchwang that Russia would never offer Newchwang competition with Port Arthur. But he concealed from them that this would happen with Dalny. Such is what one calls diplomacy. Work on Dalny's construction has been underway for only two years. On the site where the city already partially rises now, and partially will rise in the future, there are two Chinese settlements, small fishing and farming villages. These were bought up, the houses torn down, and only the trees that characterize almost every village in China were left standing. They are to form the basis for two public parks provided for in the development plan. This development plan itself is quite interesting. It proceeds from the idea that Dalny is to become a maritime city. Consequently, the so-called center of the city lies on the water. The city itself is divided into four sharply separated quarters: the part nearest the water and the harbor is to become the so-called commercial town, where the offices of the firms are planned, the warehouses—in short, everything necessary for the life and bustle of a great commercial city. The railway is also to terminate in this commercial town, running, coming from the west, for a stretch along the edge of the harbor. The station itself fronts onto a large semicircular square that opens toward the sea.

On the side facing away from the harbor lies the so-called residential town. It is to form, as it were, the European "East" of the city—that is to say, the quarter where those people are to move who are employed in the commercial town, in the harbor, and so on; in short, the city of employees and the common folk. For the heads of the firms and for all those who can afford it, a city district in the west has been provided, which will be separated from the residential town by one of the future parks and has been christened the "villa town." [[p95]] Here the distinguished private residences are to be erected. It is thus assumed, after all, that the people living in Dalny will do such good business that they will soon be able to build villas. At the diametrically opposite end, the Chinese town, the "City," is to follow, separated from the European town by the other future park.
