Since 15 June I have been safely back, have settled my affairs in Canton and Hong Kong, and tomorrow I shall embark on board the English steamer *Sunda* to travel first to Shanghai and from there via Japan to Vladivostok.

Originally I had intended to return to Port Arthur and from there traverse Manchuria northward in order to reach the connection with the Trans-Siberian Railway by this route. Unfortunately, I still do not have in my hands the passport [[p114]] promised to me by Vice-Admiral Alexeyev. I am also convinced that he will not send it, and therefore I do not wish to make my further travel plans dependent on it. In order, however, to somewhat complete my knowledge of Manchuria nonetheless, I shall take another route: namely, from Vladivostok through the formerly Manchurian parts of Siberia, and then along the Amur on the border of Manchuria. There, too, I hope to be able to carry out some interesting studies.

### 2. Von Hongkong nach Nagasaki.

Shanghai, 29 June 1901.

I am ready to travel. The numerous farewell visits to friends and acquaintances have been paid. Today at noon I ate for the last time at the German Club. My heavy luggage has already been shipped from Hong Kong to Germany on a Norddeutscher Lloyd vessel. The few suitcases I have with me contain only the most essential travel necessities. One of them is filled to the brim with books about Siberia, which I bought partly in Max Nößler's German bookstore and partly in Kelly & Walsh's English bookstore. There were not very many English books about Siberia, and far fewer German ones. For the most part, the available works are purely scholarly in nature and deal with Russia's advance in Asia. Travelogues in the German language did not exist at all, and those available in English did not cover my route.

In the roadstead of Woosung, the port of Shanghai, lies anchored the Norddeutscher Lloyd steamer *Bayern*, which is to take me to Nagasaki. The Chinese "agent" of my hotel, the Astor House, is therefore instructed to have my luggage taken to the tender *Bremen*. At 6 o'clock in the afternoon I embark there myself, and an hour later I find myself in the port of Woosung and on board the Lloyd steamer *Bayern*. Alongside lies the steamer *Rhein*, on which I had previously travelled to Taku. This time she is on her homeward voyage and fully occupied with relieved sailors, who greet us with loud hurrahs. At around 10 o'clock at night the *Bayern* puts to sea. The large ship turns slowly out of the mouth of the Huangpu, from which navigational obstacles are soon to be removed, and is soon carried by the sluggish waves of the Yangtze. Here and there, ship lights and light buoys marking the fairway blink at us from right and left; then comes the lightship, where the Yangtze pilots are usually dropped off, and shortly before midnight we steer out into the open sea. China—farewell!

30 June.

We are sailing on the high seas in fairly pleasant weather. Two years ago this German postal line between China and Japan did not yet exist; it was only made possible in the autumn of 1899 through the merger of the two largest German steamer lines, the Norddeutscher Lloyd and the Hamburg-America Line.

[[p115]]

The progress of German shipping in East Asia was quite considerable during the past year as well. It is largely connected with the journey that the director-general of the Hamburg-America Line, Herr Ballin, undertook to East Asia. Even if negotiations concerning the purchase of the ships and facilities of a large English and an even larger Chinese coastal shipping company have not yet concluded, the fact remains that, as an initial result of Herr Ballin's visit, the so-called "Jebsen Line"—which handles traffic from Shanghai to Tsingtao, Chefoo, and Tientsin, and maintains a regular, subsidised postal steamer service between Tsingtao and Shanghai—has, insofar as it is a postal line, passed into the possession of the Hamburg-America Line. A second, one might well say truly magnificent success consists in the Hamburg-America Line establishing a regular passenger service beyond Japan to San Francisco, so that the globe is encircled by a distinguished German steamer line. The success is all the more significant as no steamers currently operate on this route that could even remotely compare with the steamers *Hamburg* and *Kiautschou*, which are scheduled to run here regularly. It is of great importance that this line is being established just now. For in East Asian circles it is reportedly known that the "Empress Line" intends to establish an express steamer service between San Francisco and Dalny concurrently with the opening of the Trans-Siberian Railway. Should the Siberian railway really generate such a rush of passengers as people seem to assume, it would be a small matter for the German postal line to include Dalny and Nagasaki in its passenger service beyond Tsingtao. Then the Shanghai–Dalny–Nagasaki triangle would be closed simply by inserting the Tsingtao–Dalny–Nagasaki route.
