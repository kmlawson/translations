In spirit, we are now as good as back in Europe. To be sure, we still need eight days of travel by express train to reach Moscow, and the distance that lies behind us and still ahead of us only becomes clear to us when we determine on the map that, although we have already traveled eastward for nearly five weeks, we are still only at approximately the same degree of longitude as Singapore.

## Chapter VII. In the “Train de Luxe”.

### 1. Irkutsk, the Capital of Eastern Siberia.

We put up at the Hotel “Decko,” which is said to be the best. It will be the last time that I have to endure the miseries of Siberian inns. Yet it seems as though their drawbacks are to be brought home to me once more at the very end in a particularly vivid manner. The hotel is heavily booked, so that Nikolai Mikhailovich and I have to share a room with two beds. Earlier, we had dinner in a restaurant while a troupe of folk singers performed national dances and sang Russian songs. We retired early. Nikolai is already snoring lustily, while I, on the other hand, am fighting a battle with my mattress. Several coil springs are broken; in one [[p309]] place one sinks half a foot deep, in another the unbroken springs dig into one's back with their full elasticity. Despite sincere attempts to fall asleep, I do not succeed. Everyone will know from personal experience that the beast in man awakes when one wants to sleep and has a miserable bed, even though one pays good money for it. In my annoyance, I ring the electric bell to summon some servant. For out in the corridor I have noticed another mattress, and he is to exchange it for mine. But no one appears, and so I resolve to clear out my bed myself, lift my mattress out, carry it into the corridor, and drag the other mattress that is standing there into the room. Alone, I cannot get it into the bedstead, so I leave it lying on the floor and pile the bedding and myself on top of it. This mattress is substantially better than the previous one, but soon I feel a very unpleasant itching, which evidently comes from certain little critters that I now begin to hunt down. There are too many of them, however, and just when I think I am finished with them, new ones appear. Consequently, I put on stockings and gloves, tie my night trousers and night jacket shut at the ankles, and cover my face firmly with a handkerchief. In this way my unwelcome company cannot get at me, and I manage to spend the remainder of the night without further disturbance.

August 22.

Irkutsk is a city in which much wealth resides. It is claimed to have the relatively highest consumption of champagne in the world. The city is largely built of wood. Along the main streets, however, one finds numerous distinguished stone buildings. In the shops that line the main street, one can satisfy all European needs, though everything at a very high price. In particular, I admire the display windows of an extremely elegant clothing store, in which the most modern Parisian fashions are displayed. On the streets one likewise sees much elegance; numerous smart carriages with finely dressed people drive along them, and it causes no embarrassment when the mud splashes high from under the horses' hooves and the wheels. The city possesses a very fine museum belonging to the Irkutsk Section of the Imperial Russian Geographical Society. Furthermore, there is a monumental theater, donated by wealthy citizens. In general, given their mostly quite deficient municipal administration, Siberian towns rely heavily on private assistance. The orphanages and schools of Irkutsk owe their establishment and maintenance largely to private charitable efforts. A large foundling hospital has also been endowed, but it no longer suffices for the need. The life of the upper classes out here, even more than in European Russia, is rather loose, and in the admission of children the principle “La recherche de la paternité est interdit” is considered extended to “maternité” as well. In the city park along the Angara, concerts are held occasionally, at which high society is present, or during which they ride in procession along the street running alongside. Irkutsk is the seat of the highest authorities of Eastern Siberia, which through the railway is directly connected to European [[p310]] [Illustration: The most common type of bridge, e.g., bridge over the Ob, the Belaya, etc. (p. 298).]

In the administration, too, the railway has brought about change, insofar as Siberia, which had hitherto been governed fairly independently, has been brought into closer connection with the St. Petersburg administrative organism, and the administration of justice has been reorganized along European lines. The city is rich in monumental buildings; in it we find a wonderful Cathedral of “Our Lady of Kazan,” which is built entirely of stone and is a masterpiece of Russian architecture. The city also possesses a Roman Catholic and a German Evangelical church. The German congregation of Irkutsk is very numerous. There, one gets by almost entirely with German, while elsewhere French serves very well; English, on the other hand, is spoken almost nowhere. Ever since the railway has existed, the merchants of Irkutsk can afford more frequent stays in Europe, and we find many of them in the [[p313]] German and Bohemian spas. The city is undoubtedly the most elegant and modern of all the cities we have passed through so far—taking Siberian conditions as the basis, of course. Objectively considered, Irkutsk makes no different impression than a mid-sized German town in which a prosperous merchant class sets the tone.
