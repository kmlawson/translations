I have already spoken on several occasions of the Russo-Chinese Bank and explained that it is not merely a powerful commercial institution in China, but also an extraordinarily important political tool in the hands of the Russian Finance Minister, Witte. Of interest, therefore, is the following detail, which is probably not very well known even in financial circles, but which nonetheless sheds a peculiar sidelight on Russia's political relationship with China. At the time, people were surprised that shortly after the founding of the Russo-Chinese Bank, the Chinese Empire withdrew its deposits from the Imperial Chinese Bank and transferred them to the newly established bank. Now, it is a fact that the Russo-Chinese Bank was established with capital raised half by the Russian side, but the other half by the—Chinese government. The bank thus actually operates with Chinese money. What statesmanship must have preceded this before China decided to make common financial cause with Russia! Anyone who knows the tenacity of the Chinese in money matters can imagine it. And yet the administration of the Russo-Chinese Bank is exclusively in Russian hands. China, despite being a half-shareholder, has no say whatsoever in the administration of this institution, which operates in a thoroughly Russian spirit. Now, that is all well and good as long as China remains exclusively in the role of a coupon-clipper. It has gladly made use of this activity, usually drawing advances against the handsome dividends. The great shrewdness of Russian financial policy toward China, however, lies in the fact that, as a result of these connections, Russia can constantly exert political pressure on China through the bank. If the Russian government has any claim against the Chinese, or if anything happens that must be represented by the Russian minister in Peking, Russia is in the fortunate position of being able to dictate compensation to China by simply threatening to deduct the demanded sum from China's capital share in the Russo-Chinese Bank. This is a simple expedient that rarely fails in its effect. As a consequence, Russia is really always in advance of China; the latter's assets, insofar as they are tied up in the Russo-Chinese Bank, are therefore better suited to making China compliant with Russian wishes than any political pressure or feat of arms. And Russia does not fail to tug vigorously at these leading strings by which it holds China.

Apart from the Russo-Chinese, there is no other bank in Port Arthur. It holds a monopoly and, out here, more or less plays the part of the Russian State Bank in general. Here, too, were turned over the enormous quantities of silver that the Russians brought back from their China campaign, just as Port Arthur was the central eastern market for the Russians upon their return from China. In various places in Port Arthur one can still see the photographs in which Russian officers had their portraits taken amidst mountains of silver shoes and the bank's Chinese shroffs*) who weighed the silver.

That retail trade flourishes in view of Port Arthur's large population will come as no surprise. Indeed, in certain streets one finds shop after shop, and inside mostly practical goods—above all, fine warm clothing and footwear. But everything is exorbitantly expensive! Port Arthur is one of the most expensive places in the entire East; perhaps only Vladivostok is more expensive still. The main reason for this is said to be the abnormally high rents for dwellings and shops. Having to pay 100 dollars or more a month for a tiny, dark, and poorly furnished room is by no means uncommon.

Gratifyingly, the largest business in Port Arthur is in the hands of two German firms that enjoy a special reputation among the Russians: namely, the Vladivostok firm of Kunst & Albers and the Chefoo firm of Sietas, Block & Co., both of which have branches here.

Export trade from Port Arthur hardly exists. The hinterland, the Liaodong Peninsula, is too poor, produces too little, and trade from the interior of Manchuria passes through Newchwang. In return, however, Port Arthur supplies the inhabitants along the railway line up to Mukden

[[p88]]

with provisions, above all with canned goods. To be sure, as far as the army is concerned—and really it is only the army that comes into question—the supplies are drawn from the government depots in Port Arthur, from which the merchant community gains nothing.

For the sake of completeness, moreover, it must be mentioned that in probably no other place in the East is womanhood accorded so free a scope as right here. It is particularly the demimonde that cavorts here, against which only a meager contingent of respectable women can be set. Men of a Don Juan disposition would find here a choice among all nationalities, from the supple Japanese girl to the vulgar Austrian woman and on to the American beauty, and a Leporello could certainly bring the catalogue of beauties—perhaps less desirable than desired—up to two thousand and eleven with little effort. These conditions are the undoing of the Russian officers.
