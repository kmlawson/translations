Nevelskoy took these steps on his own responsibility; in St. Petersburg, they had very nearly brought about his demotion. He hurried to Irkutsk to report in person to Muravyov, but the latter had himself already departed in haste for St. Petersburg to take up the fight personally against the machinations of his opponents, and Nevelskoy found only orders awaiting him to follow the Governor-General at once. Both arrived in the nick of time to avert disaster. Muravyov ultimately found backing from the Tsar; Nesselrode was removed from the chairmanship of the so-called Gilyak Committee, which was still responsible for these questions at that time, and Muravyov's policy triumphed all along the line. But in order to carry out the bold politician's plans, a strong military force was required, and the financial conditions of that period did not allow Russia to make larger funds available for the creation of a Siberian army. Across the whole of Siberia, only four infantry battalions were dispersed; not even artillery was available. Muravyov knew of a remedy! In the district of Nerchinsk, that is to say on the frontier of the disputed territories, the peasants settled there were bound to hard and poorly paid compulsory labor in the crown mines. Muravyov proposed releasing the peasants from this forced labor and turning them into Cossacks. This measure, which was later carried out with great energy, meant on the one hand the liberation of the poor serfs; on the other hand, it represented the virtually cost-free creation of a dedicated Siberian military force with which larger-scale operations could be initiated. The peasants were only too glad to exchange the lot of a forced laborer for that of a soldier, and Siberia was suddenly richer by twelve battalions of Cossack infantry of 1,000 men each. Nevelskoy had meanwhile been rehabilitated and had returned to the East. He undertook new explorations along the coast of the Tatar Sea, of which the discovery of the so-called De-Kastri Bay was the most important. Its significance lay in the fact that inland, close to it, lies a lake, “Lake Kizi,” which is connected to the lower reaches of the Amur, so that the possibility existed here of creating, as it were, a new southern mouth of the Amur by means of a road to be built toward the sea. Again on his own responsibility, Nevelskoy founded the posts of Aleksandrovsk on De-Kastri Bay and Mariinsk near Lake Kizi as the terminal points of the connecting road from the sea to the Amur. Farther south, Imperator Bay was discovered and likewise occupied, and on Sakhalin the port of Dliusk. It certainly entailed considerable difficulties once again to push through the legalization of these unauthorized acquisitions. But it [[p194]] succeeded. Nevelskoy could look back with pride on his achievements. With a handful of men he had taken possession of the mouth of the Amur, the west coast of Sakhalin, and a section of the lower course of the Amur. From the sea, he reached out his hand to the Russia advancing from the west. In 1850 he received permission to take possession of Petrovskoye, and by 1853 he had already occupied the finest harbors on the Tatar Sea and the estuary region of the Amur. Ludicrously meager were the resources with which he carried out this seizure of territory. In Mariinsk were stationed eight men, in Aleksandrovsk three, in Konstantinovsk eight, in Iliusk six, later eight, and the garrison of Nikolayevsk originally numbered only five sailors.

While Nevelskoy was occupying new ports and Muravyov was drilling his newly formed troop formations, however, storm clouds were gathering in Europe that were to carry Muravyov's far-reaching projects toward their goal with unsuspected speed. The Crimean War was in the making, and Muravyov hastened to Europe to take measures for the protection of the Russian possessions in the Far East, above all Petropavlovsk. He pointed out with all energy that the only natural supply route for the Russian possessions on the Pacific Ocean was the Amur. But while he was then seeking in Marienbad to restore his badly shattered health, the Foreign Ministry played a vile trick on him by sending a letter behind his back to Beijing, in which the Chinese government was invited to the joint erection of boundary markers on the Gorbitsa, a left tributary of the upper Amur, at the point where the course of the frontier had only been determined in general terms in the Treaty of Nerchinsk. Nesselrode thereby deceitfully thwarted the policy of Muravyov, who was anxious to instill in the Chinese the conviction that they had no claims whatsoever to the left bank of the Amur, which in fact they had never occupied.
