## Chapter III. Russia's Advance from the Ural to the Pacific Ocean.

### 1. The Belt of the World.

Even for contemporaries of the fifteenth century, the world beyond the Ural Mountains—the "Belt"—was "boarded up with planks," as the fairy tale goes. And fairy-tale-like indeed were the tales told of that world. There, people imagined the dwellings of the Laestrygonians and Cyclopes of the fifteenth century, and the reports of the few who had dared to peek through a knothole in that wooden fence into that unknown land of legend served only to confuse notions even further. But the onslaught of the Mongols, who were viewed like the legions of Beelzebub, came so swiftly and passed so swiftly that an enrichment of the European world's knowledge of the land and peoples beyond the mountains could not be expected from it. The first clearer notions of the regions we today designate as Siberia were gained through the extensive expeditions undertaken by fur trappers and traders on behalf of merchants from the city of Novgorod (Newtown). In general, for centuries the fur trade was the driving, and for a long time even the sole, economic motive for the [[p179]] discovery and opening up of Siberia. Novgorod's significance for modern-day Russia is extraordinarily great. In Novgorod, a merchant aristocracy developed that was not only in possession of great wealth, but also knew how to maintain its political independence for a long time amidst the struggles between Poles, Lithuanians, Swedes, Muscovites, and Tatars. Novgorod the Great was in its day the commercial center *kat’ exochn* for the European lands east of the Vistula, and the proud Slavic Augsburg was therefore also capable of extending its political power far to the east, right up to the Urals, at a time when the Grand Duchy of Moscow could not yet contemplate taking up the struggle with powerful Novgorod in addition to its numerous foreign and domestic enemies. The latter founded on the upper Kama the colony of Vyatka, north of what was then still Tatar Kazan, one of the remnants of the dynasty of Genghis Khan. Only in the year 1478, under Ivan III, had Moscow grown strong enough to subjugate Novgorod. With that, its colony of Vyatka likewise passed to Moscow (1479), and thus the state that later became Russia acquired for the first time a common border with the lands beyond the Urals.

Ten years later, an attempt was made to gain a firm foothold in these lands. To be sure, toward the southeast and east the remnants of the realm of Genghis Khan still barred further advance. In the fifteenth century, these remnants were still the kingdoms of Kazan, Astrakhan, Khiva, Bukhara, and the territories of the Nogai and Kirghiz Hordes. But in the north of Western Asia there still dwelt Ostyaks, who had been pushed northward by the Mongol swarms and were now hard pressed by the Mongols, who were likewise expanding northward during their transition from nomadism to settled life. For protection against the Tatars—a name that subsequently always replaces the earlier "Mongols"—the Ostyaks now called upon their bordering neighbor, who had only recently demonstrated his power through his conquest of the Vyatka territory. In return, they offered tribute, a form in which suzerainty was customary expressed at that time. However, this Ostyak realm lay quite far north, as the very name "Obdorsk," i.e., "mouth of the Ob," indicates. An expedition that Ivan III dispatched there in the year 1499 brought back in terms of success nothing, apart from a number of furs and a few new fables, that was deemed worthy of record. Nor did this earliest acquisition of the land of Obdorsk gain any other significance for the acquisition of Siberia than that of an inherently rather vague episode.

More important is the fact that in the year 1552 Russia annexed to its land possessions the Khanate of Kazan, which had already been tributary to it since 1487, and that in the year 1556 it also conquered Kazan's Tatar sister kingdom, the Khanate of Astrakhan. With that, it found itself in possession of the entire course of the Volga, furthermore of access to the Caspian Sea and—what is of greater interest to us here—an unbroken common border between Europe and Asia, from the Caspian Sea right up to the Arctic Ocean.

It now appears completely out of the question that with so long a border, and with so low a natural divide as the Ural mountain range with its flat heights and gentle slopes, no traffic should have taken place across this border [[p180]] in the second half of the fifteenth century. Russian historiography extols Yermak's invasion of the land east of the Urals, which took place thirty years later, simultaneously as the discovery of Siberia, ranking it on an equal footing with the deeds of a Cortés, and non-Russian historians follow this presentation almost without exception. However, in judging these deeds, one must always take into account that commercial and economic relations between Russians and Ostyaks are demonstrable more than a century earlier—relations that ultimately led to a political relationship; furthermore, that in all probability mutual trade connections existed between the realms of Kazan and Astrakhan on the one hand and the kindred Tatars east of the Urals on the other, connections that were taken up by the conquerors, the Russians, or at least continued under the Russian flag by the Tatars who remained in the country. Furthermore, the fact is reported that under Ivan IV the ruler of the land of "Yugra"*), by which was understood at that time the entire land beyond the Urals, named Yadigar, placed himself under Russian protection against the Tatars who were pressing him; it is not known whether he was himself a Tatar or an Ostyak, presumably he was the latter.
