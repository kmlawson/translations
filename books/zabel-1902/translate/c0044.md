Added to these are the model designs for private residences and commercial buildings, the main thoroughfares—in short, the entire skeleton of the city, onto which private speculation is then to fatten the flesh. In addition, however, the construction plan provides for a relatively large government quarter, which is already nearing completion. This quarter is already a little town in its own right, where the approximately 2,000 Europeans working in Dalny reside.

At first glance, Dalny’s development plan has something captivating about it. It is the work of the brilliant director of the works, Government Architect Sakharov, who performs the functions of a chief engineer and thus at the same time occupies the position and rank of an absolute ruler in his district. At his side stands Mr. Timm, who is specifically in charge of the harbor construction. He is a Russian German by birth. The municipal organization work, especially the civil engineering, is under Engineer Gaedicke, whereas the architectural work is under a Pole named Skolimovsky, who has a technically accomplished and artistic assistant in the architect Junghändel, a Saxon.

When we arrive in Dalny, we still have about twenty minutes to walk before reaching the so-called government quarter, where Mr. Timm’s house is located. It is already around ten o'clock in the evening when we arrive there. Mr. Timm invites me into his house and goes immediately to the chief engineer to obtain permission for everything to be shown to me. In Russia, as is well known, everything that is not specifically permitted is forbidden. Permission is graciously and generously granted, and the chief engineer himself sends word that it would be his pleasure to guide me around personally tomorrow afternoon. Mr. Timm intends to devote the morning to me.

*     *     *

February 27.

And so it happens. Early in the morning, Mr. Timm and I ride across the terrain. We leave the government quarter with its numerous finished and half-finished houses and head toward the mountains that rise about two kilometers from the sea. When we have reached a vantage point high enough, we turn and now have the entire Gulf of Talienwan lying before us. Across the bay we see the houses of the town of the same name, behind which mountains rise once more, their peaks vanishing into the blue haze. Directly at our feet, however, lies the plain on which Dalny is to arise. There is indeed something sublime in the thought that in ten years a great city is to lie here, a metropolis in the true sense of the word, from which one will be able to cross Asia in eleven days to set foot once more on home soil in Berlin or elsewhere in Europe. Perhaps San Francisco once looked like this too, perhaps New York as well. Up there one can freely let one’s imagination roam and populate the vast expanse below with people of all nations, the harbor with ships, the quays with workers, and the planned streets with houses, [[p97]] above which the spires and domes of the churches and the roofs and battlements of the monumental buildings will tower. In reality, one now sees lying before oneself only the small, modern government town built in the Swiss villa style, alongside which the actual metropolis of Dalny has yet to rise. One sees the dredgers at work in the harbor and the large cranes turning and swiveling with enormous blocks of concrete that are sunk into the sea to form the side walls of the quays. We ride back down and inspect the

harbor works, which are under Mr. Timm’s particular direction. Long rows of these huge concrete blocks that are to be sunk into the depths are stacked by the water. Elsewhere, workers are busy building an embankment out into the sea. Later on, once it has become a mole, passenger and cargo steamers will dock along this embankment. Out there in the sea lie large dredgers scooping out the seabed to make the harbor navigable for the world’s greatest colossal ships. Further out in the water, rubble is piled upon rubble to construct a breakwater to protect the entrance to the harbor against the onslaught of the waves.
