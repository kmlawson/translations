So much for this question! It deals with a fundamental ill of cartography relating to China, and I therefore regret doubly that the German map series of East Asia currently in course of publication apparently disregards completely the points of view set forth here. In the case of the "Mukden" sheet, there may also have been some tacking between Chinese, Russian, and Japanese maps, the latter two of which are likewise of an extremely doubtful nature as regards transcription, especially the Russian ones. Nevertheless, much remains incomprehensible to me—to single out just one example, why the German cartographer writes "kin tschou fu" and explains in the "Explanation of Chinese Names" that "k before i and ü equals ts." Even though the first word of the city name just mentioned is pronounced quite distinctly "Chin" on the spot, spelling it with "ts" would still come substantially closer to reality. Why, though, write "k" where one is supposed to read "ts"? That is the practitioner's point of view.

But the "Mukden" sheet also puts gross errors into the world. They relate mainly to the railway. First of all, the publisher explains on the index sheet to the map that, of the planned railways, only the German projects have been included, because sufficient material was available only for these. Nevertheless, on the "Mukden" sheet one finds railways and railway projects concerning non-German lines. Compliance with one's own rules therefore does not seem to be taken very strictly. If, however, one may assume that the publisher or editor of this map sheet was subsequently able to obtain sufficient map material [[p14]] to justify deviating from his announced principles, he should at least have ensured that this map material was indeed sufficient. That, however, is unlikely to have been the case. For while the so-called "Imperial Chinese Railway" continues from Jinzhoufu for a considerable distance in the direction of Mukden, crosses the Daling River in this direction, and only turns almost at right angles to the SE at the station of Goubangzi—which cannot be found on the map at all—the cartographer of the "Mukden" sheet has the railway turn ESE immediately after Jinzhoufu, crosses the Daling River with it near its mouth, leads it through the saltwater marshes situated north of the Gaizhou Bank, has it cross the Liao River a little below the town of Tianzhuangtai, runs it past the city of Niukou (Newchwang) on the left bank of the Liao River, leaving the city several kilometers to the south, and finally has it join the Russian "Chinese Eastern Railway" at the station of "Dashiqiao." I do not wish to presuppose of the author of the map sheet that he does not know that the "Imperial Chinese Railway" ends at Newchwang, and that the "Chinese Eastern Railway" begins at the same city, so that in Newchwang itself English interests (appearing in Chinese guise) and Russian interests meet in the form of railways. Nor do I wish to presuppose in him knowledge of the fact that the Jinzhoufu–Mukden section, which he still marks as "under construction," is no longer being built beyond Goubangzi station, because the Russians refuse to allow the English railway company to cross the "Chinese Eastern Railway," which runs west of Mukden. These are primarily political questions, which concern the geographer only secondarily, although he really ought to know them as well. But as such, the cartographer should *absolutely* have known that a railway does not run through salt marshes that are, moreover, actually drawn on the map; furthermore, that a railway bridge across the Liao River has never existed; and that the "Imperial Chinese Railway" terminates on the *right* bank of the Liao River, quite close to its mouth, whereas the Russian "Chinese Eastern Railway" begins on the *left* bank of the Liao River, several kilometers upriver from the foreign settlement. These are errors that ought all the less to have occurred, since these are finished railways.

A map is a documentary record—at any rate, the geographer regards it as such—and when assumptions are included in a map that can be proven neither on the basis of verified material nor by personal experience, it is a duty to bring this to the attention of the unsuspecting map reader. I do not wish to generalize and pass a verdict on the entire "East Asia" map for these reasons. Yet one would not expect such glaring errors in an undertaking published by the Cartographic Department of the Royal Prussian Land Survey. *China, the vast, immense China, is in fact still so little explored in its details—geographically as well—and Chinese cartographic material has been so little verified and has so often proved misleading, that the time is likely not yet ripe to bring about even now the publication of a fundamental map of China whose scale is large enough to warrant the demands that one has a right to make of specialized maps.* —
