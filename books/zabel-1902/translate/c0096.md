The situation created for Muravyov by this deceitful maneuver in a matter that should under no circumstances have been broached from the Russian side was not enviable. But the disturbances of 1851, in which Russia, England, France, and Sardinia became embroiled, diverted the attention of St. Petersburg policy from East Asia, and thus Muravyov gained a free hand. The necessity of provisioning and reinforcing both the earlier and the new coastal acquisitions had to serve him—both with his own government and in the eyes of the Chinese—as a pretext to proceed with all energy to the execution of his plans, and ultimately to present both, Russia as well as China, with a fait accompli. But he was diplomat enough to do nothing that could have been construed to his disadvantage. He therefore refrained from any unauthorized action and set forth his view of the Amur question in a detailed memorandum, which was presented with such clarity and good sense that a committee established under the Tsesarevich to examine it gave its approval. Thus in 1854, for the first time, Muravyov obtained authorization from his own government to use the Amur with an armed force and supply vessels as the most direct route to the Russian possessions on the Pacific. At the same time, he was also tasked with negotiating the border demarcation question with China. A diplomatic attaché and an interpreter were assigned to him, and the Chinese government was informed from St. Petersburg that a Russian flotilla would sail down the Amur to convey troops [[p195]] and provisions to the Russian possessions on the Pacific Ocean threatened by war with European powers.

Meanwhile, the first Amur expedition had been equipped with the utmost care; the first Amur steamboat, the *Argun*, had been launched at Sretensk, and the expedition departed from there in grand style on May 14*) 1854.

On May 17, the flotilla, consisting of about 75 vessels, reached the confluence of the Argun and the Shilka, and on the morning of the 18th entered the forbidden river amidst solemn ceremonies. On May 28, it arrived in the vicinity of Aigun. Muravyov sent several of his officers ahead to scout whether the commandant of the fortress had received any instructions from Beijing. That was not the case. Although he lodged a protest against the Russians' breach of treaty, he was so surprised by the sight of the large flotilla that he made no attempt to place obstacles in the way of their onward journey, but on the contrary invited Muravyov to a parade of his troops, who assembled with bow, arrow, and fire-hardened wooden lances. Without further incidents, the fleet continued on its way, its main body reaching Mariinsk on June 14. There Muravyov met with Nevelskoy. He now distributed his men and provisions among the various occupied ports and on August 20 returned to Irkutsk via Ayan.

How Muravyov worked is shown by the activities of the 200-man detachment that had received orders to take up station at Lake Kizi. This detachment was initially tasked with building the projected road from Lake Kizi through the forest to De-Kastri Bay. Since the provisions had spoiled, the detachment was nearly destroyed by hunger, but was saved by a stroke of luck. After the road was completed—that is, cleared—the detachment was sent to Nikolayevsk and from there to Petrovskoye. Out at sea in their old Amur boats, however, the Cossacks were shipwrecked and had to forge a path to Petrovskoye along the impassable coast. From there, the transport ship *Irtysh* brought them to Ayan. Here, together with four cannons, they were put aboard the Russian-American Company's transport ship *Kamchatka* and dispatched to intercept marauding English whalers. Since their search proved fruitless, they remained at sea for only a few days and, upon their return, built two batteries. In mid-September, they were embarked once more and sent to Alaska. All this took place within a span of three months!

Muravyov's measures were soon to receive their brilliant justification. Not in vain had he taken the route along the Amur; not in vain had he sent a considerable portion of the provisions carried along and a detachment of 100 men to Kamchatka to reinforce the garrison of Petropavlovsk. And even if, in the course of time, the Russians were unable to hold the place permanently, they nevertheless won a victory here in the Far East, while in the West fortune was less kind to Russian arms. The repulse of the Anglo-French fleet's attack on Petropavlovsk served, as it were, as the acid test for the correctness of Muravyov's measures.
