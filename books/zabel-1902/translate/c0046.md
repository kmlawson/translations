Now, in conclusion, one more word that must also receive its due. Even if we are alarmed by Russia's advances in Manchuria, even if we are privately compelled reproachfully to remind Russia of the dubious role it has played out here over the past six months and must hold up a mirror to it, even if we may eventually have to be armed to call a halt to Russia where it goes too far, we must nevertheless not fail to recognize on the other hand that the center of gravity of general East Asian interests will be shifted to the north should Russia's future Manchurian plans regarding Dalny materialize. For us Germans, however, this holds three advantages: first, that Shandong—*our* Shandong—is brought closer to this center. Second, experience has shown that, of all nations, Germans have hitherto found it easiest to gain a firm foothold and do business in those places where Russia has permitted competition among nationals of different countries at all; and finally, third, perhaps an even more important question than the partition of China is the opening up of Siberia—fertile, virgin Siberia, which unfortunately possesses the regrettable trait of being almost less known than China. Through the opening of the Trans-Siberian Railway, Siberia will in a certain sense become the hinterland of Dalny. This, to be sure, is music of the future. But music of the future that will perhaps not take very long to become popular. This, too, we must bear in mind if we wish to form a judgment on the Manchurian question and do not want to expose ourselves to the reproach that our judgment is one-sided.

28 February (on the high seas).

For the present, my Manchurian travel program must be regarded as concluded. The campaign in Zhili is not yet over, and I must be prepared for the possibility that major new events might once again summon me to the theater of war. At present, however, things do not yet look that way. Nevertheless, I must expedite my departure for the sole reason that I must lose no time in dispatching some important news home from Chefoo.

[Illustration: Residential houses intended for Chinese in Dalny (p. 81).]

The passenger train to Port Arthur departs Dalny at four o'clock in the morning. As it is usually very crowded, on the advice of newly made friends in Dalny I head to the railway station late in the evening, board a compartment of the waiting train, and lie down there to sleep. To be sure, my rest lasts only until the arrival of the numerous other passengers, who soon leave me with nothing more than a corner in which I may try to let my tired eyes close from time to time. Yet the company prattles and chatters in such a chaos of sounds that sleeping is quite impossible. I have the dubious pleasure of having, directly opposite me, an elderly Russian [[p102]] lady with her roughly seventeen-year-old son take their seats. The two seem to take the liveliest interest in humor and poetry; at any rate, the young gentleman continually reads aloud poems from a Russian book, and the old lady bursts into fits of laughter from time to time. I feel a certain envy toward my Russian colleague and momentarily regret keenly that I am not a Russian author. Meanwhile, this regret soon gives way to annoyance at the perpetual disturbance, and in the end I inwardly curse the Russian poet who continually jolts me from my sleep through the effect of his works. After a journey that seems longer to me than the entire trip from Newchwang to Port Arthur, I finally arrive back in Port Arthur in the morning, unwashed, covered in dust, and with aching eyes. I hail an izvozchik, who takes me to the residence of my host, where I first attempt to revive my slumbering nerves with a cold-water rubdown; then I head to the Bund to purchase tickets for myself and my good Chinese servant for the crossing to Chefoo, and make several farewell visits in haste. The steamer is scheduled to put to sea in the evening. The connection with Chefoo is fairly regular; a ship departs almost every evening. One goes on board at nightfall, and when one wakes up the next morning and looks out the cabin window, the pleasant harbor scene of Chefoo already comes into view. One more zakuska, one more merry farewell toast—which people in Port Arthur also know how to appreciate—and then I bid farewell to my friends in Manchuria and to Manchuria itself, with the silent hope in my heart of seeing them again in the course of this campaign, only to head north, with Alekseyev's recommendations in my pocket, and thus reach the Trans-Siberian Railway.

The steamer on which I travel belongs to an English company and is anything but elegant: an old paddle steamer that seems originally to have been built for river navigation! At first I have a quite acceptable cabin berth, but later I have to give it up because a lady comes on board who relieves me of the possession of my cabin. The captain, however, has a camp bed set up for me in the dining saloon, and on this I gently fall asleep, only to awake on the morning of 1 March in the roadstead of Chefoo. Manchuria lies behind me; connection with the world has been regained. In Chefoo there are newspapers once again—yes, newspapers! I believe the last one I read was an issue of the *Ostasiatischer Lloyd* [*East Asian Lloyd*] that still bore the date of the year 1900.
