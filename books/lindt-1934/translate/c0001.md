# Incident in a Manchurian Girls' School.

## "This man wanted to murder me!"—It was only a misunderstanding. Nothing unusual in Manchukuo.

*By A. R. Lindt.*

The town's schoolmaster, a long-haired youth in a blue gown, to whom a brand-new straw hat of Western origin lent a European air, implored us to visit his school. This, like every other building, was protected by a high earthen wall. In the courtyard, boys were playing a kind of tennis.

"China's athletic awakening is the prerequisite for its national renewal," the teacher lectured before leading us into the classrooms, whose earthen walls were bare and whose windows were pasted over with parchment. The desks were the same as in any country school in Europe. A timetable set out how much time the unfortunate Chinese pupils had to spend learning the six thousand characters required to at least be able to read a newspaper. (In order to understand a classical poem, however, they would have to master thirty thousand characters.) Instruction also encompassed the history of the Nationalist Party, the Kuomintang, and Doctor Sun Yat-sen's famous Three Principles. In every room, hanging as the sole decoration, was his portrait, which, increasingly displacing the old images of gods, is also to be found in the yamen of every mandarin. I even saw it in isolated farmsteads beside the family altar.

A young girl had approached us shyly. The teacher introduced her as the headmistress of the girls' school and added that she wondered whether she might venture to ask us to pay her a visit. The rooms of the girls' school were cramped, the broken desks jammed together, and the parchment of the windows hung down in tatters. The city fathers were little inclined to waste money on the education of girls. In her tidy, clean living room, the headmistress introduced us to her assistants, who likewise were dressed not in trousers—as befits women in the countryside—but in skirts. They spoke with great passion of their struggle against bound feet.

"How is a woman who can move only with difficulty on stilts supposed to stand up for gender equality? How can . . ."

Shouts interrupted her. The door flew open. The schoolmaster pounced on the intruder in a single bound. Teacups crashed to the floor. Women and children shrieked. Breathing heavily, the two men wrestled. Finally the schoolmaster managed to pin both arms of his opponent, who even now was trying to defend himself with his feet. It was a soldier. His cap lay on the floor and a knife at his feet. Someone picked it up.

"This man," he said with the smile that accompanied everything he said, "wanted to murder you."

The schoolmaster released the soldier, who embarrassedly put on his cap and asked for his knife back. He bowed low before us.

"He took you for Japanese. Now that he sees you are white men, he begs your pardon."

The female teachers brought fresh teacups.

"If only you would finally burn your brown windbreakers," said Han.

As the schoolmaster escorted us back to the yamen, he enthusiastically professed his nationalist creed. In modern China, the intellectual rarely has access to power. And yet an intellectual professional class is increasingly gaining influence over the fortunes of China, succeeding in gradually changing deep-rooted attitudes and customs. It is the class of the poorly paid, little-respected schoolteacher, such as is found in most villages and as a family employee in large farmsteads. Barely out of university, his head filled with grand plans for China's renewal, he preaches to the peasant children that China is one nation, to which both Harbin and Canton belong. He depicts for them how intolerable the privileges of foreigners are and convinces them that it is the duty of youth to tear up the one-sided treaties with foreigners like scraps of paper. Following the teachings of Sun Yat-sen, which he himself has perhaps not fully understood, he seeks to awaken the Chinese masses, who have resigned themselves to the notion that politics is none of their business, to national consciousness. Relying on youth, he shapes public opinion despite the suspicion of the elderly. It is he who first makes any incident with a foreign state comprehensible to the people. He knows how to turn the aversion peasants feel toward all things foreign into hatred. When Japan marched into Manchuria, it was the schoolteacher who tried to fire up the indifferent peasants and timid shopkeepers to resist. Even though the new rulers of Manchuria have issued a decree forbidding all schools from continuing to disseminate the teachings of Sun Yat-sen, commanding them to teach the history of the imperial house of the Manchu and the life of Puyi instead of the history of the Kuomintang, the little schoolmaster continues to preach hatred against Japan. He is the fiercest enemy of the new state of Manchukuo, more dangerous than the rebel generals.
